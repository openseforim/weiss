Chapter Twenty-Four.

The relation of the Oral Torah to the books of the Gospels and their views

In almost every generation adversaries rose against Israel who sought to uproot the tree of its religion from its root. How many nations and kingdoms rose against them and thought to wipe out the name of Israel from the earth; how many sages and writers among the nations despised them, mocked them, and sought to profane the honor of their Torah and to make their faith and customs a derision among the peoples; within them too adversaries rose up, namely the religious tendencies: the Sadducees, the Essenes and all the parties of dispute within the Pharisees themselves. All these oppositions, born at home or born abroad [Leviticus 18:9], were a curse to the house of Israel; but the harm was only material, to the adherents of the religion, not moral, to the religion itself. For as its opponents multiplied, so the love of the Jews for it grew. Against the enmity from outside they girded themselves to stand for their lives, and against their opponents from within they set a guard; but we have not heard or seen that they went down against these or those to the point of life⚠1. The greatest denial in their eyes was that of one who denies Torah from Heaven or the resurrection of the dead, and even so they did not sentence such deniers to a temporal punishment but to an eternal one, and said of them that they have no share in the world to come. But at the end of the days of the Temple there arose one opposition that vexed them more than all before it: the opposition of the Messianic Jews. The adherents of the sect were persecuted, and with them also their master. The cause of this evil and bitter lot was not his new tendency in the religion of the Jews, but the foundations on which he built his tendency; and his sentence was not the fruit of his teaching but the fruit of the means and the instruments through which he chose to give his teaching existence. For if we consider his teaching we will find that it is not a new teaching, but all of it, from beginning to end, is the teaching of the Jews dressed in a new garment. With regard to the Oral Torah he followed the Sadducees and said that it is a human invention and a commandment of men learned by rote [Isaiah 29:13], by means of which the Pharisees uproot the commandments of the written Torah (Matthew 15); and in his deeds and customs he followed the Essenes. His relation to John the Essene and his disciples shows that he had an inclination to the teaching of the Essenes, and so it appears from the customs of his life and from the course of his thoughts and beliefs. Now the marks of the Essenes are the desire to attain prophecy and to heal the sick by skill in charms; wealth they did not seek, and they chose common property within their society and lived a life of hardship; and these are hinted at also in their writings, and all these are surely the marks of the Essenes. Nevertheless most of his teaching is the teaching of the Jews and also, for the most part, in agreement with the views of the Pharisees. Does he not testify of himself that he did not come to uproot the Torah of Moses and the prophets but to fulfill it (ibid. 5:17)? And the concept of this fulfillment is going beyond the measure of the Torah and adding stringencies to what the Torah made strict. This is clear from the particulars counted there, by which he describes the manner of the fulfillment; and in the language of the sages one would say of such fulfillment: making a fence for the Torah. Whoever considers the particulars of the fulfillment there will find that most of them are fences that the Pharisaic sages also set.{v1_12_17:1} So too his making the commandment of love a great principle in the Torah (ibid. 22:39–40), and "What is hateful to you, do not do to your fellow: that is the principal thing, and the rest is commentary" (ibid. 7:12), and acts of kindness to an enemy (ibid. 5:44): for all this there is a source in Holy Scripture and in the words of our sages, and it is engraved in the life of our nation and in all its ways in the world from the time it became a nation until this day.{v1_12_17:2} So too his principles on the merits⚠2 of the lowly, the crushed, the humble and those who thirst for righteousness, on the reward of the merciful, that they will obtain mercy, the pure in heart, that they will receive the presence of the Shekhinah, the lovers of peace, that they are called children of the Omnipresent, and those persecuted for their righteousness, that they will attain the kingdom of Heaven (ibid. 5:3–10): these are found literally, word for word, in Holy Scripture and in the words of our sages. And his principles on the love of God for His creatures, that He is good to all (ibid. 5:45),{v1_12_17:3} and on love for Him and cleaving to Him, to be merciful as He is merciful (Luke 6:36), to believe in His power and to trust in Him without worrying today about tomorrow's livelihood (Matthew 6:31–33); on the payment of reward measure for measure (ibid. 7:2); on doing good not in order to receive a reward (Luke 6:35); that those who do good in secret receive their reward openly (Matthew 6:4);{v1_12_18:1} and the principle of the repair of the world in the kingdom of Heaven, by the removal of sin and the spread of moral good, independent of anything political (in many places): all these are principles of the teaching of the Pharisees.{v1_12_18:2} And besides these principles listed, most of his sayings and parables are found in the books of the Jews, and especially in the books of the Oral Torah;{v1_12_18:3} and therefore it has become clear that most of his teaching is from the teaching of the Pharisees.

And as most of his teaching is the teaching of the Pharisees, so too his religious position did not differ in essentials from the position of the Pharisees. However, it was the custom of the Pharisees from of old not to swerve even a step from the accepted beliefs and opinions and from the principles of the religion of their Torah, the first of which is complete unity; and although the belief in the coming of our righteous Messiah was engraved on the tablet of the heart of every man of Israel and sustained them in all the hardships of their lives, nevertheless, if one or another arose among them to say, "I am the chosen one whom God has sent," they did not hasten to believe that he was the one for whom their eyes were always waiting. And indeed, not many days passed before a new change was revealed within their community. Then the members of the sect became two camps. The one camp remained in the position of the Jewish religion, and the second abolished the Torah of Moses with respect to its practical part. The leader of the second was Paul, whose Hebrew name was Saul and who claimed to be a disciple of Rabban Gamaliel the Elder. He set aside the practical commandments and made the belief in their Messiah and in his resurrection after death the principal thing, and he strove to show that all this is hinted at in Holy Scripture according to the ways of the midrash of the sages. The second prevailed over the first, and in the course of time the Messianic Jews came to an end, and the religion of the Messianists was a new one, which its first founder had not imagined. For the first founder said explicitly that he was sent to the Jews (Matt. 10:6) and did not come to uproot the Torah but to fulfil it (ibid. 5:17); and he said that although the chief thing is the commandment of righteousness and love, nevertheless it is fitting not to let go of the practice (ibid. 23:23; Luke 11:42). And he took care in every way not to give his opponents a pretext, lest they say that he came to uproot the Torah, and he explicitly commanded his followers to be careful in keeping the practice (Matt. 5:4).⚠3 And if he permitted some prohibition among the prohibitions of the Torah, he came with proofs from the Torah itself, or gave a reason for the commandment in such a way that the cancelling of the prohibition came from the cancelling of the reason, which in his opinion was not against the intention of the Torah.{v1_12_18:4} And once the Pharisees argued with him because his disciples ate without washing hands and transgressed the words of the Scribes, and he did not protest against them. And he answered them that the statutes of the Scribes are only "a commandment of men learned by rote" [Isaiah 29:13], and God did not command them; but what enters a man's mouth does not defile, only what comes out of it defiles (ibid. 15:11). Now although these words show that he was not strict about all the forbidden foods that the Torah forbade, nevertheless it appears from the end of his words there that his heart did not dare to say so explicitly; on the contrary, he tried to explain his words in such a way that they would not be understood of all forbidden foods. For he said: The evil thoughts go out from the heart and they defile the man, but to eat without washing hands does not defile (ibid. 15:20). We see that he set down a general rule aimed at all forbidden foods and explains it by means of a particular which is only an impurity from the words of the Scribes; and we understand from this that the general rule gives us a slight sense of what was in his heart, and the particular is like an excuse in the face of the complaint. At all events it appears that he refrained as far as possible from appearing as if he wished to uproot anything from the Torah. And therefore his disciples, who did not reach the depth of his mind and did not understand the hidden things of his heart, remained in the position of the Jewish religion with respect to practice. But Paul understood very well the things that were in his heart, and did not fear to bring the thought into action, and what Jesus had hidden in the secret of his heart Paul taught publicly. He said in clear words that from the day that Jesus the Nazarene arose to teach and instruct, the commandments and statutes were abolished, and the merit of a man, whether Jew or non-Jew, depends on faith. And whoever acknowledges the mission of Jesus and his lordship and believes in his resurrection after his death merits eternal good and happiness (Rom. 10:4–11). So he spoke in general terms; and when he speaks of the particular statutes he says absolutely that their observance is not necessary for the members of the faith, and in particular he abolished the commandment of circumcision. And in all these teachings of his he came with proofs from the Holy Scriptures according to the ways of midrash customary in his time.

These few hints about the deeds of Jesus and his disciples, about the essence of their teaching and their endeavours, will suffice to know the position of this religious society over against the position of the accepted Jewish religion. The system of the first founder, like the system of Paul, was regarded as a danger to the old teaching of the Jews, since both of them contained new beliefs that impaired the accepted faith of Israel. Therefore it is self-evident that the sages of the first generations were compelled to rouse themselves against them and to keep the danger away by means of enactments and decrees, and to make known that the new beliefs and opinions are in contradiction with the Jewish religion. And so enactments came into being, some of which were meant to define the legal status of the Messianists, and in what respect they were to be considered members of the Jewish religion, and some to make a safeguard against their opinions and to warn against being drawn after them. Of the enactments defining their legal status only a little has reached us, but from this little it follows that they were regarded in their eyes as gentiles. Not many days passed before they decreed concerning their slaughtering, their bread and their wine, and that their produce is untithed (Ḥullin 13a), and that meat found in their hands is forbidden and regarded as sacrifices to the dead; and they decreed against intermarrying with them (Tosefta ibid. ch. 2), and that a Torah scroll written by a min is to be burned (Gittin 45b). All these enactments are aimed against the Messianic Jews, for against their followers from the nations they did not need to decree concerning their slaughtering, their bread and their wine, since the prohibition had long been in force. But more numerous are the enactments that they made as a safeguard against their opinions and customs. At first they used to recite the Ten Commandments too in the Shema, and they abolished them because of the complaint of the minim,{v1_12_19:1} so that they should not say that these alone were given to Moses at Sinai (Berakhot 12a, and Yerushalmi ibid.). And indeed it was said thus in the name of Jesus their Messiah, that the chief thing is the Ten Commandments and the commandment of love (Matt. 19:18). This enactment was made in the first century of their era, for R. Nathan, who lived around the middle of the second century, speaks of it as of something enacted in a past time. Further, they enacted because of the minim that "Blessed be the name of His glorious kingdom for ever and ever" should be said aloud in the Shema (Pesaḥim 56a). The content of this blessing is the acceptance of the kingdom of heaven, and it was instituted by the Zealots, who said that one must accept no yoke of human kingship but only the kingdom of heaven (R. Meir Friedmann in his commentary on Sifre Va-etḥanan 31);{v1_12_19:2} but they said it in a whisper for the sake of peace with the government. Now many of the Pharisees were suspected of heresy (Yerushalmi Berakhot 5:3), and perhaps they found that some of the suspects joined the name of Heaven and the name of Jesus in the whispered acceptance of the kingdom of heaven; therefore they enacted that the acceptance should be said aloud. Around the end of the first century of their era, in the first generation after the destruction of the Temple, they made an addition to the blessing "who humbles the arrogant,"{v1_12_20:1} aimed against the minim, and it is called the blessing of the minim (Berakhot 28b). And they were so strict about saying it that they made it a rule that whoever did not say it is called back, to examine him lest he be a min (Yerushalmi ibid. 5:3). And they decreed that the gilyonim [Gospels] and the books of the minim be burned (Shabbat, Yerushalmi and Bavli ch. 15, and Tosefta ch. 11); and they decreed not to deal with them in disputations and not to be healed by them even for a brief span of life (Avodah Zarah 27b), but to keep as far away from them as possible (Mekhilta Mishpatim with M"S ch. 20;⚠4 Avodah Zarah 17a), because heresy draws the heart (Sifre Shelaḥ 115; Avodah Zarah 27b). They likened it to snares and nets that trap the soul; and in fact several sages were caught in it for moments, as happened to R. Eliezer ben Hyrcanus (Avodah Zarah 17a), and to Ben Dama, the son of R. Ishmael's sister (ibid. 27b), and to Ḥananiah, the nephew of R. Joshua, and to Judah ben Naqosa, and to other sages (Ecclesiastes Rabbah on "All things" and on "And I find"). And every custom of theirs, even one of "what does it matter," was forbidden to the Jews, so as to widen the distance. Such as their custom of slaughtering into a pit in order to let the blood go to waste; for even after they had voted and resolved to abolish the practical commandments, they still held to the commandment of slaughter and warned against eating the blood (Acts 15:20, 29). Against this the sages decreed that one must not slaughter into a pit, because he is doing their statutes, and whoever does so must be examined afterwards (Ḥullin 41a, and Tosefta ibid. ch. 2). And likewise they decreed that one who says, "I will not pass before the ark in coloured garments," may not pass even in white ones; "I will not pass in sandals," he may not pass even barefoot (Megillah 24b); and this is for fear of heresy. For such was the way of the full-fledged Essenes, and they were suspected of heresy (*Bikkurim* I, 183, by R. Simeon Santa).⚠5 It is also related that the minim used to put the tefillin on the forehead and on the palm of the hand, and in the wording of their blessing they used to say, "The good shall bless You" (Megillah ibid.). And they also alluded in their prayer in various ways to their belief in a partnership [in the Godhead], which they called "two powers"; and against every hint of this kind and anything like it the sages were strict with all force (Berakhot 33b), and they strove to show that there is nothing in the proofs that the minim brought them to demonstrate the partnership from the Scriptures. Against the teaching of Paul they warned with extreme warnings. Rabban Simeon ben Gamaliel the Elder had already warned against neglect of the practice, saying (Avot ch. 1): "Not study is the chief thing but practice"; against the view of Paul, who says that merit comes not through practice but through faith. In the Gospels and in the writings of Paul and the apostles we find that they expounded the reasons for the commandments and made the attributes of the Holy One, blessed be He, mercy. Against this the sages stood up and determined that the commandments of the Torah are nothing but decrees.{v1_12_20:2} And it is clear that they determined so with a particular intention. For the sages who were zealous against seeking out the reasons for the commandments are mostly the sages of the first generations after the founding of the Christian religion. One was Rabban Yoḥanan ben Zakkai, who said to his disciples concerning purification by the waters of lustration: "By your life, the dead does not defile and the water does not purify; but the Holy One, blessed be He, said: I have laid down a statute, I have decreed a decree; you are not permitted to transgress My decree" (Numbers Rabbah ch. 19). And likewise R. Eleazar ben Azariah said: "A man should not say, I have no wish for pork, I have no wish for sha'atnez; but [he should say], I do wish it, but my Father in heaven has decreed so upon me, as it is said, 'and I have separated you from the peoples, that you should be Mine' [Leviticus 20:26]; thus he separates himself from transgression and accepts upon himself the yoke of the kingdom of heaven" (Sifra, end of Kedoshim). And likewise we have seen that other Mishnah teachers also determined so (end of Megillah). Against this, we have seen that they themselves expounded the reasons for the commandments, and Rabban Yoḥanan ben Zakkai in particular sought at all times to reveal the spiritual intention in the statutes of the Torah and to expound their reasons.{v1_12_21:1} From this we understand that their warning was aimed only against the seeking of reasons for the purpose of overturning the practice. And therefore the warning was only temporary. Formerly in Israel the sages loved this kind of exposition. So did Hillel, and his disciples saw it from him and did likewise. And now, if a change suddenly appears before our eyes in their view on this point, it is only from the compulsion of events. And when the compelling cause passes, the effect no longer stands; and therefore we find that the very reasons against which they warned (Megillah ibid.) were expounded at a later time without objection (Leviticus Rabbah ch. 27, and in many places). For from the time when the teaching of Paul prevailed, things changed, and the position of the Jews towards the sect of the Christians was not as at first; and from then on they were no longer strict about seeking out the reasons for the commandments. Paul and his party set their faces towards the nations, and to draw their hearts to their teaching they abolished all the practical commandments; and so their teaching changed its character, and in the course of time it became a new teaching that had nothing to do with the teaching of the Jews; most of its people and leaders were gentiles, and from then on the separation was a complete separation. About all these enactments and warnings they were very strict, for they did not regard them as misled but as deniers by will and knowledge, and they called them people who burn their food in public (Berakhot 17b; Sanhedrin 103a). And they said of them that they know their Creator and deny Him (Shabbat 116a, and Yerushalmi ibid.). And of those who know their Master and intend to rebel against Him they said that even if they have so far come only to a small degree of denial, their end is to deny the root principle. Their beginning is that they do not study and do not do, and through this they despise others, and hate the sages, and do not let others do, and deny the commandments that were spoken from Sinai, and the end is that they deny the root principle (Sifra Be-ḥukkotai ch. 2). In short, in their eyes the deeds of this sect did not come from the heart; rather, they thought that their only aim was to throw off the yoke of the Torah; and therefore they called them hypocrites, and hypocrisy and heresy were of equal weight for them. And how harsh are the words they said of them, that they are burners of the world: indeed, if they were given permission they would burn the whole world with its inhabitants in a short moment (Genesis Rabbah ch. 48).

We have now passed in review most of the enactments and decrees made with respect to the minim, and most of the warnings that the sages issued, and their harsh judgment of them and of their ways; but there is not a single one of all these that was said, or that is fit to be said, of the members of this sect whose origin is from the nations and who took upon themselves the teaching of the Messianic Jews. Even with slight reflection every discerning person will know the matter correctly: all the sayings mentioned have nothing to do with the Christians among the nations, but are all aimed at the Messianic Jews who lived among them and mostly followed the customs of the Jews; against these was their complaint. From the time when, through Paul's efforts, their teaching became a new teaching that had nothing to do with the teaching of the Jews, and the Messianic Jews dwindled, and the few who remained changed their religion entirely, and most of the members of the religion were from the nations, and its leaders and heads were from them, from that time the Jews no longer had religious enmity against the adherents of the Christian faith. And if their souls grieved over the loss of many of their covenant brethren who went after the new opinions, against this they were consoled⚠6 by the fact that some of the foundations of the Jewish religion, of the principles of righteousness and love and deeds of loving-kindness, and several of their beliefs spread among the nations, and many of the peoples of the earth who formerly worshipped idols and were sunk in corrupt opinions were now drawn to the belief in one God and took up the moral teaching of the Jews. And although they gave the Holy One, blessed be He, a partner, they did not regard this as idolatry (see Sanhedrin 63a, and see there in Tosafot, and Bekhorot 2b in Tosafot). For whoever of the nations believes in the Lord, the God of heaven, even if he associates something else with Him, and keeps the teaching of righteousness between man and his fellow and mercy towards creatures, and guards himself from violence and walks in the ways of morality, has fulfilled, according to the tradition of the Pharisees, the will of God, and kept the Torah and the commandment that the Lord apportioned to the sons of Noah (Sanhedrin 56a), and he is called pious and has a share in the world to come (ibid. 105a). And therefore it was a great satisfaction to the Jews to see that the Torah of Moses had cleared itself a path among the nations, and that a large part of it was accepted by them and they believed in its divinity. And for this reason too they distinguished between the nations in general and the minim among the nations (Avodah Zarah 65a),{v1_12_22:1} and they said explicitly that all the complaint they had against the Messianic Jews was void with respect to the minim among the nations, for these were regarded as resident aliens (ibid., and Ḥullin 13b). In short, it appears that from the time when the Christian religion separated entirely from the Jewish religion, and most of its adherents were from the nations, the Jews had no religious enmity against them. On the contrary, the Jews strove not to give them a pretext for complaint. Thus it is related that at the beginning of the Christian religion there was still a custom in Israel to fast on the first day of the week, and later, when the Christians made the first day of the week a day of rest, the Jews abolished their custom, because they feared the complaint of the Christians, lest they say: because we rejoice on the first day, they fast (Soferim ch. 16). From that time the blessing of the minim no longer had a place; therefore they changed its wording and set it against the informers. And if the first Christians looked on this blessing with an evil eye and thought that it was aimed against them, they were misled at all times by informers; for against the minim among the nations the Jews had no religious enmity. Rather, the enlightened among the Jews saw at all times in the spread of the ideas of the Torah among the nations the first step towards the fulfilment of the exalted promise that the day will come when all the inhabitants of the world will recognize the true unity, "and the Lord shall be one, and His name one" [Zechariah 14:9]. But the narrowness of eye and baseless hatred of individuals caused a delay in the matter, and the hope of the good has not yet come; for instead of compromise and peace they thought at all times to uproot the Jewish religion like a tree, and "the daughter rose up against her mother" [Micah 7:6]. Yet not the daughter, but the members of her household, were incensed against the children of the mother. But vain is the counsel of man. For the mother, though old, has not ceased to give her strength; still, like a fresh native tree, "she bears fruit in old age" [Psalms 92:15]. And better than those who came after him did Jesus their Messiah understand the matter, when he spoke of the eternity of our Torah, saying: Even heaven and earth shall pass away and change, but not one letter of the Torah shall fall to the ground.
