Chapter Three

From the time of David to the time of the destruction of the Temple.

The pleasant plantings that Samuel planted in the vineyard of the Lord of hosts grew quickly and prospered, and also bore fruit. The blossoms of holy poetry appeared in the land, and the song of the Lord opened upon it like a lily. The psalms and hymns handed down to us in the name of David king of Israel, and the sublime poems that the poets of his time sang, were glorious in holy splendor. The spirit of the Torah of Moses spoke in them, and they were lifted up by the fear of the Lord. Therefore it is very likely that that time was more wonderful in this respect too than the times that had passed, in that they set their heart to meditate on the Torah of God. And how shall we turn to this? When we see how very exalted this king was in holding fast to the Torah and making it the measuring line in all his deeds. In his last words, when he charged Solomon his son, his command to him was: "keep the charge of the Lord your God, to walk in His ways, to keep His statutes and His commandments, His judgments and His testimonies, as it is written in the Torah of Moses" (1 Kings 2:3). And if he sometimes departed from the statute of the Torah, this was only because of a momentary weakness of his soul. And although in all the stories we have of his life and deeds there are only very few traces of the Torah's statutes, this is not a sign that inquiry into them was lacking, but because the narrator found no place or reason to mention them. Yet for all that, traces of great value for our purpose are scattered across the stories of his life and of the lives of the kings after him. Thus it is told that David sentenced the Amalekite stranger to death because he confessed of himself that he had killed Saul. Likewise he condemned Rechab and Baanah to death on their own confession. Now if we could know that David did so and decided their case according to the law of the Torah in capital cases, we could draw from this a conclusion about the nature of the law of conviction at that time, and say that the confession of the party was sufficient testimony to decide a death sentence on it. But who knows this? Perhaps David acted so as an emergency measure and not according to law.{v1_03_02:1} But we should pay attention to one expression that the narrator uses and puts in David's mouth, namely what he said to the Amalekite stranger: "Your blood be upon your head, for your mouth has testified against you, saying, I have slain the Lord's anointed" (2 Samuel 1:15)⚠1. Now it is all one to us whether David really said it or not; in any case this expression that the writer uses is very old, and its origin is from the days of the kings (see below, ch. 4). And from its form it appears that "your mouth has testified against you" was a common expression for a person's testimony against himself, and its sense is that the confession of a party counts as witnesses. Such expressions never arise except through use as a common thing.⚠2 Therefore there was surely an established law that the confession of the sinner's own mouth is a full testimony [aniyah], and aniyah is nothing but testimony. For the word anah is a fixed and technical term in the law of the Torah for the notion of testimony, as in "You shall not bear [ta'aneh] false witness against your neighbor" [Exodus 20:13], and likewise "to testify against him [la'anot bo] of wrongdoing" [Deuteronomy 19:16].{v1_03_02:2} Now the expression "your mouth has testified against you", whose meaning is that his mouth is the witness against himself, necessarily shows that at the time this expression was used there was a fixed law that a man's mouth and his confession are as witnesses in capital cases. And this principle agrees with the foundations of the law of all lawgivers, ancient and recent, down to our time, among all of whom the most trustworthy testimony was a person's testimony about himself, namely the confession of his mouth. And if we consider the words of the Torah that speak of witnesses in capital cases, we find that they can be explained in this direction in a fitting way; and no doubt the words of the Torah's law were understood so, in agreement with the expression mentioned, in the time of our earliest ancestors, and they thought that when the law of the Torah says "Whoever kills a person, the murderer shall be put to death at the mouth of witnesses; but one witness shall not testify against a person that he die" (Numbers 35), and likewise "At the mouth of two witnesses ... shall he that is to die be put to death; he shall not be put to death at the mouth of one witness" (Deuteronomy 17), it does not mean by this to exclude one who confesses of himself. For no special statute is needed for one who confesses of himself, since about one who confesses that he killed a person there is no doubt of the truth of the matter. But since for the most part a murderer does not confess of himself, and one must rely on the testimony of witnesses, Scripture speaks of the usual case, to judge by witnesses; and not to exclude one who confesses of himself, but to exclude a single witness. Therefore wherever it states that capital cases are to be decided by witnesses, its opposite is also stated explicitly, namely that they are not to be decided by one witness. Thus the meaning of the statute will be: at the mouth of two witnesses shall he that is to die be put to death, but not at the mouth of one witness. This is what it means to exclude, and not one who confesses of himself; for one who confesses, it is self-evident that we believe him, and all the more so. And this old interpretation has been preserved for us in an old aggadah (Yerushalmi Sanhedrin 6:1) that tells of Achan the Zarhite: Achan said to Joshua, "Do you catch me by a lot? Did not Moses our teacher teach us, 'at the mouth of two witnesses shall a matter be established'? You have already begun to err in judgment!" Joshua was afraid that they would bring an evil name upon the lots by which the land was to be divided; so Joshua began to appease Achan and to adjure him by the God of Israel, saying: "My son, give glory to the Lord God of Israel!" And Achan answered and said: "Indeed I have sinned!" (see Bavli Sanhedrin 43b and Numbers Rabbah ch. 23). It follows from the phrasing of these words that Achan's confession was considered as testimony and as proof of the matter in capital cases. On the other hand, we have not found even one incident from the time when they judged capital cases in which they refused to decide the case on the basis of a person's own confession. It is true that it is told of Simeon ben Shetaḥ (Sanhedrin 37b) that he ran after a man who was pursuing his fellow into a ruin, and saw a sword in the pursuer's hand dripping blood and the slain man writhing; Simeon said to him: "Wicked one, who killed this man, I or you? But what can I do, for the Torah said, 'at the mouth of two witnesses shall he that is to die be put to death.'" But there is no proof that if the murderer had confessed he would not have been sentenced to death; for what he said, "what can I do, for the Torah said, at the mouth of two witnesses shall he that is to die be put to death", means only this: since you do not confess, and there are no witnesses to the matter, I cannot sentence you to the death you deserve. However, halakhot have been handed down to us that teach that one is not put to death on a person's testimony about himself; and the clearest of them is what they said: "all those liable to death [are executed only] by a court, witnesses and warning" (Sanhedrin 80b; Tosefta ibid. ch. 10), the sense of their words being that it was said so precisely and to exclude self-confession. But for all that it appears clearly that as long as capital cases were judged, this opinion never became a matter of practice. Such opinions are only in the nature of theoretical opinions (theoriae), and did not come into practice; and this is because the later sages were opposed to the death penalty in general, as is said of R. Akiva and R. Tarfon (end of Makkot ch. 1). Only from the root of this wish to lighten the death penalties came various strange expositions founded on weak inferences; as when they say that if one of the things the Torah laid upon the witnesses cannot be fulfilled, as when the witnesses' hand is cut off, so that we cannot fulfil "the hand of the witnesses shall be first upon him" [Deuteronomy 17:7], then the death sentence of the accused is annulled (Sanhedrin 45a). And how strange are all the inferences they drew concerning the stubborn and rebellious son (ibid., Mishnah ch. 5)⚠3. Now surely from this root came also the rule that a man is not sentenced to death on his own confession, and all the details of the laws of the warning and of the acceptance of death.⚠4 But we have no means of bringing proof that a murderer was ever exempted from death although he confessed of himself, either because he had not been warned or because he had not accepted death upon himself. And in what follows we will succeed in bringing proof to the contrary.

Also from the history of Solomon's reign a bright light shines for us on the subject of the Torah and religion. Solomon, on coming to the throne, kept the command of David his father with all care. When he blessed all the congregation of Israel at the dedication of the house he had built, this was the request he asked of his people: that their heart be whole with the Lord, to walk in His ways and to keep His statutes all the days, as on the day they stood before Him (1 Kings 8:61). So too the proverbs that he left behind as a blessing, which are with us in Scripture, bear upon them the spirit of the Torah of Moses. And although in the historical narratives of his time, too, the traces of the Torah are very few, it cannot be denied that this little is of great value by its character, and especially in that one sees in it that the line and measure in practice was the inquiry into the reason of the statutes. This character, engraved on those few traces, was without doubt the root of an old aggadah telling that King Solomon inquired into the reasons of the commandments and stumbled (Sanhedrin 21b). And so it truly was, as we shall see. Of the building of the house that Solomon built it is told: "And the house, when it was being built, was built of stone made ready at the quarry; and neither hammer nor axe nor any tool of iron was heard in the house while it was being built" (1 Kings 6:7). From the place of their quarrying they removed great stones to lay the foundation of the house, hewn stones, and they carved and prepared the stones for building the house (ibid. 5:31–32). We see from all this that he wanted to build the house of hewn stones; but they did the cutting and the dressing at the quarry, so that the sound of iron was not heard in the house, for everything had been prepared beforehand. There is no doubt that the narrator alludes here to the statute written in the Torah: "And if you make Me an altar of stones, you shall not build it of hewn stones" (Exodus 20:22), and "you shall not lift up any iron upon them" (Deuteronomy 27:5). But from the sense of this statute it appears that it warns against lifting iron only upon the stones of the altar; but for the house, even for the inner sanctuary of the house, which is the Holy of Holies, they were not warned about this (Mekhilta Yitro ch. 11). Yet from the matter told here it appears that it speaks of the house as a whole, that no hammer or axe was heard in it. It is further made clear to us from what it said, "was not heard in the house while it was being built," that it meant that it was not heard inside the house, but they dressed the stones outside and brought them in. So on the one side they widened the prohibition to the whole house, and on the other side they permitted making the stones hewn, only on condition that they be dressed outside (see Sotah 48b). This widening on the one side and the permission on the other will become known to us in their reason if we grasp the cause why iron may not be lifted upon the stones of the altar. And this cause is stated explicitly in the Torah, namely: because lifting iron causes the profanation of the altar. It is not as among the other peoples in ancient times, who held unhewn stones in honor, so that the ancient Greeks worshipped such stones (Pausanias 7.22.3); and the reason why unhewn stones were honored and esteemed among them was that they still stood in their natural state, as they came from the hand of the Maker (Knobel, Ex. 20:25). Not so the reason of the Torah; it is without doubt because iron is destined to be the instrument of destruction in war, while the altar is destined to be the place from which life and peace come (Tosefta Bava Kamma ch. 10; Bavli and Yerushalmi Kiddushin ch. 1). And not by chance does Scripture use the words "for if you lift up your sword upon it, you have profaned it" [Exodus 20:22], to hint that the purpose of the altar is the opposite of the purpose of iron, which is the sword.{v1_03_04:1} Therefore it is no wonder that this statute was widened to the whole Temple, for the very same reason for which lifting the sword upon the altar was forbidden, since the altar is part of the whole Temple and the purpose of both is one. But on the other side they saw the fulfillment of this reason only when the iron was brought into the interior of the Temple, and did not object to dressing the stones outside. Also at the dedication of the house that Solomon made we find that he departed greatly from the statute of the Torah, in a way whose ground we cannot know unless we decide that he permitted himself to depart because he had reached the depth of the Torah's intent and grasped its reason and purpose. For Solomon made the dedication of the altar seven days, joined to the Festival, which fell at that time for seven days, so that in that year they kept a festival that lasted fourteen days and ended on the twenty-second of the seventh month, for on the twenty-third he sent the people away (1 Kings 8:65; 2 Chronicles 7:9–10). From this it appears that the festival of consecration began on the ninth of the month, and the seventh day was already the first day of the Feast of Tabernacles, and after it came seven days with the festival of the Eighth Day, which is the Atzeret. It follows that they began to celebrate the festival on the ninth of the month, which is the beginning of the Day of Atonement, as the plain wording of the Torah indicates: "on the ninth day of the month at evening ... you shall keep your sabbath" [Leviticus 23:32]. And in that year they did not keep the Day of Atonement according to its rule (Mo'ed Katan 9a). Surely this was because for a purpose as great as the dedication of the altar and the Temple they did not hesitate to depart from the statute; especially since the dedication of the altar also has the function of atonement, to make atonement for it, so that it be most holy (Exodus 29:37), like the Day of Atonement, which is also appointed to atone, and especially for the holy sanctuary and the altar. For this reason he deliberately made the ninth the first day of the festival of consecration, and was obliged also to count the first day of the Feast of Tabernacles as the festival of the dedication of the altar.{v1_03_05:1} And who knows whether from that time on, in memory of that event which took place on the Day of Atonement, they did not also make the whole Day of Atonement a day of festival and joy, as it is told: there were no festive days for Israel like the fifteenth of Av and the Day of Atonement, on which the daughters of Israel would go out and dance in the vineyards; and they applied to it the verse "Go forth, O daughters of Zion, and behold King Solomon with the crown with which his mother crowned him on the day of his wedding and on the day of the gladness of his heart" [Song of Songs 3:11]: "on the day of his wedding", this is the giving of the Torah; "and on the day of the gladness of his heart", this is the building of the Temple (Ta'anit 26b). Surely this refers only to the building of the Temple that Solomon built, when the Day of Atonement was a festival and a holiday; and for this reason they made the whole Day of Atonement a joy and a holiday, in memory of that Day of Atonement.

These several proofs will show that the method of interpreting the Torah was insight into the reasons of the statutes and reflection on their spiritual purpose. This also follows from all the religious traces that we have in the writings composed in the time of Solomon, whether he himself was the writer or one of the men of his generation, or that were written in the generations after him and speak of his affairs; so much so that we can almost say that these writings are the most correct interpretations of the statutes of the Torah. Let us consider here what their view was regarding the worship of God in the Temple. Just as we saw that already in the eyes of Samuel the sacrificial service had worth and value only as a means to attaining a moral goal, so, and with even greater force, this appears from the writings and the sayings attributed to Solomon or speaking from sources of his time. What in Solomon's eyes was the purpose of the house that he built, and what was its function? Was it to be only a house of sacrifice{v1_03_05:2} and no more? If we pay attention to the prayer that he prayed, we find that in his eyes the function of the house was to be an open spring for the house of Israel, from which would issue the sources of life for the spirit of religion. This house would gather the hearts of the individuals of the nation like sheaves to the threshing floor, and from there God's providence would go forth over the nation as a whole and over its individuals in particular. And in all the deeds that he did on that occasion, it appears that his conception of religion rose above material attributes; therefore the worship of God that Solomon established had no other purpose than to bring his people to a true knowledge of the God of their fathers and to unite all Israel at one point. So much for the worship of God in general! As for the sacrificial service, they did not depart from the way that Samuel had paved before them. It is true that both David and Solomon held to the sacrificial service as Moses commanded, but the service was very, very evil in their eyes if it was done according to the views of the multitude, who thought that only the outward act is what God desires; just as the matter was evil in the eyes of the prophets of their time and after them. Who was more zealous against the outward service in sacrifices than David in his psalms and Solomon in his proverbs? In clear words David declares his view: that God has no desire for sacrifice and offering, and has not asked for burnt offering and sin offering (Psalms 40:7). And another of the poets of his time also speaks in this direction, saying: that only then is the sacrifice acceptable to God if the one who sacrifices makes the covenant of the Lord over the sacrifice; God will not accept burnt offering and sacrifice, but He will accept a thanksgiving sacrifice which will be a sign of the feelings of his heart, for only by a thanksgiving sacrifice does he honor God (ibid. 50). And this same spirit hovers also over Solomon's words in his Proverbs. He too says that God does not desire the sacrifices of the wicked, and they are an abomination if they are brought with evil intent (Proverbs 21:27), and that to do justice and righteousness is more acceptable to the Lord than sacrifice (ibid. 21:3). And in the song that was sung for the glory of the dedication of the House, the one who ascends the mountain of the Lord to stand in His holy place is not asked to multiply his sacrifices and the cattle on a thousand hills, but is asked for clean hands and a pure heart, that he not lift up his soul to vanity nor swear deceitfully (Psalms 25:3, 4).⚠5 And if we look further at the psalms and songs that were sung when the tribes of the Lord went up to see the face of the Lord God three times a year, the root and reason of this statute also emerge for us from their content and from the idea that governs them: that the chief cause and purpose of the pilgrimage was, first and foremost, nothing other than to awaken and arouse in them national love and to strengthen the holy bond that unites all the individuals of the nation. The assemblies of the tribes of the Lord will be a testimony to Israel. And Jerusalem, which is the place of holiness, for there is the house of the Lord and there sat the thrones of the house of David, is itself the point from which this union goes forth. Therefore who will not ask after its peace? Will not all who love it prosper in its peace? And who will not speak peace in it for the sake of the brothers and companions who are joined in it soul to soul? (ibid. 122). And ideas like these emerge for us from all the songs that were composed to be sung as they went up to see the face of the Lord God in Jerusalem. And if it is said, "and they shall not appear before Me empty" [Exodus 23:15], and Deuteronomy states explicitly, "every man shall give as he is able, according to the blessing of the Lord thy God which He hath given thee" [Deuteronomy 16:17], this is not the main reason but a secondary condition. Now we will see how great the idea of this statute was according to the understanding of the early time, and how small, in comparison, its intention was according to what is described by the later ones in the days of the Second Temple, who already took the secondary as the main thing and applied petty niceties to it. And so we see in all this correct interpretations, and we rightly say that all the writings from that time and after it are the most correct interpretations of the statutes of the Torah.

One foundation which has no source except in the oral tradition came into being in the course of this period. This foundation is the hope for the revelation of the King Messiah. Many have sought the source of this hope in the Torah of Moses. But they erred in vision, and the matter is not so, for it cannot be. If we unroll before us the sublime visions that depict the face of the time in which this hope will be fulfilled, then we will see that its foundation is not in the Torah of Moses, and that by its nature it is not even possible to seek its foundation in the Torah and in the time of the nation's childhood. The moral perfection of all human society through the Israelite nation, which will raise the banner of the knowledge of the true God to all the peoples; a kingdom of God led by the hand of a human king of the seed of Judah; the flourishing of truth and uprightness, peace, love, general happiness, the knowledge of God and the fear of Him: these are the signs that mark the face of that time as the prophets described it. And where in the Torah of Moses is there even one hint of all these? And how can we search the Torah for a source for a hope of establishing a human kingdom, when according to the law and the will of the Torah there is to be in Israel only the kingdom of Heaven and no human king?{v1_03_06:1} And even if we say that according to the Torah too a human kingdom was within the bounds of the possible, still how could it enter our mind that the Torah would set up a thing that is against its will as the model of their hope and of their final national goal? Moreover, it plainly follows from the Torah of Moses that its intention was to implant in them the thought that they are a treasure among all the peoples and that their function is to be separate from the nations in their character, their statutes and their customs; and in the time of the nation's childhood this was very necessary, out of concern lest they mingle with the nations and learn their deeds. How then could it enter the mind that at that time it was proclaimed in the nation that one kingdom would be in the land, shared by Israel and all the nations together? (See Luzzatto in his commentary on Isaiah 2:5.) But let us see what are the traces in which, in their view, this hope is laid down. First, they hint at the general promises, some of them to the patriarchs (Genesis 17:4-7; 22:18; 26:4; 25:14;⚠6 35:11) and some to the nation itself (Leviticus 26:44, and in Deuteronomy in many places). But who does not know that all these promises are no more than general assurances that the Lord will keep His covenant with them, and that they have nothing to do with the hope of the Messiah? Only in Deuteronomy is it said, in a slight hint, that the function of the nation is also to be a banner to the nations: "and all the peoples of the earth shall see that the name of the Lord is called upon them,⚠7 and they shall be afraid of them" (Deuteronomy 28:10). But this too is very far from what the prophets describe of the hope of the Messiah. It is true that promises speaking of a particular individual are also hinted at. One is what is said in the blessing of Jacob: "The scepter shall not depart from Judah, nor the ruler's staff from between his feet, until Shiloh come, and unto him shall the obedience of the peoples be" (Genesis 49:8).⚠8 Many have wished to find in this promise a hint of the Messiah. One explains "until the man comes to whom the kingdom belongs" (Genesis Rabbah 99), or "he to whom they bring a gift [shai lo]" (*Yalkut*, remez 160); another says that Shiloh is a proper name of the Messiah (Sanhedrin 98b); and others say that Shiloh is like "Prince of Peace" [Isaiah 9:5], which is an epithet of the Messiah (Rosenmüller, Hengstenberg). See how weak this idea would be if it had no other foundation than such poor niceties. And it is very clear that among the ancients the word Shiloh was not expounded as referring to the Messiah; for if it were so, why did they not choose the name Shiloh to call the Messiah by, since it would be the epithet given him in the Torah? And if we explain the words according to their meaning and their true plain sense, far from the ways of midrash, it will become clear to us that the Torah did not think of hinting at the kingdom of the Messiah, but set before its eyes the function of the tribe of Judah at different times. For until they came to Shiloh, Judah went at the head of all the men of war in the conquest of the land; from the days of the Judges onward its dominion was held back, until the kingship came to David, "and unto him shall the obedience of the peoples be": not only all the tribes, who are also called peoples, but other peoples too served him and were placed under his rule. Therefore, whoever wishes to expound this saying of the Torah as referring to the Messiah by way of midrash or aggadic figure of speech, we say: leave him be, for great is the license given to every preacher. But to one who says that this is truly the intention of the Torah, we say that he errs and leads others astray. One of Balaam's prophecies too was expounded as referring to the Messiah. It is what he said: "I see him, but not now; I behold him, but not nigh; there shall step forth a star out of Jacob, and a scepter shall rise out of Israel, and shall smite through the corners of Moab, and break down all the sons of Seth. And Edom shall be a possession, Seir also shall be a possession, while Israel doeth valiantly" (Numbers 24:17-18). But anyone who has eyes will see that these words are aimed at the kingdom of David, for he was the man who smote through the corners of Moab and broke down all its helpers; he made Edom a possession, "and all Edom became servants to him" (2 Samuel 9);⚠9 and these peoples and their helpers were the sons of Seth, or the sons of tumult [she'on] (Jeremiah 48), whom he would break down, and did break down. And how could it enter the mind that their hope of enlarging the border in the days of the kingdom of the Messiah was only for the conquest of Moab and Edom and no more? Were not the borders of the Land of Israel enlarged still further at various times, as in the days of Solomon and also in the days of John Hyrcanus I, and still they hoped for the Messiah who would come? Indeed this error of expounding these verses as referring to a Messiah grew only from their seeing that R. Akiva expounded the verse "there shall step forth a star out of Jacob" as referring to Bar Kokhba, whom they held to be the Messiah. But who does not know that R. Akiva did not think to explain the verse; rather he expounded it by way of aggadah, as a play on words, to arouse the heart of the people, in the manner of preachers? But in truth the sages of the Talmud too did not think to rest the foundations of this hope, as it is described in the prophets, upon the Torah, since they knew that it is not found in it and its root is not in the Torah. Therefore we do not find that they ask anywhere, "Where in the Torah is the coming of the Messiah derived from?", as they ask in several places concerning the resurrection of the dead. Rather, the foundations on which this hope was built were the kingdom of David. Already in the days of David the idea took root in the nation that its function is to be an example to all the peoples, so that they too would learn to fear the Lord and to recognize that the Lord is one God and there is none beside Him. David's own heart was full of this great idea, and he sang of it in his psalms, in his hope: "Nobles shall come out of Egypt; Cush shall hasten to stretch out her hands unto God; ye kingdoms of the earth, sing unto Him" (Psalms 68:32, 33). And with still greater force this idea found its place in the song attributed to him, which he sang when he set the ark of God inside the tent (1 Chronicles 106:23-34; Psalms 96, 96).⚠10 The reign of David was the most exalted and the happiest of the reigns of all the kings of Israel in every respect. The political situation, the abundance of good and happiness in the land, and likewise the moral situation and the spread of knowledge and the fear of the Lord, stood at a level whose like had not been before him nor after him among all the kings. And when they saw that the moral state was very high and lofty, and that the tree of knowledge and the Torah grew and bore fruit, and when they saw that bound up with this their political success too was flourishing and their heritage very fair, this was a testimony to them that the height of their moral state is also the cause of their temporal success, and that because of it the happiness they had been promised of old reached them. And since David had raised them to this state, they therefore saw that the continuance of this happy state, and its rising from strength to strength in the future, was bound to David and his kingdom and would never be separated from it. This idea was strengthened by the prophets, who prophesied so in the name of the Lord. In particular Nathan the prophet poured strong confidence into David's heart when he said: "And thy house and thy kingdom shall be made sure for ever; thy throne shall be established for ever" (2 Samuel 7:16). Such promises were repeated many times, as we see from David's last words to his son Solomon, when he handed on to him such a promise that he had received in the name of the Lord: "There shall not fail thee a man on the throne of Israel" (1 Kings 2:4). These assurances sank deep into the chambers of David's heart, in his hope that the Lord would make great His salvation and show kindness to His anointed, to David and to his seed for ever. Yet, like every great idea that in its childhood is not yet clear, because opinions have not yet been purified, and reveals itself only little by little until it shines forth in its strength, so it was with the idea of the perfecting of the world through the kingdom of the house of David, which already at the beginning of Solomon's reign began to reveal itself with greater force. In clear words Solomon speaks of this perfecting when he prays to the Lord that He fulfil the requests of the stranger who will pray in His sanctuary, "that all the peoples of the earth may know Thy name, to fear Thee, as do Thy people Israel" (1 Kings 8){v1_03_08:1} and "that all the peoples of the earth may know that the Lord, He is God; there is none else" (ibid. 9).⚠11 The desire that all these good things be fulfilled, and that all the wishes of his heart come true in the future, was the reason that Solomon strove to show the peoples the wisdom that flows from the source of the Lord's religion; and his desire also prospered in his hand, for the fame of his wisdom went forth for the name of the Lord (ibid. 10:1). And what was this wisdom? Not the wisdom of the children of the East, of Tyre and Sidon, Canaan and its borders; not the wisdom of Egypt, Cush and Babylon and their regions, all of whose wisdom came to naught in the course of time; but his wisdom was a wisdom that will never be removed. For the contemplation of the sublime sights in the nature of created things, the contemplation of man in his moral qualities, and the contemplation of God's providence as seen in the history of man in particular and of the nations of the world in general: this was his wisdom. And from where was this wisdom taken? From the Torah of Moses in the first place, and from the teachings of the prophets and of the sages of the generations and their poets. The spirit that was in these was not a spirit of vanity from the vanities of the nations; therefore it was not a passing spirit but one that stands and endures and is fitting for all times and for all mankind together. With wisdom like this Solomon could hope that it would find lovers among the peoples and would win their hearts, to bring them into the covenant of the Lord. These ideas are written by the hand of the poets of the time in their songs of praise. In the song that was sung in honor of Solomon on the day he came to the throne, the poet speaks in praise of the king and depicts him in the excellence of righteousness and justice in his judgment of the poor of his people: that he will save the children of the needy and crush the oppressor; for his sake the mountains will bear peace to the people as long as the moon endures; he shall have dominion from sea to sea and from the River to the ends of the earth; before him the desert-dwellers will bow, all kings shall prostrate themselves before him, all nations shall serve him; there shall be abundance of grain in the land upon the top of the mountains, its fruit shall rustle like Lebanon, and they of the city shall blossom like grass of the field; his name shall endure for ever, and all nations shall be blessed in him and call him happy (Psalms 72). And if we consider this picture, here before us is the picture in its completeness, as the prophets describe the King Messiah, his time and his kingdom. It is indeed true that the kingdom of David is the foundation of the hope of the Messiah. But the first point of the beginning of this hope was not in the time of David or of Solomon. For, according to what we have seen, the kingdom of the house of David was the sublime picture in whose likeness they hoped for the kingdom of the King Messiah. And it is self-evident that during David's reign there was no room for such a hope, since the hoped-for kingdom was present in actuality, and what would hope do? Rather, the hope of the Messiah began to sprout when the success of the kingdom went backward; for so it lies in the nature of things: when the good is lost, we seek comfort in the hope that the days to come will bring it back to us and will be better than the present. Already in Solomon's last days signs of decline were evident in his kingdom. For the ways in which Solomon went to achieve the mission of Israel⚠12 were not the right ways. He favored the peoples of the land who were his servants and gave them leave to act according to the law of their religion, hoping that in the course of time they would turn and recognize the true religion. But the opposite happened, for the abominations of the nations became a snare to him and to his people. He increased the glory of his house with outward splendor, thinking to exalt his kingdom by this, but this was his undoing, for under the pressure of the taxes and his heavy yoke the people were crushed and the strength of their endurance failed; therefore his kingdom did not continue over all Israel. In the days of his son Rehoboam, who erred in his father's errors and did even worse than he, the kingdom was already divided. And because of this they moved far away from the goal. The confidence they had placed in the continuance of the house of David came to nothing. Before two generations had passed, their political and moral success had passed away into nothing; and in such a time the longing to restore what had been lost grows, and it is the mother of the hope that the good will return in the future; and in the evil blows to the kingdom they saw only a temporary delay, not eternal despair. Therefore in this evil time the hope of the Messiah began to sprout. And we rightly say that the reign of David was the quarry from which the stones were hewn to build the palace of the sublime idea of the revelation of the kingdom of the Messiah. Two generations after this the first stone of its foundation was laid in Zion; and builders labored to complete the building of the palace in all the generations that came, setting stone upon stone. The house was one in its form and structure from outside, but its structure within and all its inner parts changed time after time, according to the change of the spirit of the time, according to the prevailing concepts, and also according to the demands that the time made. Therefore its completion to the end is still wrapped from our eyes in the darkness of distant times⚠13 that are yet to come, and it is to us today still a hidden riddle. And this riddle is one with the great and hidden secret of the goal of the development of enlightenment, of the life of society and of the justice of law, of morality and of faith, for all the multitude of mankind together.

Now we see that the first point from which the hope of the revelation of the kingdom of the Messiah went out was the time of the decline and the division of the kingdom of the house of David. But from that time no traces of it have been preserved for us in the Scriptures. Both the writers of Chronicles and the prophets from the days of Rehoboam to the days of Uzziah are silent about it. Amos was the first who speaks of it in his prophecies. Only he does not mention who the Messiah will be, nor his tribe and his stock. But he says that the fallen tabernacle of David will be raised up from the time the kingdom was divided, and they will possess the remnant of Edom and all the nations; and the captivity of Israel, which was taken captive in the wars that the kings of Judah and Israel had with their neighbours, God will bring back and plant them upon their land, and they will no more be pulled up out of their land (Amos 9:11–15). There is one ancient prophecy, perhaps more ancient than the prophecy of Amos, which, although it does not speak of the kingdom of the house of David, is the most precious, because it makes clear the final goal set for all the nations together. That prophecy is found in Isaiah and also in Micah, and it is very likely that both of them took it from an ancient prophet whose name is not stated (Rosenmüller, Hitzig). The content of this prophecy is: "And it shall come to pass in the end of days, that the mountain of the Lord's house shall be established ... and peoples shall flow unto it. And many nations shall go and say: Come, and let us go up to the mountain of the Lord, and to the house of the God of Jacob; and He will teach us of His ways, and we will walk in His paths; for out of Zion shall go forth the Torah ..." And after that he speaks of the perfection of justice, of judgment, of love, and of peace between man and man and between nation and nation, and of the good and the abundance of prosperity that there will be in those days (Micah 4:1–4; Isaiah 2:2–4). On the foundations of this prophecy Isaiah built his picture of the Messiah, in a sublime manner without equal. "And there shall come forth a shoot out of the stock of Jesse ... and the spirit of the Lord shall rest upon him, the spirit of wisdom and understanding, the spirit of counsel and might, the spirit of knowledge and of the fear of the Lord." He will raise up justice and faithfulness and remove wickedness and violence from the earth. He will awaken love and make peace between man and man and between nation and nation; and oppression and wickedness will no more hurt nor destroy, but the whole earth will be full of the knowledge of the Lord. "And this root of Jesse, that stands for an ensign of the peoples, unto him shall the nations seek, and his resting-place shall be glorious" (Isaiah 11:1–10). When Isaiah prophesied this prophecy, his eyes did not roam over some final time and the end of days; rather his eye and his heart were set on a man who was already among the living in his days and ready to ascend to the kingship, and this man was Hezekiah. The prophet had observed his ways and knew his talents and his spiritual and moral character, and from him he hoped for all these things: that he would restore the kingdom and its perfection as at the first, and that in his days the dispersed of Israel who had been driven away to the land of Assyria in the days of Pekah son of Remaliah would be gathered, and that he would gather the scattered of Judah, who had been scattered time after time, and especially in the days of his father Ahaz (2 Chronicles 29); and then Ephraim would not envy Judah and Judah would not vex Ephraim (Isaiah ibid. 12, 13). Therefore it is surely very likely that the prophet, when he speaks of gathering the exiles from their lands, does not lift his eyes to a distant time, with the intent of exiles in the future, but to the exiles who had already gone into exile: they are the captives of their wars with the nations and those dispersed in the lands. We see also in another prophecy how strong was the hope that Isaiah set on the reign of Hezekiah, when he says of the son who is given to us and upon whose shoulder is the government (that is, Hezekiah) that his name will be called "Wonderful Counsellor, Mighty God,{v1_03_10:1} Everlasting Father, Prince of Peace; that the government may be increased, and of peace there be no end, upon the throne of David and upon his kingdom, to establish it and to uphold it through justice and through righteousness" (ibid. 9:5, 6). Now in all these pictures we see that their source was the picture of the kingdom of David as it truly was and lived in their vision. The prophet Hosea too speaks of the good that is to come to the Israelite nation, and the marks of the hoped-for good are: the eternity of the nation (Hosea 2:21), its temporal happiness (ibid. 23); and then Israel will sprout and blossom as in the days of its youth and as on the day it came up out of Egypt (ibid. 17); at that time the children of Judah and Israel will be gathered together and appoint themselves one head (ibid. 2), and this head will be the king in Judah, a shoot from the roots of David (ibid. 3:5). And greater than this, we see that one prophet (the first Zechariah), of the contemporaries of Isaiah, in comparing the image of the kingdom of the Messiah to the kingdom of David, also uses, to describe the kingdom of the Messiah, expressions that are taken word for word from the expressions with which his predecessors, who lived and wrote in the days of David, described the kingdom of the house of David (Zechariah 9:10; Psalms 72:8). The prophet Zephaniah makes the chief principle of his picture the recognition of the Lord by all the peoples, that all of them call upon His name and serve Him with one consent. Only the King of Israel, the Lord, will be in their midst (Zephaniah 3:9, 15); but he does not mention a human kingdom of the house of David, just as Amos, and likewise the prophet whose words were copied into the prophecies of Isaiah and Micah, did not mention it. This spirit and this thought lie in the words of all the prophets who prophesied in the days of the kings. Seeing that all the kings who reigned in their days did not have the qualities and the virtues fit to fulfil their hope and to match the pictures that they had drawn for themselves in their vision of the happy kingdom whose coming they hoped for, they turned their faces still further to the future and prophesied the realization of this happy state in days to come. But for all of them the image and the character of the hoped-for king were one. Indeed, in all the days of these prophets, while the kingdom of Judah still stood and the strength of the nation had not yet failed to the point of ruin, this hope found the possibility of its fulfilment in the strength of the nation itself, and everything depended on their return to their moral calling, which they had on the side of religion and Torah. That is: on the side of the people's spirit, that there be a faithful will to return to the good that they had abandoned and to hold fast to it; and on the side of the king, it was required that he have spiritual talents in fitting measure and also strength of bronze to turn the steps of the people to the right way and to lead it on this way step by step to the goal of perfection. And if their calling were always before their eyes, so that the kingdom would grow strong, then their hope and the divine promise would be fulfilled of themselves, and there would be no need for wonders and signs to be done on the earth to fill the hope. Therefore we do not find that the early prophets prophesy of special wonders, of signs and portents in heaven and on earth that go out beyond the bounds of nature, that would be done at the time when the promise and the kingdom of the King Messiah, for which they hoped, would be fulfilled. It was not so in the later time. Already in the days of Jeremiah the strength of the nation was broken to destruction. They had corrupted the way of morality; faithfulness had perished and was cut off from their mouth. And there was no man who did justice, who sought kindness and righteousness. The prophets too prophesied by Baal, and the priests, those who handle the Torah, did not know the Lord. From that time all confidence was lost from the true prophets, and their eyes, which waited for the return of the good and for the renewal of an enduring kingdom in the likeness of the kingdom of the house of David, no longer roamed over the present or the near generation. Jeremiah's heart was firm and sure that the fall of the kingdom of Judah was a destruction decreed by the Lord, and that there was no more hope of upholding it; the shepherds who shepherd the people would not hold back the evil decreed against them, for they themselves were destroying the sheep of their pasture and scattering them and driving them away. Because of this evil state, and under the pressure of such deeds, the prophet despaired of all hope of the return of the kingship to the house of David in a natural way; but he hoped that in a miraculous and wondrous way the kingship would return to Zion. Not from the hand of man would salvation come to them, but only from God: He that scattered Israel will gather him and redeem him from the hand of him that is stronger than he, and will make a new covenant with them, to put His Torah in their heart (Jeremiah 31); great and hidden things He will do with them, until they are a name and a glory to all the nations of the earth (ibid. 33); and He will multiply them and make them honoured, and their children will be as of old, and their congregation will be established before Him, and He will punish their oppressors (ibid. 31);⚠14 and at that time He will cause a shoot of righteousness to grow up unto David, and Judah will be saved and Jerusalem will dwell safely, and this is the name by which she will be called: The Lord is our righteousness (ibid. 33). And Jerusalem will be called: the throne of the Lord, and all the nations will be gathered to it, to the name of the Lord, to Jerusalem (ibid. 3). Now we see that in all these acts no human hand will be in the midst, but the Lord will work great wonders to restore the kingdom of the house of David as at the first.

This comes out to us even more from the words of the prophets who prophesied at the time of the exile. How they had despaired of all hope in the days of the prophet Ezekiel, and said: "Our bones are dried up, and our hope is lost" (Ezekiel 37:11). Therefore he prophesies to them that it will not be so; rather the bones will live, and He will bring them up out of their graves and bring them to their land. Already in this picture, in which the prophet likens the return of the nation to its happy state to the resurrection of the dead who have already died, a thing that cannot happen in a natural way, we see that the prophet hoped for wonders that God would do to fulfil His promise. And he speaks of the great judgments that will be done on Gog and Magog and on many peoples, with pestilence and with blood, and an overflowing rain and great hailstones, "and I will magnify Myself, and sanctify Myself, and make Myself known" among the nations (ibid. 38:18–23); and a king will arise for the house of Israel, and the kingdom will no more be divided into two kingdoms, and this king will be of the house of David, and he will be prince over them for ever (ibid. 37). In the words of one prophet whose name is not known,{v1_03_11:1} a man of this same time, the picture is drawn in miraculous images: signs and portents will be done in heaven and on earth, the sun will be turned into darkness and the moon into blood before the day of the Lord comes (Joel 3:3, 4). And how very great are the wonders that Zechariah prophesied would be done on the day when the great day comes (Zechariah 14). And in the prophecies of Malachi the coming of Elijah the prophet to announce the redemption is already spoken of.{v1_03_12:1} Such are all the pictures of the establishment of the kingdom of the Messiah among all the later prophets, and they differ from the pictures that the early prophets had; but they were one in regard to the repair of the world and the establishment of the kingdom under a righteous king from the root of David.

However, from the prophecies that were prophesied at the end of the days of the exile by a prophet whose name is not known (Isaiah 40–66), it appears that he described the matter in a manner very different from the way the prophets described it in all the days of the kings and of the Temple; indeed, his descriptions were as the time in which he prophesied asked of him. There it is not hinted, not even in one word, of the founding of a new kingdom under a ruling sceptre of the kingdom of the house of David, a shoot out of the stock of Jesse. And although he mentions the making of an everlasting covenant, "the sure mercies of David" (ibid. 56:3),⚠15 for all that we see how careful the prophet was not to speak of the establishment of the kingdom of the house of David; he makes the fulfilment of the promise depend on Cyrus king of Persia, and this king he calls a shepherd who performs the pleasure of the Lord (44:28), and likewise he calls him by the name: the Lord's anointed (45:1). And why all this? Only because those prophecies were not meant for the people of the Lord alone, but also for the Persian king, for Cyrus (see above, chapter 1), and perhaps some of them were directed to him in particular, for from him they hoped and asked that he would fulfil the word of the Lord. Not so the prophecies of Ezekiel, who also prophesied in the exile, but at a time when the wicked kingdom of Babylon still stood, and his prophecies are directed to his own people in particular. And those prophecies of that prophet whose name is not known to us, and which are ascribed to the prophet Isaiah, bore fruit in the heart of Cyrus, and he believed in them with complete faith, so much so that he said: "All the kingdoms of the earth has the Lord, the God of heaven, given me; and He has charged me to build Him a house in Jerusalem, which is in Judah" (Ezra 1:3).⚠16 And it is very likely that the words of Cyrus, when he says that the Lord gave him all the kingdoms and charged him to build the house, are directed to the prophecies that were appended to the book of the prophecies of Isaiah. Now after this truth and these matters, we have found one principle of faith that was founded on tradition and whose origin is from the days of the first kings. Yet the pictures of it changed from time to time according to the change of the spirit of the time, according to the ideas that ruled among the people, and also according to the demands that the time made.
