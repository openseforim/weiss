v1_07_04:1) Graetz, vol. 3, [p.] 516, wrote that the Pharisees said that a stream coming from an unclean vessel and poured into a clean vessel does not render the clean one unclean; he meant to say the reverse, a stream coming from a clean vessel into the unclean vessel. In my opinion the Sadducees based their view on the interpretation of the verse "and all drink that may be drunk in every such vessel shall be unclean" (Leviticus 11:34), and they held that vessels render food and drink unclean through their airspace, as in the initial supposition of the Sifra, Shemini ch. 9. If so, in their view logic dictates: since the part of the stream that has come into the airspace of the unclean vessel has become unclean, how can we say that half of the stream is unclean and half clean? Necessarily one must say that the whole stream is unclean, and consequently what is in the clean vessel is unclean as well.

v1_07_04:2) This dispute is not mentioned in the Talmud, but I found an allusion to it in Yerushalmi Sanhedrin 1:3, where they said: "At first they used to write in deeds of ḥalitzah: In the session of so-and-so and so-and-so, and she spat before us spittle that was seen on the ground." There it appears that this is an ancient ordinance; and although in Yevamot (39b) it is brought that Rav Judah ordained so, it is possible that the ordinance had lapsed, as the word "at first" indicates, and Rav Judah restored it to its former state. It seems to me that they took care to write "spittle that was seen on the ground" against the Sadducees, who said "in his face" literally; and when the hand of the Sadducees declined they abolished this, and Rav Judah ordained anew a deed of ḥalitzah for the reasons explained in the Gemara, and ordained it like the ordinance of the ancients, to write "that was seen on the ground," although its being seen on the ground is not indispensable (see Yerushalmi Yevamot end of ch. 12, and Tosafot Yevamot ibid.); but at first the ordinance was to write precisely "that was seen on the ground" against the opinion of the Sadducees.

v1_07_06:1) R. Abraham Geiger, in his book *Urschrift*, from p. 100 onward, speaks wisely about the origins of the sect of the Sadducees. Some of his words were a guide to me. I have seen fit to copy briefly what he wrote there concerning the beginning of the sect. At first the dispute was born of the jealousy of one man toward another. For the Sadducees, that is, the priests of the house of Zadok, being from of old the chosen ones of the priesthood, were also the heads in every matter, both in political and in religious matters; they were the heads of the Scribes, and from them went forth instruction for commandment, for statutes and for judgments. It is self-evident that they took their part in the expansion of the Torah, and therefore they were the bearers of tradition. In the course of time they were pushed from their greatness, and this caused jealousy and rivalry against the other sages, and for this reason hearts and opinions were divided (ibid., p. 133). But he drew from this the conclusion that the Sadducees held to what was received, and that the ancient halakhah and the tradition are their work, while the expansion and the new midrash are the work of the Pharisees. This conclusion is not correct in my opinion. For if the Sadducees who separated were of the Scribes, then it becomes clear to us from this that before the separation the Pharisees and the Sadducees were one, and both together engaged in the midrash of the Torah, in understanding Scripture, in care and deliberateness in judgment, in raising up many disciples, and in making a fence for the Torah, as the Men of the Great Assembly commanded. And we see the Pharisees after the separation walking in all these qualities, while against this we see that the Sadducees after the separation did not walk in this way and lacked all these qualities. From this we judge that because of their jealousy and rivalry they left the first path in which they had walked together with the Pharisees and went a way of their own; therefore the Sadducees were the opponents and not the reverse, for the Pharisees remained in their first position, only advancing in it from step to step, whereas the Sadducees turned from the path in which they had walked before. Such is the case of every opponent from of old, even if his opposition is for some other reason.⚠6 And if we hold that from of old the Scribes and the sages expanded and interpreted the Torah, we must admit that the Pharisees fulfilled this office after the separation as before, and conversely we must also admit that the Sadducees did not do so after the separation; therefore it was they who turned from the way of what was received, and not the Pharisees.

v1_07_09:1) It is a bad practice, which has almost become the custom in our generations, that several authors hang conjectures on a slight and doubtful hint; and when they find in some saying in the Talmud a little resemblance between it and the opinion of some sect among the sects, they at once decide that the saying came from the mouth of one of the members of that sect. And not only this, but sometimes they create sects out of the material of a single word, like the one who makes the Vatikin into Essenes and the "poor" in Psalms into a sect of Levites; and in truth there is neither taste nor smell in these and their like.
