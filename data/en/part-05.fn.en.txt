v1_03_19:1) His meaning in the word "His statutes" here is like the matter of judgments that God laid upon them; and its parallel is "before the decree bring forth" etc. (Zephaniah 2:2), where the meaning is: before the divine judgment that was decreed⚠9 for them is born. This is like the doubled saying: "before the fierce anger of the Lord come upon you." And of this kind also is what is said in Micah (7:11): "In the day that thy walls are to be built, in that day shall the decree be far removed" [yirḥak-ḥok]. The commentators strained in explaining it, and some of them join yirḥak-ḥok into one word, as an intensification of the matter, that is: that day is very far off. But it means: the day will come to build thy walls, and on that day the decree that fell upon him of old will be removed.

v1_03_20:1) See Zunz, *G[ottesdienstliche] V[orträge]*, p. 15, note f, who counts ten psalms which in his opinion were written during the seventy years of their exile. Ewald, in his commentary on Psalms, p. 217, counts nine more. According to the testimony of Eichhorn in his *Introduction* to Holy Scripture, vol. 3, p. 469, the Alexandrians already attributed psalms to Jeremiah and Ezekiel, Haggai and Zechariah. And de Wette, in the introduction to his commentary on Psalms, p. 22, cites in the name of Augusti, in his *Introduction*, p. 189, that Psalm 48⚠10 is to be attributed to Mordecai, who composed it in honor of the Persian king. I do not agree with all these conjectures, but it is clear to every intelligent student that several psalms of the Psalter are children of the time of the exile. And see *Moreh Nevukhei ha-Zeman*, gate 11.

v1_03_20:2) Besides the prophecies of Ezekiel, Haggai, Zechariah and Malachi, and some of the prophecies attributed to Isaiah, namely chapters 13, 14, 23⚠11–27, 34, 35, and from chapter 40 to the end of the whole book. On all this see the commentaries of Gesenius, Ewald, Hitzig, Knobel and all the other Christian commentators, and the *History of Israel* of R. Herzfeld, vol. 1, p. 290, and *Moreh Nevukhei ha-Zeman*, ibid. Also concerning the last prophecies in Jeremiah, chapters 50 and 51, from verse 1 to 58, they have cast doubt and assigned them to the time of the exile; see Herzfeld, ibid., 284. And since our purpose here is only to show the breadth of the prophetic literature of the time of the exile, what we have hinted at is enough.

v1_03_20:3) The books of Samuel and Kings, in the form in which we have them, cannot have been composed before the latter half of the exile. And it is clear that the last writer, that is, the one who joined the separate histories into one complete book, was one and the same for both these books. For we see that the writer of the book of Samuel knew of the death of David (2 Samuel 5:5), and therefore it would surely have been fitting for him to close his book with the account of David's death. He did not do so, and this shows that he meant to continue his narrative further. And he began this continuation with the account of David's last words, because they are connected with the first matters of Solomon's reign. Accordingly the book of Kings is nothing but the continuation of the book of Samuel, and both flowed from one source. If so, the final closing of the two books cannot be dated earlier than the second half of the days of the exile at the least, as follows for us from the close of the book of Kings. And from the difference in character and language between these two books and their parts no proof can be brought that they are by different authors (as de Wette holds in his *Introduction*, p. 262, edition of the year 1845), for the last writer was for the most part only a collector from old sources and copied from them in their wording and their language, adding only what was needed for joining and connecting; therefore they cannot have one character. Nor, for this reason, can a proof of the antiquity of the whole work be brought from what is said (1 Samuel 27:6), "Therefore Ziklag pertaineth unto the kings of Judah unto this day," which would show that this was written at a time when the kingdom was already divided but kings of Judah still sat on the throne. For here too we say: since the author composed his book from ancient sources, he without doubt copied this passage⚠12 from an ancient source and left it in its original form. But since this inquiry into the antiquity of the books is not within the purpose of our work, it is enough for us to have remarked this, and we refrain from inquiring further into the other books that we have and that are doubtful, which perhaps are children of this time and perhaps not. We remark further only on the book of the prophecies of Obadiah, which the destruction and the exile must necessarily precede. And likewise we remark that we see that several books were attributed to that time, such as Daniel, Ezra and Nehemiah, and several books of the Apocrypha, some of them perhaps rightly and some of them wrongly; from which it follows for us at any rate that at all times people thought and believed that that time, that is, the time of the exile, was the time of the flowering of Hebrew literature.

v1_03_21:1) Below, in chapter 23, I have spoken of the time of the composition of the book of Daniel, and I decided that the book as it lies before us is late and cannot be dated earlier than the time of the Maccabees. In any case it appears that the story in chapter 1 was received in the nation, and Ezekiel will prove it; and therefore, from the fact that Daniel's righteousness is described in his keeping far from drinking their wine, it is clear that this was a custom of prohibition from days of old.

v1_03_24:1) These are the words of the verse there: "Sigh in silence, make no mourning for the dead; bind your headdress upon you, and put your shoes on your feet, and do not cover your upper lip, and do not eat the bread of men" [Ezekiel 24:17]. From this it appears that he distinguished between the inner mourning, which is in the heart, and the outward signs of mourning. He was commanded not to perform the outward signs, but he may grieve for his wife in his heart. Perhaps these words of the prophet are the source of what is said in the Mishnah, that even where one is not permitted to mourn, he grieves, for grief (aninut) is only in the heart (Sanhedrin 46b); and see what I wrote in my book *Mishpat Leshon ha-Mishnah*, p. 48. As for the outward signs of mourning, the Torah has the uncovering of the head and the rending of the garments (Leviticus 21:10). In the opinion of some, the uncovering of the head is the same as the removal of the headdress [pe'er], and the matter of both is the removal of the turban (see the Septuagint translation; so too Rosenmüller and Michaelis understood it).

v1_03_24:2) I will remark here on the statute of the impurity of priests: "And they shall come at no dead person to defile themselves, except for father and for mother, etc." [Ezekiel 44:25]; and in the Torah a phrase is added: "except for his kin, that is near to him" [Leviticus 21:2]. From the fact that he left out this phrase it appears that he did not think that it points to a particular relative, but that it is only a general term followed by a specification, where the specification explains the general term; and the prophet left out the general term because it suffices to list the particulars. But in the Talmud "his kin" (she'ero) is expounded: this is his wife (Yevamot 22b, 90b; Sifra Emor ch. 1). This midrash is necessarily late, and it seems that it is an offshoot of their midrash on inheritances, where they expounded she'ero as referring to the wife (Bava Batra 111b). Perhaps the Sadducees disputed this halakhah, that the husband inherits his wife, as they disputed other halakhot in the laws of inheritance (ibid. 116b), because they did not find the matters stated explicitly in the Torah; and the Pharisees wished to give it a hint from the Torah and found it in the word she'ero. Once she'ero had been explained as referring to the wife in one place, they necessarily had to expound the "she'ero" written in the law of the impurity of priests in this sense too. Therefore it seems that the halakhah that a priest defiles himself for his wife is late. It further seems that this halakhah was still weak in their hands even at a late time, as we see in the story of Joseph the priest, who did not want to defile himself for his wife, and his brothers the priests pushed him and defiled him against his will (Sifra Emor ch. 1; Zevaḥim 100a). And Josephus, *Antiquities*, part 1, book 3, ch. 12, likewise does not mention this.
