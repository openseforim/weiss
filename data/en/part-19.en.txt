Chapter Nineteen

The House of Shammai — the House of Hillel —

Shammai and Hillel founded two houses for midrash and for halakhah, and they brought the Oral Torah to a new level. We have already seen that inquiry into the Torah and into the halakhah widened. In the midrash of the Torah they were occupied with finding the source of the halakhah, and they also tried to clarify the halakhot by their dialectic. Hillel investigated and inquired to grasp the reason of each halakhah, and according to his reasoning he judged whether it should stand or be set aside. But to Shammai every ancient halakhah was a statute not to be transgressed, and he tried with his dialectic to give it standing in every matter and in every way. So it emerges from the words told in their names, and it will become still clearer from the disputes of the House of Hillel and the House of Shammai, a great part of which may be ascribed to their time. Before that time there was no dispute in Israel, and the reason has already been made known to us why from their time the bond of agreement was loosened. "The beginning of strife is as when one lets out water" [Proverbs 17:14], and among the disciples it became a flooding river. But the fruit of the dispute was the widening of the halakhot without end. And like the sight we saw in the opposition of the Sadducees, which was a cause for the increase and widening of the halakhot, so that new laws were born, so we will see in the dispute of the Houses: it was the mother of new statutes that the ancients had not imagined. Of Hillel's disciples who sat before him two are mentioned by name: Jonathan ben Uzziel, the greatest among them, and Rabban Yoḥanan ben Zakkai, the least among them. Jonathan ben Uzziel they praised greatly and extolled his learning (Sukkah 28a), but we have from him no halakhic statement that he taught; only from a deed that he did is a halakhic statement learned (Bava Batra 134a). To him they ascribed the Targum of the Prophets (Megillah 3a); but whether the Targum as we have it is from him still needs proof.{v1_09_22:1} R. Yoḥanan, the least: Hillel himself called him father of wisdom and father of generations (Yerushalmi Nedarim 5:7, and Bavli Sukkah there); and it is true that he became a father of generations, and he saved the remnant of Israel from destruction. There was yet another sage who lived at that time and is mentioned by name, and he was a disciple of Hillel: Yoḥanan ben ha-Ḥoroni.{v1_09_22:2} No halakhot are mentioned in his name, but it is told of him that once the elders of the House of Shammai and the elders of the House of Hillel went to visit him and found him sitting with his head and most of his body in the sukkah and his table inside the house; and the elders of the House of Shammai said to him: If you used to act so, you never fulfilled the commandment of the sukkah in your life (Sukkah 2:6).⚠1 The sense of these words shows that Yoḥanan was not of the House of Shammai. And once he did not want to accept moist olives from R. Zadok for fear of impurity, for he thought that R. Zadok followed the House of Shammai, since he was one of them; until he appeased Yoḥanan and said that although he is a disciple of the House of Shammai, all the same he acts only according to the words of the House of Hillel (Yevamot 15b; Tosefta Sukkah ch. 2). We see from this that Yoḥanan ha-Ḥoroni was of the House of Hillel and R. Zadok of the House of Shammai. Of Shammai's disciples four are named. The first is R. Zadok, as we proved above, and he clung with all his strength to the House of Hillel, as will become clear further from his history. The second was Bava ben Buti, a pious man, greatly honored, whose words were heeded among his people. Herod blinded his eyes and killed most of his family (*Antiquities*). And after Herod had taken the light of his eyes, he went himself secretly to his house and disguised himself to him, in order to probe his opinion about him and about his rule; and when he recognized from his words that Bava ben Buti had no intention to do him evil or harm, neither in deed nor in word, Herod turned to him for good, and perhaps in his heart he regretted the evil he had done him; and from that day on he took counsel with Bava from time to time (Bava Batra 4b). He was more God-fearing than many and trembled at the word of the Lord{v1_10_02:1} (Keritot 6:3). And they greatly extolled his piety and his forbearance (Nedarim 66b). Particular halakhot we do not have from him, only one tradition that he handed down in the name of Shammai the Elder (Gittin 57b). Although he was of Shammai's disciples, all the same he followed the House of Hillel, and he was the one who worked to fix the halakhah according to the House of Hillel (Betzah 20b; Yerushalmi Hagigah 2:3; Tosefta there, ch. 2). The third was Dosetai of Kefar Yatmah; he brought one halakhah in the name of Shammai (Orlah 2:5) to refute the words of his colleagues of the House of Shammai and to uphold the words of the House of Hillel. The fourth was Yo'ezer of the Birah. He was one of Shammai's disciples and lived in the days of Rabban Gamaliel the Elder, who was head of the House of Hillel; and he brought from him a halakhah which he agreed with and accepted (Orlah 2:12).

Of the halakhot mentioned in the name of the House of Shammai and the House of Hillel, some may be ascribed to the time of Shammai and Hillel or close after their death, and most of them were taught during the time from the death of these two leaders until the destruction of the Temple. For when the Temple was destroyed, the name of the House of Shammai sank away entirely, because all the sages joined into one fellowship to strive for freedom of inquiry, even though they did not agree on one opinion in the study of the Torah; and then the houses of study multiplied. The difference of opinions between these two Houses became a stumbling block for the study of the Oral Torah, so that later [sages] complained of the change by which the times had changed: at first there was no dispute in Israel, but when the disciples of Shammai and Hillel who had not served [their masters] sufficiently multiplied, disputes multiplied in Israel and the Torah became like two Torahs (Sanhedrin 88b, and the Yerushalmi and Tosefta there, and in many places). And from then on disputes did not cease, even after the Houses ceased. The aim of both was to uphold religion according to the views of the Pharisees and to make a fence for the Torah, but they differed in the choice of the way to reach that aim. The character of their teachings is easily recognized if we consider the nature of their disputes. The number of their sayings in all is three hundred and sixteen. They are of three kinds: halakhot, midrashim, and fences. The number of halakhot is two hundred and twenty-one; the number of midrashim, twenty-nine; and the number of fences, sixty-six. Their disputes, by the orders of the Mishnah, are as follows. In the order Zera'im there are thirty-five halakhot,{v1_10_03:1} in seven of which the House of Shammai is lenient, and in all the rest the House of Hillel is lenient, except one in which the House of Hillel retracted to teach according to the words of the House of Shammai.{v1_10_03:2} In this order seven disputes in midrash are found, three of them among the leniencies of the House of Shammai,{v1_10_03:3} and sixteen in fences.{v1_10_03:4} So the number of their sayings in the order Zera'im is sixty-six.⚠2 In the order Mo'ed fifty-two of their halakhot are found, and six of them are among the leniencies of the House of Shammai;{v1_10_03:5} eight disputes in midrash, and among them one of the leniencies of the House of Shammai;{v1_10_03:6} and there are seventeen fences.{v1_10_03:7} So the sum of their sayings in the order Mo'ed is seventy-seven. In the order Nashim forty-four halakhot of theirs are found, and twelve of them are among the leniencies of the House of Shammai;{v1_10_03:8} five disputes in midrash, and among them three of the leniencies of the House of Shammai;{v1_10_03:9} and in fences there are eight, two of them among the leniencies of the House of Shammai.{v1_10_03:10} So the sum of their sayings in the order Nashim is fifty-seven, and in four the House of Hillel retracted to teach according to the House of Shammai. In the order Nezikin there are eleven halakhot, and six of them are among the leniencies of the House of Shammai;{v1_10_04:1} two in midrash;{v1_10_04:2} and in fences there are six, five of which are among the leniencies of the House of Shammai, and in one the House of Hillel retracted and taught according to the House of Shammai.{v1_10_04:3} In the order Kodashim, seven disputes in halakhah, two of them among the leniencies of the House of Shammai;{v1_10_04:4} seven in midrash;{v1_10_04:5} and one in fences, among the leniencies of the House of Shammai;{v1_10_04:6} the sum of all of them is fifteen. In the order Tohorot their disputes in halakhah are sixty-five, and among them seven of the leniencies of the House of Shammai, and in one the House of Hillel retracted to teach according to the House of Shammai;{v1_10_04:7} in fences there are eighteen, and in one the House of Hillel retracted to teach according to the House of Shammai.{v1_10_04:8} Now in this great number of halakhot over which the House of Shammai and the House of Hillel disagreed, only fifty-five places are found in which the House of Shammai went to leniency and the House of Hillel to stringency. Most of the places where the House of Hillel is stringent are in halakhah or in midrash, and few of them in fences and decrees. And mostly, in this small number too, the stringency was a necessary consequence of some opinion or midrash that they held elsewhere. For example, if we find that the House of Hillel forbid a man to dismiss [his wife] with an old bill of divorce (Gittin 8:4), and require a second bill of divorce for one who divorced his wife and lodged with her in an inn (there, mishnah 9), this is only a consequence of its midrash, in which it expounded that a man may divorce his wife even if she burned his dish (there 9:10). Therefore by law one must be concerned, if he lodged together with her after a time when his anger had already subsided, that perhaps his desire for her awoke as in the first days and he had relations with her. For this reason too he may not dismiss his wife with an old bill of divorce, that is, when he was alone with her after the bill of divorce was written (see the Yerushalmi and *Tosafot Yom Tov*). So too his other fences rested on reasoning, if we consider them. In most of the disputes that we have recorded among the stringencies of the House of Hillel, this is not the opinion of all the sages; rather other sages disagree and say that the House of Shammai and the House of Hillel did not disagree on this matter, or that they did not accept the dispute.{v1_10_04:9}

But the investigator would waste his time in vain⚠3 if he sought a particular order and system for each of these two Houses, for these men were simple in their ways; they knew no systematic structures of learning, but went along in innocence in their exposition. Yet this cannot be denied: from the sum of their disputes it emerges for us that each of these two Houses had a particular leaning in its learning. In various matters that we have from the House of Hillel the characteristic is recorded and explicit: in clarifying their opinion they would often add a reason for their words, and sometimes they would also give a reason for the words of their opponents, so that it was said of them (Eruvin 13b) that for this reason the House of Hillel merited that the halakhah be according to them against the House of Shammai, their colleagues.{v1_10_05:1} The House of Hillel very greatly wished not to make the Torah a burden on those who keep it; rather they strove to lighten the load of the commandments for the sake of the good order of the world.{v1_10_05:2} Their footsteps were known in the ways of Hillel's traits, which are the traits of kindness and of setting people's minds at ease, and these traits also shine through their teachings. In many of their halakhot we have seen that they decided the law only according to the measure of kindness and of doing good to people.{v1_10_05:3} Above all, their eye was always on the moral law and the spiritual thought in the statutes of the Torah. This is revealed and seen from their opinion concerning the bond of marriage. They held that this bond is tied only with cords of love and by free will. According to the law of the Torah the father had the right to give his minor or adolescent daughter in betrothal (Kiddushin 2:1); such betrothals were binding, and she had no power to refuse them: against her will she was betrothed and against her will she must keep them, for so they understood the plain sense of Scripture. But for a minor girl who has no father, the sages gave her mother or her brothers the right to marry her off with her consent, or even without her consent (Yevamot 107b); this was a settled halakhah in the days of the House of Shammai and the House of Hillel. This custom was established for her benefit. For in their time and in their land they were accustomed to marry off their daughters while they were minors, and therefore, for the advantage of the minor who has no father, they gave the right to her mother and her brothers. But if she refuses the husband whom her mother and brothers designated for her, she has the right to annul the betrothal. Now the halakhah of refusal (mi'un) was fixed with regard to a betrothed girl, but they were in doubt what her law is with regard to a married girl. Likewise the halakhah was not fixed for the case where the husband died and she had not refused, and she fell before the levir: whether she has the right to refuse the levir. Nor was the halakhah fixed whether this refusal must be in the presence of the husband and in a court, or also not in his presence and not in a court. On all these matters the House of Hillel made their opinion known in a way from which it appears that they strove to remove, as far as possible, compulsion and constraint from matters of marriage. Therefore they made it a law that even if she had been married off in a full marriage and her soul does not desire her husband, she may refuse and annul the marriage; and not only with a husband but also with a levir she may refuse. And lest her heart be faint and she be ashamed to refuse him in his presence, or be ashamed to go to a court, and so unwillingly load upon herself the yoke of compulsion and not refuse, while her heart will not be with her husband all the days of her life, they made it a law that she may refuse even not in his presence and not in a court (Yevamot 13:1). This spirit also governs the law of divorce in the system of the House of Hillel. The House of Shammai did not permit the husband to divorce his wife unless he found in her some unseemly matter [Deuteronomy 24:1], and in no other way does he have the right to divorce her, even if the husband sees, after he has brought her into his home, that she has qualities of soul or body that are not fit to win his love, and he knows in himself that he will never find contentment and domestic peace in her company. Even so he has no right to send her away from his house, but against his will he must say of the joining, "It is good" [Isaiah 41:7], while in his heart "it is bad, it is bad," he will say of it [Proverbs 20:14]. Without doubt they decided thus because they believed in a divine decree spread over every individual person and over each individual one of his deeds; for from of old the saying was held among the people that forty days before the forming of the child it is decreed in heaven who will be his or her mate (Sanhedrin 22a). Therefore the bond of marriage was in their eyes a bond tied in heaven, and it is not fitting that man should untie it unless religion and the Torah require its untying. But this view was not fit to establish the happiness of man in general and the peace of the home in particular, and if it were put into practice it would easily become a cause of the corruption of morals. The House of Hillel did not think so, for they said: the Torah wishes by its statutes to found morality and purity of life, and it would not give a law through which danger and harm to morality could come; the law of divorce would be harmful to morality if it were limited as the House of Shammai limit it, but it would be of great benefit to morality, to man's happiness and to the peace of the home if it gave the husband the right to divorce his wife for anything he found in her that is not according to his soul and his will, and that would become a cause of hatred instead of his loving her. The trait of kindness they kept even where the law compelled them to decide a judgment to punish a person, and in every possible way they lessened the measure of his punishment.{v1_10_06:1} Now, since these foundations were their foundations, it is self-evident that they did not readily agree to decree decrees, and if they found even a small reason to refrain from decreeing a decree, they refrained and had no misgivings;{v1_10_06:2} and they went one step further, in that at times they permitted evading a prohibition by means of a stratagem (Pesaḥim 36a, and the Tosafot there). In all these characteristics the way of the House of Hillel was marked, and the House of Shammai the reverse.

In a great number of their disputes on matters of halakhah they made it their aim to clarify old halakhot: either to explain their meaning and reason,{v1_10_06:3} or to define their conditions,{v1_10_06:4} or to extend them;{v1_10_06:5} and if we consider their sayings we will find that most of their disputes on halakhah fall into these kinds. Their way of clarifying and explaining the old halakhot was the same as the way that each side chose for itself in clarifying Scripture. The House of Shammai did not depart from the meaning of the words as written in interpreting the Torah, while the House of Hillel went deep into the intent of the verse and decided by weighing with their judgment and by reasoning.{v1_10_06:6} Their interpretations in understanding the halakhot received from their predecessors are also marked by this characteristic. The House of Shammai held every received halakhah to be like planted nails [Ecclesiastes 12:11], from which one may in no way move even when reasoning requires a deviation; and therefore it is self-evident that this way caused the House of Shammai not to cease giving precept upon precept [Isaiah 28:10] and decreeing decree upon decree, and therefore the House of Shammai were always among the stringent. But the House of Hillel refrained⚠4 wherever possible, on the basis of reasoning and weighing of judgment, so as to distinguish between cases, and for this reason they could for the most part lean to the side of leniency.

The length of time that the two Houses continued we cannot define precisely, for we do not know when Shammai's house of study was founded, or when he became head of the court (av beit din). But this is clear: the division of the Houses did not continue beyond the time of Rabban Yoḥanan ben Zakkai. And if after the destruction we still find some traces of the existence of sages of the House of Shammai, this is no sign that the Houses continued in force one against the other; for even if the association was dissolved, it does not follow that in one moment all its parts would dissolve and change their opinion. It is true that individual men lived even after the destruction who did not depart from their former opinion, but on that account we cannot call them by the name of a House. Therefore the later sages called the House of Shammai by the title "the first generations," as against R. Dosa ben Harkinas, whom they called by the title "the last generations" (Gittin 81a), although this R. Dosa lived in the time of the Temple and after it (Yevamot 16a). It is true that there are a very few of the disputes between the House of Shammai and the House of Hillel that appear from their content to be children of the time after the destruction; but from this there is no proof that the Houses existed after the destruction. For if we look at the foundations of those halakhot, we will find that nothing compels us to attach the dispute only to the time when the Temple was gone; rather one can find a place for those disputes also while the Temple stood, and the later sages after the destruction attached the disputes to examples fitting their own time. Of this kind we find two disputes. The first: one who had produce in this time, and the time of removal arrived: the House of Shammai say he must redeem it for money, and the House of Hillel say, it is all one whether it is money or produce (Ma'aser Sheni 5:7). The core of this dispute is from the time of the Temple. For at various times they could not bring up the second tithe to Jerusalem because of the disorder of the roads and the wars, and the sages of the Houses disagreed whether this falls under "the place is too far" [Deuteronomy 14:24] (see *Tosafot Yom Tov*); and the later sages who received this halakhah phrased it as fitted their own time.{v1_10_07:1} So we find in several of their disputes that the later sages reported things from them in a way that seems to us as though the House of Shammai and the House of Hillel said so; but it is not so: rather the formulations were formulated by the later sages, who put them thus in the mouth of the House of Shammai and the House of Hillel.{v1_10_07:2} The second dispute: the House of Shammai say: one who says, "I take upon myself a marḥeshet" — it shall be left until Elijah comes; and the House of Hillel say: there was a vessel in the Temple and its name was marḥeshet (Menaḥot 63a). From the outer form of this dispute one may judge, and it has already been judged (*Darkhei ha-Mishnah* p. 54), that it is from the time after the destruction of the Temple. But this dispute too, by its content, is possible in the time of the Temple. Someone vowed while the Temple stood, in the words "I take upon myself a marḥeshet," and asks how he is to fulfil this vow: surely one who is in doubt about the meaning of the word marḥeshet, and does not know whether marḥeshet is named after its making, so that the vessel is called marḥeshet because a meal-offering called marḥeshet is baked in it, or whether marḥeshet is the name of the vessel itself, cannot give his questioner a clear answer, but must leave the matter in doubt. The House of Shammai, who were in doubt, were therefore forced to say: it shall be left until Elijah comes; but the House of Hillel, for whom it was clear that marḥeshet is the name of the vessel, therefore oblige the one who vowed to bring such a vessel to the Temple. Now, what is it that compels us to say that this dispute is from the time after the destruction? Nothing but the outer form of the saying of the House of Hillel, who said: "there was a vessel in the Temple." But who will tell us that this is not just a formulation that was formulated at a late time? Surely it is so, for anyone versed in the language of the early Tannaim will understand that the formulation of this dispute, as it was formulated in our Babylonian Talmud, is not original, and bears on its forehead a stamp that was stamped in the late period. The truth is that these disputes were transmitted by one of the disciples of the House of Shammai who still lived after the destruction, or that the sayings here called in the name of the House of Shammai were said by one of his disciples after the destruction, and they called them by the name of the House of Shammai. Therefore in no case can one bring a proof from this for the existence of the Houses; and in particular it is hard to picture the matter of the two Houses from the death of Shammai and Hillel onward. The House of Hillel remained in existence, for from the seed of Hillel came the Patriarchs and the heads of the Sanhedrin and of learning. But what became of the House of Shammai after the death of its head? Had there been a separate house of study, someone must have stood at its head, and how is it that it is not mentioned who the head was? This compels us to think that they had no separate house of study; rather, after the death of Shammai a great number of his disciples remained alive for a long time, and they are called by the name House of Shammai. In some halakhot we can recognize from their content which of them are from the time of Shammai and Hillel. Every dispute in which, as we know, the opinion of Shammai himself leans to the opinion of the House of Hillel, or is neither like these nor like those, but he had a separate opinion — such a dispute is from a late time. For example, in their dispute about impure leaven that leavened the dough, what its measure is (Orlah 2:4), where one of Shammai's disciples testified that he heard from his master the measure of the House of Hillel (ibid. 2:5): this dispute is from a late time. And perhaps also in other places (Eduyot 1:7, 8, 10, 11) where the House of Shammai and the House of Hillel disagree and Shammai had another opinion, the House of Shammai departed from the opinion of their master without knowing it, and only one of the disciples knew it and made it known to his colleagues. And we find a dispute between the House of Shammai and the House of Hillel in which Hillel spoke neither like these nor like those, but it is said: "Hillel for his part used to say such and such," that is, he remained alone in his opinion (Ma'aserot 4:2 and Tosefta ch. 3). In this it is explicitly hinted that it was so in his time, and that his colleagues and disciples did not agree with him. In their dispute whether one lays hands on a voluntary burnt-offering on a festival (Beitzah 2:4 and parallels), it is clear that they disagreed so in the days of Hillel (Yerushalmi Ḥagigah ch. 2 and Tosefta ch. 2). In one dispute in which the House of Hillel retracted to rule like the House of Shammai, it appears that this was in the time of Shammai and Hillel. It is this (Yevamot 115b): the House of Shammai say she may remarry on her own testimony and collect her ketubbah; and the House of Hillel say she may remarry but may not collect her ketubbah. Why did they say she may not collect her ketubbah? Because they did not expound the wording of the ketubbah, in which it is written, "When you are married to another, you will take what is written for you." And why did they not expound it? Because Hillel their master too did not expound it. But Hillel changed his mind and expounded it. Therefore it appears that as long as Hillel did not expound it, his disciples too did not expound it, and there was a dispute between the House of Shammai and the House of Hillel; and when Hillel changed his mind and expounded it, his disciples too expounded the wording of the ketubbah and retracted to rule like the words of the House of Shammai (see Yerushalmi Ketubbot ch. 4 and Tosefta ch. 4, and Yevamot (116a)⚠5 with the Tosafot). Likewise their dispute whether one may vow an oath to murderers, to confiscators and to tax-collectors, where the House of Shammai say one may not swear and the House of Hillel say one may swear (Nedarim ch. 3), was also in the time of Shammai and Hillel, because of an event that happened: Herod, seeing that the heart of the people was not faithful to him, issued a decree that every single man must enter into a sworn oath to be loyal to their king, and those who refused to swear he punished with a fine of money or in their bodies. Shammai and Hillel and the sect of the Essenes he did not make swear, for he trusted in the character of their spirit, that they were lovers of peace (*Antiquities* 15, ch. 10). About six thousand refused to swear, and he punished them with money (17, ch. 2). Without doubt, because of this event they were roused to this question and disagreed in their opinions. Those who refused to swear were of the House of Shammai, and they said that one may not swear. Where the elders of the House of Shammai and the elders of the House of Hillel are mentioned, this does not refer to Shammai and Hillel themselves; rather the distinguished men of the Houses were called elders (Sukkah 2:6⚠6; Tosefta Rosh ha-Shanah ch. 2; Menaḥot 41a; *Darkhei ha-Mishnah* p. 55). Among the multitude of their disputes we find no more than eight in civil law and no more than four in the laws of sacrifices.{v1_10_08:1} The reason is that the performance of the sacrifices was entrusted to the priests, and they knew the details of the procedure, it being a thing practised every day; and the disputes on the laws of sacrifices among the later sages came about because after the destruction the sacrificial service ceased and the laws were forgotten. Civil law was entrusted to the courts, and the sages of exposition left it to the judges who were appointed to establish justice. For this reason we do not find that they disagreed on the laws of the Sanhedrin, nor on punishments for transgressions. In particular it appears that most of their disputes are on matters that concern the individual Jew and on laws that are in the hands of every person. Nor is there any dispute between them on the laws of the Megillah and its reading, nor on the laws of fasts, of the sotah and of skin-afflictions (*Darkhei ha-Mishnah* p. 55).

Even though they were divided in their opinions, their hearts were not divided, but they behaved toward one another in brotherhood. "They loved truth and peace" [Zechariah 8:19] (Yevamot 14b), and the House of Hillel went one step further, in that they were gentle and forbearing (Eruvin 13b). But the events of the time, which turned to evil for Israel, worked for evil on the peace of the Houses as well. All the days of Herod and his sons were evil for the people, but the measure of evil was not filled until Rome cut off the kingdom of Judah root and all, joined its land to the Roman empire and made it one of its provinces, and put all its affairs under the hand of the Roman governor. He laid taxes upon it and raised its levy; capital cases were under his hand, and it was in his hand to review the judgment of the Sanhedrin. New arrangements such as these were the cause of a new upheaval; then the sect of the Zealots (Zeloten) arose. This sect was fiercely zealous against the Romans; the sign it bore on its banner was: "The kingship belongs to the Lord alone, and Israel has no king besides Him"; "Woe to the man who sets a mortal as lord over himself and bends his shoulder to bear the yoke of flesh and blood" (*Antiquities* book 17;⚠7 see Graetz). The founders of the sect were Judah the Galilean and Zadok. This sect grew stronger day by day and did its deeds in secret and in the open. It is not our task to tell the deeds of its might and its valor, but it is fitting to consider its relation to the Houses. The House of Hillel too, although they were gentle and forbearing, nevertheless took ill the conduct of the Romans and their decrees. For how could their heart be at rest in the face of the oppression with which they oppressed Israel, robbing and plundering all their labor, laying on them a poll tax and a levy on the produce of the land, on their houses and on all their property? It was the reverse. Because of these decrees the sages made new statutes to disgrace and make abhorrent any man who lent his hand to the Romans to be among their helpers in raising the taxes, the levies and the customs. They said of the tax collectors and the customs collectors that their repentance is hard (Bava Kamma 94b), and in their eyes they were like robbers and plunderers (Sanhedrin 24b); and they decreed that one does not change money from the customs collectors' chest or from the tax collectors' purse, and one does not take charity from them (Bava Kamma 113a). We have found no one who opposed these statutes, and without doubt the House of Shammai and the House of Hillel alike were of one heart in this matter. But it lay in the character of the House of Hillel, since they were gentle and forbearing, that they submitted under the yoke of their burden, for they knew and understood that there was no strength to stand against the Romans; therefore they were silent before the Lord and waited patiently for Him [Psalms 37:7]. Not so the House of Shammai: they were open opponents of the government, and without doubt their hand was with the Zealots. This thing caused the division between the Houses to widen, until even their disputes over laws were conducted with bitterness of soul. Such a dispute flared up when they assembled together in the house of Hananiah ben Hezekiah ben Garon (Shabbat 1:4). At that assembly they disputed eighteen matters; in eighteen the House of Shammai outnumbered the House of Hillel, and on eighteen they agreed and decreed. On that occasion they stood against one another in great wrath, and the disciples of the House of Shammai laid hands on the disciples of the House of Hillel. And it was said of that day that it was as hard for Israel as the day on which the calf was made. The sages of the following generations were not of one mind about the benefit of the many fences and decrees made on that day. One of the sages (R. Eliezer ben Hyrcanus) praised their deed, saying that on that day they heaped up the measure and multiplied fences in heaping measure, and did well. But another sage (R. Joshua) condemned the deed and said that on that day they leveled the measure, making fence upon fence and precept upon precept [Isaiah 28:10] until it grew too heavy to bear; and so, in multiplying decrees, they multiplied stumbling (Yerushalmi and Tosefta there, and Bavli 153b). Already the ancients did not know what the matters were that they decreed, on which they prevailed and on which they disputed. The time of this event was in the last generation before the destruction, at a time when they no longer behaved toward one another in peace and brotherhood as at first. For at the beginning of the founding of the Houses they behaved toward one another in peace and fellowship, and after a time they parted in their opinions also with respect to their position toward the government: the House of Shammai were opponents of the government and their hand was with the Zealots, while the House of Hillel kept away from rebellion and submitted to the Romans. The difference of opinion on this bred enmity between them, and this enmity spread also to their disputes over laws; and so we understand the reason why the sages of the Houses rose up against one another in burning anger at that assembly. We can also know approximately the time of the assembly: it was not long after the death of Agrippa I, when the Zealots grew strong in uniting with great force against the Romans. And even if we say that this assembly was not in the days of Hananiah ben Hezekiah but in the days of his son Eleazar, who was one of the heads of the Zealots (Graetz, vol. 3, note 26), it is nonetheless certain that from the time the House of Shammai was drawn after the Zealots and their hand was firmly with them, from then too the peace between them was broken and turned into enmity and strife all the time.{v1_10_10:1} To know the relation of the Houses to one another we were compelled to go some steps forward in the course of time. Now let us return and pursue the knowledge of the particular events that happened from the death of Hillel onward.
