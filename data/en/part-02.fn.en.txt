v1_02_15:1) In the Gemara, Bava Batra (14b), the scroll of Ruth is ascribed to the prophet Samuel. Without doubt they were moved to hold this opinion by seeing that the whole purpose of the book was to trace the lineage of David to the tribe of Judah, which was destined to be the one from which the rulers in Israel would come forth; and since Samuel sought to found the kingship in the house of David, he saw fit to set forth his lineage first, to show that the kingship befits him by his descent. But to this argument there is an answer. First, we do not find that it was made public in Samuel's lifetime that he had anointed David king; for he did all this in secret. A second thing is proved from the phrase "And it came to pass in the days when the judges judged" [Ruth 1:1]: that between the writer and the Judges a long time lies. It is more fitting to ascribe the composition of this scroll to the time of the division of the kingdom, that is, the time of Rehoboam, when the author wished to show the lineage of the house of David. Yet this too is forced, for then there would be only five generations between the time of the writer and the time of the event; and from his saying there, "Now this was the custom in former time in Israel concerning redeeming and concerning exchanging" [Ruth 4:7], it appears that there was a great distance between the time of the writer and the time of the event. This too is a proof against the view of the Gemara that Samuel wrote it. Some place it as late as the time of the exile (see the commentary of the Christian scholar Bertheau on Judges and Ruth, p. 237), and his proofs are from the character of the language, in which Aramaic traces are already found, such as timtse'in, tidbakin, teda'in, ta'asin, kayyem, Mara; and Shaddai is found in place of El Shaddai, and the whole phrase has a parallel in the book of Job: "and Shaddai has dealt very bitterly with me." We also find that this composition has a reflective character: it is not enough for it to tell only the events that happened before it,⚠18 but it adds to them also its reflection on them, as when it explains the ancient custom and its difference from the custom of its own time concerning the manner of acquisition. Of this kind too is what it adds at the end of the composition, the lineage of David; and this custom was not found among the writers of chronicles of ancient times. But for all this we have a clear proof that the scroll was written at an early time, from what is said there: "to raise up the name of the dead upon his inheritance." By the word "his inheritance" the speaker without doubt intended the purpose of the statute under discussion, which is that an inheritance not pass from tribe to tribe; and certainly at the time of its writing they still guarded the permanence of the inheritance according to the division of the land among the tribes. And it is a wonder that in Deuteronomy nothing is mentioned of raising up the name of the dead upon the inheritance.

v1_02_16:1) See Yerushalmi Kiddushin 1:1 and Midrash Ruth: "At first they used to acquire by drawing off the shoe; they changed to acquire by kezatsah; they changed to acquire by money, by deed and by possession." It is explicit that this mode of acquisition ceased at some time, and then it returned to its old form, to acquire by a kerchief, which is like drawing off the shoe (Bava Metzi'a 47a). And it had already become unknown how this acquisition worked — who gave to whom? (Yerushalmi, and Bavli ibid.). This is a sign that the custom had lapsed for a long time.

v1_02_18:1) I have seen fit to remark here on the view of the one who says (Makkot 11a) that Joshua wrote the section of the cities of refuge in the Torah, which the Gemara strained to explain. According to our way we say that his intention is that this commandment was among the things that Joshua received from Moses orally, and when the time came to fulfil it, that is, after the conquest and the settlement, he wrote them in the Torah; and he learned this from the language of the verse in Joshua 20:2: "Speak to the children of Israel, saying: Appoint for yourselves the cities of refuge, of which I spoke to you by the hand of Moses"; from his saying "of which I spoke" he understood that the speech was oral. And if this is his intention, we learn that Joshua was the first who wrote down oral matters.

v1_02_19:1) The Masoretic reading in Samuel is: once "al ha-dam" [upon the blood] and once "el ha-dam" [to the blood], and both are one in intent. But according to the Septuagint translation, it is rendered in the Torah as if it were written "al he-harim" [upon the mountains], and in Samuel they translated as if it were written "et ha-dam" [the blood]. But we do not know the origin of this reading. For in saying "and they slaughtered on the ground" [1 Samuel 14:32], he indicates that they did not receive the blood in order to eat it, but that it was spilled on the ground. Therefore, if we emend to "et ha-dam," the word "on the ground" has no explanation; for he wanted to tell us on purpose that they slaughtered on the ground, and in this matter, that is, in slaughtering on the ground joined to eating the slaughtered animal in that place, lies the sin.⚠19 Whereas if they had eaten the blood, the slaughtering on the ground would neither add nor take away. Therefore the correct reading in Samuel is as we have it. And now it is self-evident that in the Torah too the true reading is that of the Masoretic text. For the words "Behold, the people are sinning by eating upon the blood" must refer to some statute, and it is no other statute than the one in the Torah; whereas if the reading were "upon the mountains," we would not know what the sin is in eating upon the blood.

v1_02_20:1) According to one of the sages of the Talmud, the Torah was given scroll by scroll (Gittin 60a); and from then on, when all the scrolls were put together,⚠20 they were called in Deuteronomy "the book of the Torah" (Deuteronomy 31). Those who say that "the book of the Torah" refers to Deuteronomy, or to particular parts that were written, and not to the whole Torah, must bring proof. It is not our purpose to argue about this. And see Bavli Makkot (11a), where R. Judah and R. Nehemiah disagree on what Joshua wrote in the book of the Torah of God: one says the eight verses, and the other says the cities of refuge. Now the one who says the eight verses clearly expounds against the plain sense of the words, for how would he explain "these words"? And according to the one who disputes him, the section of the cities of refuge was written by Joshua at the end of the days of conquest and settlement. The Talmud's resolution there is also against the sense of the verse. In any case, the argument of R. Meir in Sifre Deuteronomy, section 357 (or according to the Gemara, Bava Batra 15b and Zevaḥim 30a,⚠21 R. Simeon), that it is possible that Moses gave the Torah lacking one letter, is no argument; for according to other sages we see that it is indeed possible. This needs further study.

v1_02_20:2) See the introductions to Holy Scripture by Christian scholars, who brought proofs for this. Likewise the traveler Strabo relates (book 14, p. 734) that several nations he reached in his travels had the custom of depositing their holy writings in their temples. Likewise Eusebius relates (book 1, ch. 9) that a priest of Tyre wrote a chronicle of his people and his land, and drew his narratives from writings that were kept in their holy temple. See further what the scholar K. O. Müller⚠22 wrote on the writings of Sparta in his book (*Die Dorier*, vol. 2, p. 90), and likewise the historian of the Romans, Niebuhr (vol. 1, p. 526).

v1_02_20:3) An example of the name "devir" in this sense is found elsewhere: "and the name of Debir before was Kiriath-sepher" (Joshua 15:15), which was no doubt so called because their holy writings were deposited there. See the *Book of Roots* by Dr. Julius Fürst, s.v. devir. And the Aramaic Targum renders Kiriath-sepher as "arkhei" [archives], whose meaning is a place where writings are kept; and in the Gemara (Avodah Zarah 24b) [it is said] that in Persian devir is a name for a book. Gesenius cites this, and a Christian commentator, one Keniaum,⚠23 copied it in his commentary on the book of Judges (1:17),⚠24 and said there that it is impossible to explain an old Hebrew word from the Persian language; and in this I agree with him. But I do not agree that he understood the intent of the Talmud, and without doubt he never saw it. For these are the words of the Talmud: "From where do the Persians call a book devir? From here: 'and the name of Debir before was Kiriath-sepher.'" Here we see that it did not occur to it to derive the Hebrew from the Persian, but on the contrary, that the Persians called a book devir after the Hebrew. And Kiriath-sepher is also called Kiriath-sannah (Joshua 15:49), which is also from the sense of learning, and its parallel is shinnen in the intensive conjugation (Fürst, s.v. shanan).
