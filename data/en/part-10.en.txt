Chapter Ten

Simeon the Just, one of the last of the Men of the Great Assembly.

Reports of particulars of the Oral Torah that are children of the first period have not reached us in writing; and what has been told us about them is the utterance of later writers, and we do not know which of their reports rests on tradition and which comes from conjecture. And we should not wonder at the lack of clear early reports on matters of the Torah: even of the sources of the nation's history in general from ancient days only poor and scanty remnants have reached us. And had not one of our honored priests at the end of the days of the Temple, namely Josephus Flavius, called Josephus the Priest, left us in writing in a book precious remnants of the history of our people, of our antiquities and of the wars of the Lord that were fought against the enemies of Israel, then the history of the whole period of the Second Temple would have been forgotten and every memory of it lost.{v1_05_12:1} Yet of the times after their return from the exile, while they were still under the rule of the last Persian kings, even he could not report their history. And even the one incident that he tells (*Antiquities*, book 12, chapter 6)⚠1 of what happened in the days of Artaxerxes II (about the year of the world 3628), of the quarrel between two brothers who were priests, is only a small thing for the history of the nation. But for the work before us it is right for us nonetheless to mention it here, as it gives a little light on the religious situation at that time. Jehoiada son of Eliashib died, and his son Joḥanan served as high priest in his place, in the reign of the Persian king Artaxerxes II (about the year of the world 3628). The king's general was Bagoas. He was governor of the province of Syria and Tyre, and the Land of Israel too was under his command. This governor had a friend named Jeshua, the brother of Joḥanan the high priest. Jeshua envied his brother, and his eye and his heart were always on the high priesthood. This longing of his was strengthened by the governor's love for him and by the promise the governor had made him to take the high priesthood away from his brother Joḥanan and to make him priest in his place. This promise emboldened Jeshua, until he began to stir up a quarrel with his brother and went up to Jerusalem to carry out his plot. And it came to pass one day, when both of them were in the Temple and quarreled with one another, that Joḥanan rose up in his anger and killed his brother in the Temple of the Lord. When Bagoas heard of the abomination that had been done in Israel and in the Sanctuary of the Lord, he hastened to go up to Jerusalem to investigate what had been done. When he came there he wished to enter the Sanctuary of the Lord, but the priests wanted to prevent him, for it is a statute in Israel that no foreigner may enter the Temple. Then his anger was kindled against them greatly, and he said: "Is this one, who murdered a human being in the Temple, purer than I?" And he entered by force. Then he laid a penalty on the Temple, that they should pay fifty drachmas for every lamb offered on the altar of the Lord. But Joḥanan was not removed from his priesthood and remained in his office. Behold, from this incident we see clearly that the hand of the statute of the Torah was not strong over the high priests; or else that the halakhah that a murderer, even when standing at the altar, is taken to be put to death and does not complete the service (Sanhedrin 35a) was not founded until after that time, for some reason unknown to us. For had there been such a statute at that time, why did they leave Joḥanan in his priesthood? And here we cannot say that they were forced to it by order of the king and the governor; on the contrary, the governor opposed him and was angry with him, and would have said nothing had the court wished to remove him.{v1_05_13:1} Apart from this story nothing particular has reached us from that time that touches its religious situation especially.{v1_05_13:2} Also of the names of the men of distinction, the members of the Great Assembly, none is known to us except one, namely Simeon the Just, of whom it is said that he was one of the last of the Great Assembly (Avot 1:2). Although we have no reason to cast doubt on the fact that the beginning of this Assembly was immediately after their return from the exile, and that Ezra was one of its men and without doubt its head, as is hinted in the record in the book called by his name, nevertheless we cannot determine clearly and precisely the length of time for which it lasted, because this too is not clearly known to us: who was this Simeon who was one of the last of the Men of the Great Assembly, and at what time did he live and hold his priesthood? According to what comes out for us from the account of the Talmud, Simeon the Just lived in the days of the great conquest in which Alexander conquered all the lands of the Persian kingdom, and among them also the whole land of Syria. For so it is told in the Talmud (*Megillat Ta'anit* 9; Yoma 69a): when Alexander conquered the kingdom of Persia and subdued under his feet all the lands that had been under its rule, he also went up to Jerusalem. The Jews, whose spirit had been faithful to the kings of Persia, feared Alexander's wrath. And as he drew near to the city, they sent messengers to meet him, of the notables of the people and of the priests, and at their head was Simeon the Just, the high priest, clothed in the garments of his priesthood. And when they met Alexander, he got down from his chariot, gave honor to the high priest, and bowed down to him. His officers wondered at this. And the king told them that he did this because the appearance of this old priest awakened in him the memory of a wondrous dream that he had dreamed when he set out for war: in his dream, behold, the likeness of this honored old man was walking at his right hand, leading him to victory and giving him deliverance in all his wars. For this reason the priest found grace and favor before him, and he promised him that he would do no harm to his people but would show it great kindness. And Alexander also fulfilled his promise. When he came to Jerusalem he offered sacrifices in the Temple. And he agreed to give the Jews license to judge their cases according to the statutes of their Torah, and gave them freedom of belief and religion. He granted them a remission of the tax in every seventh year, which is the year of release; and to the words of accusation with which the Kutim [Samaritans] slandered the Jews he paid no attention (Josephus, *Antiquities*, book 12, chapter 2).⚠2 The king asked him to set up the likeness of his image in the Sanctuary, but the priest answered him that this was against the religion of the Jews. But he promised him that he would make him an everlasting memorial among the Jewish people: every male child born to the priests in that year would be called by his name, Alexander. Now this is told in the Talmud of Simeon the Just. But Josephus the Priest tells it so of Jaddua the high priest. This priest Jaddua died not long after the death of Alexander (about the year of the world 3660). After him Onias his son served as priest, and when he too died his son came in his place, and that was Simeon the Just (Josephus, ibid.). And according to this view Simeon the Just ruled in the days of the turmoil and the wars between Alexander's generals who inherited his kingdom after his death. At that time Ptolemy Lagus besieged Jerusalem and took it, and carried off a great captivity from Israel and led them to Egypt. But also at a time later than this there was a high priest who was likewise called Simeon, and to him too the men of his generation gave the surname "the Just"; he was the son of Onias son of the first Simeon. Simeon the Just the Second held his priesthood in the days of Antiochus the Great (about the year of the world 3720). Some students of antiquity think that this Simeon, who was the grandson of the first Simeon, was the Simeon the Just of whom it is said that he was one of the last of the Men of the Great Assembly.{v1_05_13:3} Now we have no clear proof in hand to decide between the views on this matter, and a decision on it is not needed for our purpose. But it is not right for us to turn our eyes away from the state of affairs of the Jews with regard to the government in the time of Alexander, and likewise in the time of Antiochus the Great. From all that has been made known to us in this respect of the time of Alexander, we have seen that from the time the kingdom of Persia came into Alexander's hand their political situation did not grow worse. He promised them not to change anything in their political and religious affairs and to leave them to their customs and their laws, and he also fulfilled his promise; therefore we have seen no compelling reason for the abolition of the Great Assembly at that time. And likewise there is no reason for its abolition in the days of Antiochus the Great, who was a gracious king to Israel. For although in his wars, which he fought with Ptolemy Philopator, the cup of hardships passed over Israel too, these hardships had no lasting effect; for when Antiochus prevailed, he did very well by the Jews and gave them all the privileges their hearts asked. And in the letter he wrote to his friend and general, the existence of "the great council" is mentioned explicitly, and he wrote to him that he grants them freedom to live according to the law of their religion, and that the members of the great council shall be exempt from the poll tax. So how could we say that at that time the Great Assembly was abolished? But it is not so when Ptolemy Lagus came to Jerusalem: then the matter was reversed. He laid the rod of his wickedness on the lot of the Jews and oppressed them with great oppression; therefore without doubt turmoil came in his wake in their internal affairs as well, and this is a great reason for the abolition of the Assembly. And for this reason we may rightly suppose that at that time the Great Assembly came to an end; and since we have seen that in the days of that turmoil Simeon the Just the First was high priest, therefore he too is the Simeon the Just mentioned in the Mishnah, who was one of the last of the Great Assembly.

Simeon the Just is praised with many praises. He was a man who walked whole-heartedly with God, and in love and kindness with men (Josephus, ibid.). And as he himself cleaved to the Torah of the Lord, served his God with a whole heart and did kindness to men, so he taught his people and instructed them to walk in this way to which his foot held fast. The preciousness of these foundations and the greatness of their worth he described in a short saying, after the manner of the ancients, when he said: "On three things the world stands: on the Torah, on the service, and on deeds of lovingkindness" (Avot ch. 1). This saying, by its inner content, bears the stamp of the longing of his spirit and of the needs of his time; and by its form one already finds in it the pattern of the sayings ascribed to the Scribes, in that they are counted out item by item. He very strongly condemned outward service in the performance of the commandments, and praised those who perform them if they do them with attention and with a pure mind. For this very reason he took no pleasure in those who vowed naziriteship to mortify themselves. Therefore all his days he refrained from eating the offering of a nazirite, for he said that whoever lays a prohibition on himself by naziriteship is counted a sinner. He was the man who laid the cornerstone of the rule that those who came after him stated at a later time, that whoever sits in a fast is called a sinner (Nedarim 10a). Once a man came to him from the South, with beautiful eyes and of good appearance, his locks curled, and he was a nazirite. Simeon the Just said to him: "What did you see, my son, to destroy this beautiful hair?" He told him: "I was a shepherd in my town, and I went to draw water from the spring, and I saw my reflection in the water, and my inclination rose wantonly against me and sought to drive me out of the world. I said to it: Wicked one, you are swelling with pride over something that is not yours! It is upon me to consecrate you to Heaven." Simeon bowed his head to him and said to him: "My son, may those who do the will of the Place multiply in Israel like you! Of you the verse says: 'a nazirite, to separate himself to the Lord' [Numbers 6:2]" (Nedarim and Nazir ch. 1, Bavli and Yerushalmi, and Sifre Naso, and in many places). We see from this with how much insight Simeon went deeply into the words of the Torah. For when the Torah said "a nazirite, to separate himself to the Lord," it does not mean that if a man mortifies himself for the honor of Heaven the Lord has pleasure in it; rather, the meaning is that the naziriteship by which a man separates himself is truly "to the Lord" when the one who vows the naziriteship has a moral reason for it. The form of this aggadah bears on its face the stamp of its antiquity, and perhaps the words that the narrator put in Simeon's mouth were his very words; then it would appear from this that already in his days they were accustomed to use the word "Heaven" to name God who dwells in heaven, and likewise the name "Place" for the name of the Lord. There is no doubt that these two names are not native to the Hebrews. In particular the name "Heaven" for the name of the Lord is a metaphor taken from the Greeks, who call heaven and the God of heaven by one name. And their fear of pronouncing the Name in its letters was the reason why they sought for themselves various names by which to call the Name.

From the effort that Simeon made to hold the people back from naziriteship we may rightly judge that at that time they were very unrestrained in vows and naziriteships, as we shall see in the generations after him, in the days of the war of the Maccabees, when there were people who had vowed naziriteship and came to Jerusalem to complete the days of their naziriteship (1 Maccabees 3:49). This licence, in which a man loads mortifications on himself by vows and naziriteship, is for the most part a sign of the hardships of the time and of the troubles breaking out. For from of old men thought that by laying a prohibition on themselves and afflicting themselves with mortifications and self-denial they would obtain favor from God, that He would have mercy on them and save them from their troubles. This thought took root in them because they saw that all religions and faiths in general, and the divine religion in particular, lay prohibitions on a man's soul and command statutes whose performance depends on things from which they impose restraint. And because they saw that, according to the promise of the giver of the religion, keeping these statutes and restraint from forbidden things are the fitting ways to obtain God's grace and favor, they thought that the more they forbade themselves even things permitted to them by religion, the more they would find grace in God's eyes and obtain His favor, so that for this He would be a help to them in trouble. Therefore, as the hardships multiplied from then on, so those who vowed vows and naziriteships multiplied. Now we have seen that Simeon the Just had no liking for these, and opposed those who vowed naziriteships and vows, who were rife in his days. So it is not wondrous nor far-fetched if we suppose that in that time they enacted that the sage would have authority to release vows. For when they saw that the people were unrestrained in vows, they enacted that the sage has power to release the vow upon the regret of the one who vowed, saying that it was an error. And although the release of vows has no clear and explicit proof from the Torah, there is surely a hint of the matter: the Torah gave power to the father to annul his daughter's vows and to the husband to annul his wife's vows; therefore they decided by reasoning that likewise the sage would have power to release the vows of the people, who are led by the sages of the generations. By force of this reasoning they enacted the ordinance of the release of vows. The early masters of the Mishnah already knew that this ordinance was new, only the forgetting that came in the wake of the hardships caused the time of the enactment to be hidden from them; therefore they said (Ḥagigah, end of ch. 1): "The release of vows floats in the air and has nothing to rest on." But it seems that there were among the sages of the generations some who, although they strove to hold back vows, did not think it right to agree that one who vowed a vow should go to the sage to release it. One of the sages who spoke against the bad custom of those who vow vows was the author of the book of Ecclesiastes. We already know that this book was composed at the end of the days of the Persian kings (above, ch. 1, in the note), and he says explicitly that a man ought to guard the door of his lips from vowing a vow. Of vows of consecration and the like he says: "Better is it that you should not vow, than that you should vow and not pay" (Ecclesiastes 5:4). But of the vows that men vow to lay a prohibition on themselves and to mortify themselves, of these he warns that one should not vow at all, and that a man should not "suffer his mouth to cause his flesh to sin" [Ecclesiastes 5:5], to mortify and afflict himself,{v1_05_15:1} and should not be forced to annul the words of his mouth and to go before "the messenger" [Ecclesiastes 5:5], that is, the sage who teaches, who bears this name also in the later prophets (Haggai 1:13; Malachi 2:6⚠3), and to say that what he vowed was an error, because he vowed in his haste and now regrets it and wants the sage to annul his vow. We see from this that when the book of Ecclesiastes was composed, the ordinance of the release of vows was not yet a settled and accepted ordinance; therefore our supposition is very likely, that in the days of Simeon the Just they made it a fixed ordinance, because they saw that the hour required it.

One of the exalted virtues for which those who told his history praised him is the virtue of prophecy, for they said of him that the holy spirit spoke in him. In the last year of his priesthood,{v1_05_16:1} when he came out of the Holy of Holies on the Day of Atonement, he prophesied that he would die in that year. And so it was: seven days after the festival of Sukkot the righteous man went to his eternal home and was buried at a good old age. They mourned very greatly over his death, for he left no righteous man among the living who equaled him; the priests lamented that a loss had been lost that could not be replaced. For this very reason his brothers the priests refrained from then on from blessing with the Explicit Name (Yoma 39b, and Yerushalmi there, ch. 6). It has not become clear to us what the particular reason for this refraining was, but this much comes out explicitly from the content of the story: because they saw that faith and the fear of Heaven had diminished, they thought that neither they nor their generation were worthy to pronounce the Name explicitly, even in the Priestly Blessing, and they refrained from pronouncing it although they had been specially commanded about it. For to pronounce the Name in its letters as it is written — for the rest of the people, or also for the priests outside the Priestly Blessing — they already refrained in the time of the first Scribes, and this too was among their ordinances. Just as they ordained various substitute names in order to give honor to the name of the Lord, sometimes by a change in the reading and sometimes also in writing, so they ordained a substitute for the Explicit Name of the Lord, so that it should not be read as it is written. But in the blessing with which the priests were commanded to bless Israel, they pronounced the Name; and already at that time they understood the Torah's words "Thus shall you bless the children of Israel" [Numbers 6:23] to mean: "thus," with precision, the words as they are written, and therefore they must bless with the Name as it is written (Sifre, portion Naso, piska 25⚠4). And it is said explicitly: "And they shall put My name upon the children of Israel" [Numbers 6:27] (Sotah 38a). It is clear that these verses were already expounded in this way by the ancient Scribes, since we see that until the death of Simeon the Just they blessed with the Explicit Name, although already from then on they refrained from pronouncing the Name as it is written. Yet although they understood the verse to mean that it is a commandment to bless with the Explicit Name, and did so until the death of Simeon, nevertheless they abolished this commandment and refrained from blessing with the Name. It is true that the later sages (Sotah 38a, Tosafot s.v. harei) try to show that this was not an uprooting or abolition of the commandment by an ordinance of the sages, but that they abolished it relying on the will of the Torah itself. For according to their view, and according to what they derived by the ways of midrash elsewhere, they decided that the name of the Lord may not be mentioned in any case except in a place and at a time where there is a revelation of the Shekhinah; and since, in the priests' view, the revelation of the Shekhinah ceased after the death of Simeon the Just,{v1_05_16:2} this commandment was abolished by the law of the Torah itself. But if we consider the words of the Talmudic sages themselves, we find that they did not think, and it did not occur to them to say, that from the days of Simeon the revelation of the Shekhinah ceased in the Temple. We have no proof or hint that any of the masters of the Talmud thought or believed so, and all the more we have no hint that the priests after Simeon's death thought that with his death the Shekhinah departed from the Temple. But in truth it is as we said: the particular reason that the priests had for not pronouncing the Name when they blessed the people has not been preserved for us; but this lies in the nature of the matter, that as they raised Simeon's standing after his death, so, over against his standing, they made their own standing small, and thought that neither they nor their generation were worthy to pronounce the Name even in the Priestly Blessing.

From the few things that have been told us of the standing of Simeon the Just, and from the aggadot attached to his name, it has become clear that he was the most distinguished of all the heads of the generations in the days of the Great Assembly. Therefore without doubt his time too was distinguished by excellent ordinances enacted for the good of the nation, and it is very likely that the ordinance of the closing of the Holy Scriptures was also the work of his time. For from the time that they went up from the exile, led by Ezra the Scribe, the Torah struck its roots in the heart of every man of Israel, and these roots spread over the land of Israel; and then everyone called by the name of Israel was stirred to hold fast to the teachings of the men of understanding. And although there were among them adversaries who harassed them with their wiles, namely the Kutim [Samaritans], whom the king of Assyria settled in the cities of Samaria and who accepted and took upon themselves the religion of Israel, these too lacked strength, and it was not in their power to do anything, great or small, to disturb Israel in their faith. "From the time of Nehemiah until the days of the reign of Alexander they did not then separate in complete separation from the Jews, and some of them celebrated their festivals in Jerusalem" (Kirchheim, in *Karmei Shomron*, p. 2). And there were no other challengers among them against the customs and views of the Jews who would force them to publish some ordinance meant to show opposition to some challenge. "From the days of Alexander of Macedon they — these Kutim — began to call themselves by their own names over the land, and became a lowly kingdom to do evil to Israel — and their temple on Mount Gerizim was a snare and a trap to the inhabitant of Jerusalem. And without doubt at that time was made the copy of the book of the Torah that is in their hands, with the variants and changes without which there would be no basis or root for their worship and their sanctuary" (*Karmei Shomron*, pp. 2–7). And perhaps they made the falsifications already before the time of Alexander, for when Alexander came to Syria their temple already stood on Mount Gerizim, and without the falsifications they made there is no place for worship on Mount Gerizim. Besides the variants and changes that they falsified in the Torah, they opposed the Jews in another matter, which is a great foundation among the principles of the Jewish religion: they denied the prophecy of the other prophets and did not accept their books. Now the falsifications they made in the Torah, and likewise their opposition to the books of the Prophets, were a great cause that moved the sages of the generation to make a fence and a safeguard in this, and to hold with all the strength of their might to the true text of the Torah — and who knows whether the Men of the Great Assembly did not mean this in their saying "Make a fence for the Torah," to warn against falsification, variation and change — and further, to proclaim widely the error of the Kutim and of those Jews who followed them in denying the holiness of the rest of the Holy Scriptures and the books of the Prophets; and they were forced to make known and to declare publicly that all of them are holy and were hewn from a divine source. Now this was one of the causes that compelled them to gather together the number of the Holy Scriptures and to join them together in the holy collection. And the closing [seal] of the Holy Scriptures alludes to this, namely the last four verses of the book of Ecclesiastes (*Moreh Nevukhei ha-Zeman*, gate 11), where it says: "The words of the wise are as goads, and as nails well fastened are the masters of assemblies; they are given from one shepherd" [Ecclesiastes 12:11]. This is a great hint of their intention, to say: that all the words in the Writings flowed from one spirit and their foundation is in holiness. But they had yet another reason to close the Holy Scriptures: from the time the Greeks came to the land of Judah, new views were brought to them, some of which found favor in the eyes of the Jews and their sages, and some of which were dangerous to the Torah and to the faith. Their acquaintance with these views no doubt stirred the hearts of the seekers of wisdom and of the writers among the Jews to follow them in their inquiries, and they also wrote books or essays on the foundations of foreign views such as these. Therefore they were compelled to set a fence and a hedge around the Torah and the authenticated Holy Scriptures, and to guard their unity with extra care, out of worry lest holy seed mingle with profane seed, and books written according to new views be joined to the holy books whose issue is from the spirit of God. And they made it a statute not to be transgressed, that to the accepted Holy Scriptures nothing may be added and from them nothing may be taken away. Now for this reason the second verse of the aforementioned closing also gives a hint, warning: "And further, by these, my son, be admonished: of making many books there is no end" [Ecclesiastes 12:12]. Its meaning is to warn against making books beyond our holy books, for only they have their foundation in holiness, and apart from them they are "much study, a weariness of the flesh" [ibid.]. Now the closing of the Holy Scriptures is not to be put later than the time of the end of the Great Assembly, and that is about fifty years after the Greeks settled in Syria and in the Land. True, it would have been proper for them to bring order to the matter already in the earlier time, since occupation with the Torah was a fixed statute and counted a great commandment, and they also ordained the reading in the Torah and in the Prophets and made translations for the common people; therefore it would have been self-evident that it was fitting to clarify, to arrange and to make known which books were designated for fulfilling the commandment and to be read by religious law.{v1_05_18:1} But the truth is that in early times there was no need for the closing, because they had no reason to worry lest the profane mix with the holy; but in the time we are dealing with the matter was reversed, therefore they were forced to bring order to the matter and to set a fixed statute which books are holy and divine.

It is very, very astonishing to us about the masters of the Mishnah and the Talmud, who sought for themselves places enough to record various enactments that the heads of the early generations enacted, and did not find even a small place to record so great an enactment as this: to say when the Holy Writings were sealed and who sealed them. But in truth the matter is clear: the sealing of the Holy Writings was revealed and known to them, and the one who sealed the whole of all the books makes the act of sealing known explicitly in writing. Therefore they did not specify this enactment separately but only hinted at it in various places, because for them the existence of the sealing was an established assumption, which there is no need to spell out, so well known was it. But there are a great many hints in the words of the sages that point to the sealing. An excellent hint is given us in an aggadah told about the Scroll of Esther. It is told that Mordecai and Esther sent to the sages that the letter of Purim should be written for the generations. At first they refused, until the Holy One, blessed be He, enlightened their eyes, and they found it written in the Torah, in the Prophets and in the Writings. And they interpreted the words of the verse "and it was written in the book" (Esther 9:32) as referring to the Writings (Yerushalmi Megillah 1:5). It is not our purpose to inquire into the nature of this aggadah, but this much comes out explicitly: they thought that what is said, "and it was written in the book," means that they attached the words of Esther to the Writings. And it is also very likely that this is the plain sense of the verse. It appears that in their view the Holy Writings in the days of Mordecai and Esther were divided into Torah, Prophets and Writings, and the whole of the Writings was called "the book." This does not contradict our estimate placing the sealing in the last generations of the Men of the Great Assembly. For it is a clear matter that the Scroll of Esther that we have was composed at a time later than the event, as is seen clearly from several verses in this scroll (26–32); and in the time of the last writer the Holy Writings had already been sealed, and therefore it was hard in the eyes of the sages to attach the scroll to the Writings (see *Moreh Nevukhei ha-Zeman*, gate 11), whose sealing had already taken place earlier. Now the basis of this aggadah stands firm: after the Holy Writings were sealed, they attached to them also the Scroll of Esther, whose time of composition, in the second letter,⚠5 is very late. And this agrees in part with another reasonable hypothesis that deserves to be mentioned here:{v1_05_18:2} namely, that the Holy Writings were not collected at one time. The first collection was very old; it included the Torah and the book of Joshua and was founded in the days of the First Temple. For this reason no more than the five books of the Torah and the book of Joshua are found among the Samaritans — although their book of Joshua has changed much from ours. The second collection was made after their return from the exile, and it includes also the Prophets; its seal is the last verses of the book of Malachi, from "Remember the Torah of Moses My servant" [Malachi 3:22] to the end of the book.{v1_05_19:1} The third collection was later; then the section of the Writings was added, namely most of the book of Psalms, Proverbs, Job, Ruth, the Song of Songs, Lamentations and Ecclesiastes, and the seal of the whole is the last verses of the book of Ecclesiastes. This was still under the rule of the Persians. And there was yet a fourth collection, which added the books of Daniel, Ezra, Nehemiah and Chronicles, and its seal is the Scroll of Esther. This was in the time of the Maccabees. The book of Esther in the text before us was composed in the days of the Maccabees on the basis of manuscripts that they had from an early time. This book was needed in that time, when the wicked kingdom sought to destroy the Jews, as in the early time, in the days of Ahasuerus and the wicked Haman.

After all these things, there is one thing that we can hold with clarity: that the sealing of the Holy Writings and their division took place, at the latest, at the end of the days of the Great Assembly. This follows for us from clear hints in Scripture and from compelling proofs that lie in the events of the times, as we have seen before. Besides these, it appears that in every generation from the time of the Maccabees onward this opinion was taking root among the Jews. Already in the days of the Maccabees it was widespread among the people that the collection of the Holy Writings had been completed in an early time. A hint of this is found in the second book of Maccabees (2:13, 14). And as for the books of Maccabees, although we do not know the time of their composition exactly, it is at any rate clear that they were written while the rule of the Maccabees still stood on its base (*Urschrift* by Dr. A. Geiger, p. 206). The sages of the time of the destruction of the Temple speak of the division by which the Holy Writings were divided into three parts, Torah, Prophets, Writings, as of a matter well known and familiar from days of old (Megillah 7a); and it is self-evident that these parts were sealed and delimited from an early time. So too the saying "All the Holy Writings may be rescued from a fire" (Shabbat ch. 15, mishnah 1)⚠6 shows that there was a delimitation of the Holy Writings from long before. And that mishnah is very old, for early sages already disputed its meaning and the meaning of its companions. A great hint of the sealing of the Holy Writings is found in an aggadah that speaks about the prophecies that were fixed to be kept for the generations. It tells: many prophets arose for Israel, double the number of those who went out of Egypt, but a prophecy that was needed for the generations was written down, and one that was not needed for the generations was not written down (Megillah 14a). The inner idea of this aggadah is that they selected among the words of the prophets which prophecy was fit to be kept for the generations and which was not. And what is the chief idea of the sealing if not the selection of which of the writings or the prophecies should be brought into the body of the Holy Writings and which not? It is further told in a baraita about the writing of the individual Holy Writings: Moses wrote his book and the section of Balaam and Job; Joshua wrote his book and eight verses in the Torah; Samuel wrote his book and Judges and Ruth; David wrote the book of Psalms through ten elders; Jeremiah wrote his book and the book of Kings and Lamentations; Hezekiah and his company wrote Isaiah, Proverbs, the Song of Songs and Ecclesiastes; the Men of the Great Assembly wrote Ezekiel and the Twelve, Daniel and the Scroll of Esther; Ezra wrote his book and the genealogy of Chronicles up to himself, and Nehemiah completed it (Bava Batra 15a). Now it is not our purpose to decide whether the details of these matters are founded on a true tradition or only on conjecture; but this much comes out explicitly, that their view was that there were several collectors at different times. And so we saw in the aggadah mentioned first, that each collector selected what was fit to be kept for the needs of the coming generations, and set aside and removed what was not needed. And since we have also seen that they thought that the Men of the Great Assembly were the last writers, it is self-evident that to them they attributed the last selection among the writings and the prophecies, which of them should be kept and which not; and this selection is itself the sealing of the Holy Writings. Josephus ben Gorion the priest⚠7 also speaks of an ancient division by which the Holy Writings were divided into three parts, and all of them together are twenty-two books, all of which are justly regarded as divine; and several writings that were written from the days of Artaxerxes until his own time were not regarded as so trustworthy as the ancient books, because prophecy had ceased (*Against Apion* 1:8). Now he does not state here his own opinion in particular, but tells us what the opinion was that had spread among the people. And we are informed also in the Talmud that this belief lived among the people. They believed that all the Holy Writings — whose number Josephus puts at twenty-two, and the sages of the Mishnah and the Talmud at twenty-four — were all given by the holy spirit, and that in them a Jew should toil to learn and to teach, and not in other books apart from these (Yerushalmi Sanhedrin ch. 10). The later sages exaggerated this matter in their figurative speech and said: The Holy One, blessed be He, said, I have written twenty-four books for you; take heed not to add to them (Numbers Rabbah ch. 14); for whoever brings into his house more than twenty-four books brings confusion into his house (Ecclesiastes Rabbah, at its end). And from an ancient tradition we have heard that at first they would say that Proverbs, the Song of Songs and Ecclesiastes were hidden away, because they said these were parables and not of the Writings, and they went and hid them away, until the Men of the Great Assembly came and interpreted them (Avot de-Rabbi Natan ch. 1).{v1_05_20:1} Now, besides the proofs that come from the sealing of the books of Ecclesiastes and of the Scroll of Esther, by which the antiquity of the sealing of the Holy Writings has been made clear, and besides all the hints given in several ancient aggadot, we have seen that the sages who lived in the time of the destruction of the Temple and before it spoke of the collection of the Holy Writings and of their division into three parts as of a matter well known and familiar from long before. And now, if we join to this the causes that compel the sealing of the Holy Writings in the last generation of the Men of the Great Assembly, it is neither wondrous nor far-fetched that we set the time of the sealing in this generation.

From this time, when the Holy Writings were sealed, a special name arose for the other books that were not included among the Holy Writings, and they were called by the name "external books." Most of them, children of that time, are lost. The book of Ben Sira is one of the external books; of it we have small Hebrew remnants in the Talmud, and the whole of it is in a Greek translation. This book too is not included in the Holy Writings, although all of it is moral instruction in parable and figure, like the Proverbs of Solomon. The reason, without doubt, is that at the time this book was composed the Holy Writings had already been sealed; and not as the one who says (Hitzig, in his commentary on Psalms, 118) that no Hebrew copy was spread among the people, because the original book remained in the hand of his grandson, who took it with him when he went down to Egypt, and so the last collectors could not attach it to the Holy Writings. For how is it possible that the original book was lost, when after several hundred years we find it in the hands of the sages of the Talmud, who copied sayings from it? And when we see that the author asks the children of his people at the end of his book to meditate in it to learn wisdom and instruction, surely he made copies of his book in order to attain his wish and his aim. But on the other hand, we cannot bring a proof that the Holy Writings had necessarily been sealed before this, on the ground that otherwise they would have attached the book of Ben Sira to the Holy Writings; for even if he completed his book before the sealing, it is possible that it was not attached to the Holy Writings for another reason, namely because the author put his name on his book, and this was enough for those who sealed not to bring his book in among the Holy Writings. Yet the truth is indeed that the book of Ben Sira is later than the sealing, as we have seen from other proofs. However, even if after the sealing they did not add to the collection of the Holy Writings, nevertheless they did not refrain from time to time from breaking this statute in part, by adding individual sections to the ancient writings; for in this they did not see a breach of the statute, since the warning was only against making many books without end. All the psalms that were composed in the days of the Maccabees were not put together in a book of their own but were attached to the ancient book of Psalms, because they were strict about the warning against making separate books and adding to the Holy Writings, but they were not strict about adding sections to the books. Only for a great need did they turn aside from this and add a separate book, such as the Scroll of Esther, which they attached to the Holy Writings in the days of the Maccabees, as said above. And yet, on the contrary, we have seen that at a later time the fixed sealing did not hold them back from questioning it. For in the last half-century before the destruction of the Temple the sages gathered in the house of Ḥananiah ben Hezekiah ben Garon and wished to hide away the book of Ezekiel, and but for the efforts of Ḥananiah they would have hidden it away (Shabbat 13b; Ḥagigah 13a). It is also told that they sought to hide away the book of Ecclesiastes (Shabbat 30b), and the same is said of the book of Proverbs and the Song of Songs (Avot de-Rabbi Natan ch. 1). And although this account moves the time of the event back to the days of the Men of the Great Assembly, the matter is not certain, and it seems more likely that all these hidings took place at one time, as will be made clear further below (ch. 22).

From the extreme warning by which they were warned not to make books there came also the restraint from writing words of Torah in general, so that they refrained from writing their teachings and their midrashim in a book. Now in this we find the first root of the prohibition of writing for all matters, new or old, that were received; for whatever was beyond these they took care not to write. But what the later authorities thought in this matter, that it was so intended by the Torah and hinted at in what is said, "Write thou these words" [Exodus 34:27] — these you write, but you do not write halakhot (Temurah 14b) — is a midrashic figure of speech. For from of old the prophets wrote their prophecies in a book, and several matters that are of the kind of laws are within their prophecies; and if we say that the statute from former times was that oral matters you are not permitted to say in writing, why did they write their traditions? True, one can answer this that the spirit of the Lord upon them gave them understanding; yet for this reason there is still no proof of the necessity that the matters be written, for we do not find that they were commanded or permitted to write the matters in a book. And according to the opinion of the sages of the Talmud, several prophets did not write their prophecies at all, and those who came after them wrote them (Bava Batra 14b); and who permitted them this? Therefore it can only be that from the time they were warned against making books without end, from then also they refrained from writing both halakhot and interpretations of the Torah, and not before. However, the cause of the restraint from writing their halakhot and their interpretations in a book lies in the nature of the interpretations and the halakhot. We have already seen above that the interpretations of the Torah depended on the agreement of the great court of each time (above, ch. 9), and the received traditions too were destined to expand; therefore it was more fitting for them to be expounded orally than to be written in a book (*Moreh Nevukhei ha-Zeman*, ch. 13), for when they are written in a book they are nailed fast and become a fixed statute, which is not so when they are expounded orally. Now this cause is indeed reasonable for the restraint from writing words of Torah and their interpretations and composing them into a book; but this cause does not hold with regard to writing such matters for the benefit of remembering them or for another purpose. Therefore we have no compelling cause to think that they refrained from writing anything small or great for their own use. And indeed a slight examination of the Talmudic literature from the first times shows the opposite. If we consider how much the old mishnayot, which are marked especially by their brevity and their language, differ from the mishnayot of the later generations, then we must necessarily decide that they are written remnants that the early ones wrote for themselves; especially since it was a rule and a duty with them that every man should say words of Torah in the language of his teacher. Therefore the disciples surely wrote for themselves the words of their teachers in order to know them in their exact language; for the mind cannot bear that so great a sum as the old halakhot remained in the memory of every single disciple in the exact language of the early ones, unless we say that they were recorded in writing with them. We further find that at times they turned aside from the habit and in time of need wrote down established matters⚠8 in a special scroll, as in the days of Ḥananiah ben Hezekiah ben Garon they wrote Megillat Ta'anit (Shabbat 13b). We also find that several sages departed from the habit. For in the days of Rabban Gamaliel the Elder a book of a Targum of Job was found, and this was without doubt by an early translator (Shabbat 115a); and every Targum was regarded by them as oral matter. And surely the Targum of Job was not the first that they wrote down; rather, Targums of other books too were with them in writing at that time (Zunz, *Gottesdienstliche Vorträge*, p. 62). So too the Greek translation of the Seventy was in writing, and whoever studies that translation will find in it several oral interpretations; and there was no one among the sages who questioned it on the ground that it is not permitted to write; rather it is said that the day on which this translation was given was hard for Israel because the Torah cannot be translated adequately (Tractate Soferim ch. 1). Not only that, but from this story — if it is indeed an old tradition — we can bring a clear proof that the masters of the Talmud too thought that the early ones did not refrain from writing words of tradition. For although their saying that the day was hard for Israel because the Torah cannot be translated adequately may mean the same as what the grandson of Ben Sira said about his Greek translation, in which he translated the book of his grandfather (in the preface to his translation), that the Greek version of the Holy Writings is not able to attain the force of the Hebrew language in every way, nevertheless it is also possible, and more in agreement with the views of the sages of the Talmud, to say that the reason the Torah cannot be translated adequately is that the translators, according to the aim they had before them, had to translate the words according to their plain sense, while the Torah must be interpreted according to its midrash. And so we see that even in the view of the sages, had the translators been able to interpret and translate adequately, it would not have occurred to them to question this matter on the ground of the prohibition of writing.

But at a later time this prohibition reached a new level. When other sects arose in the midst of our people, which turned far from the faith of their fathers' tradition, countless various scrolls were written according to the new opinions, also in the Hebrew, Aramaic or Syriac language. We have a small remnant of one of these scrolls in the Aramaic language (Shabbat 115b). At that time the habit became a fixed statute. R. Ishmael, who lived at the end of the first century of the rise of this sect, is the one from whom the first hint of the prohibition of writing halakhot has reached us. His disciples brought in his name in these words: "Write thou these words" [Exodus 34:27] — these you write, but you do not write halakhot (Temurah 14b). And whoever considers the multitude of the midrashim of the Gnostics and of other scrolls full of midrashim, which were written at the end of the aforementioned first century and also after it, will find that they were compelled to make a fence about the matter and to warn against writing new books in general. The aforementioned cause through which they upheld the prohibition of writing is hinted at in an aggadah which a Talmudic sage put figuratively in the form of a conversation between the Holy One, blessed be He, and Moses: When the Holy One, blessed be He, said "Write thou," Moses asked that the Mishnah too be in writing; but because the Holy One, blessed be He, foresaw that the nations of the world would in future translate the Torah and read it in Greek, and they would say, "We are Israel," and until now the scales are evenly balanced, the Holy One, blessed be He, said to them: You say that you are My children; I know only that those who hold My mysteries are [My children]; and which is that? The Mishnah (Tanḥuma, Ki Tissa). The core of this aggadah is told in different forms (Exodus Rabbah ch. 47; Numbers Rabbah ch. 14), and from this we see that the basis of the aggadah and its idea had already been forgotten by them. But its basis is to show that the cause of the restraint from writing oral matters lies in the rise of the aforementioned faith, when writings in the form of midrashim spread, and they were compelled to warn against writing. And we already know that such extreme warnings are mostly proofs of a habit to the contrary. Therefore, from the fact that they were so strict in warning, we see that the custom was to write. But it is hard for us to prove that this warning had a lasting effect on all the sages of that time and after it, so that we should say that from that time onward all of them refrained from writing anything of halakhah and midrash, even a single letter (Rashi, Eruvin 62b); for there are trustworthy proofs that in every time sages departed from the custom and wrote books and scrolls. True, an excuse is offered for these scrolls, and they are called by the name "hidden scrolls," which they would write and put away in hiding (Shabbat 6b, and Rashi's commentary). But what does the hiding help, if from of old the prohibition of writing stood as a fixed statute? Moreover, besides the scrolls that were called hidden scrolls, we find that there were scrolls written by the greatest of the sages, such as the Ribbit of the school of R. Ḥiyya (Bava Metzia 62b), the Nezikin of the school of Karna (Sanhedrin 30b), and the Kiddushin of the school of Levi; and one of the later sages found an ancient Tosefta (Yerushalmi Shabbat 8:1); and Levi had written mishnayot and corrected them (Yevamot 10a); and R. Jeremiah said to R. Zerika, "Go, look in your Mekhilta" (Gittin 44a); and R. Yoḥanan, who heard of the existence of the baraitot arranged in the Sifra, went and learned them in three days and reasoned over them three months (Yevamot 72b), and it is not said from whom he learned them, so surely the Sifra existed in writing. And all the more so in aggadic midrash, in which there were many written books, among them the Aggadeta of the school of Rav (Sanhedrin 57a), and books of aggadah that were before R. Joshua ben Levi (Yerushalmi Shabbat 15:2) and that were before R. Yoḥanan (Gittin 60a); and R. Abba, the brother of R. Ḥiyya, would write aggadot in a book (Yerushalmi Shabbat, ibid.). Here we have before us that despite all the warnings by which they were warned, they wrote down every kind of words of Torah. In this account we have gone several steps ahead on the path of time, in our wish to show that in all times the sages did not keep this warning with great strictness and were not strict about it, but wrote oral matters in a book. In short, it is clear that the beginning of the warning against writing books on matters of Torah was because they did not want to make the Oral Torah, which is destined to expand, fixed and cut off. Later they fenced this in even more because of the spread of the sect of the minim. But nevertheless the sages did not refrain, for themselves, from writing for the purpose of memory; and all that many of the sages after the Talmud, down to the latest generations, have decided in order to prove the opposite of this is built only on fine points of detail, which, if we pass over them with a spirit of faithful criticism, are gone and are no more.{v1_05_23:1}
