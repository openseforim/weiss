v1_07_10:1) The Talmud gives a reason why Syria is reckoned as the Land of Israel: because the conquest of an individual counts as a conquest, and David conquered the land of Syria (Gittin 8b). There are various explanations of what "the conquest of an individual" is (see Avodah Zarah 21a, in Tosafot). And if we say that the first sanctification did not sanctify for the time to come, what does it help that the conquest of an individual counts as a conquest, seeing that those who came up from Babylonia did not conquer it? But the truth is that this halakhah was taught in the days of Yoḥanan, when he conquered part of Syria, and at that time they enacted the three things listed in the Tosefta that we quoted in the text; and surely at a later time they added to them; and in vain did Tosafot, s.v. *kasavar*, raise their difficulty — see on this, for I have been brief. And I will list most of the places that speak of the law of the land of Syria, and you will easily judge for yourself what is early and what is late: Demai 6:11; Shevi'it 6:2 and 6:6; Ma'aserot 5:5; Ḥallah 4:7 and 4:11; Orlah 3:9; Rosh Hashanah 1:5; Bava Kamma ch. 7; Avodah Zarah 1:8; Ohalot 18:7; Tosefta Pe'ah ch. 4, cited in Ketubbot (25a), and in the Tosefta there ch. 3; and [Tosefta] Demai ch. 1, Terumot ch. 2, Shevi'it ch. 4, Ma'aserot ch. 3, Orlah ch. 1, Shekalim ch. 3, Avodah Zarah ch. 2, ch. 4 and ch. 5. Besides these, see in both Talmuds on all the mishnahs I have cited.

v1_07_11:1) See Gittin (8a). The reading in the Babylonian [Talmud] is: "One who wishes to enter it in purity may enter. — But did you not say its soil is impure? — In a litter, a chest or a cupboard." The reading of the Tosefta, "that if one can enter in purity, it is pure," implies that the land is pure, and this reading has no explanation according to the plain sense of the Gemara. But it appears from the Mishnah in Ohalot that this reading is the principal one, since in the Mishnah too the word "pure" refers to the land; and see there the commentaries of Maimonides and of R. Samson [of Sens] on this mishnah, who agree with our explanation and departed from the explanation of the Talmud. And it is difficult in my eyes to decide that the baraita in the Talmud is a different baraita, for anyone who has eyes will see that it is the baraita in the Tosefta, only its version in the Gemara is corrupt. And this corrupt version caused the masters of the Talmud to depart from the plain explanation that the Mishnah requires.

v1_07_12:1) Geiger, in his book mentioned above [*Urschrift*], p. 178, thinks that in the halakhah of the Talmud too there is a memory of this old halakhah, and he brings the baraita of Sifrei Re'eh, piska 72: "R. Yose says: One might think that they are liable only for ṭevel from which nothing at all has been separated; whence [do we know that if] terumah was separated from it but first tithe was not separated from it; first tithe, but second tithe was not separated from it; second tithe, but the poor man's tithe was not separated from it — whence etc." And it is proved from his language that he speaks of three tithes in one year. But I deliberately left out this proof, because even if we say that there is in it a memory of an old halakhah that deviates from the new, there is still no proof in what respect it deviates: whether, as Geiger says, the old [halakhah] held that in the third year there are three tithes, or it deviates in this, that it held that in the third year there are two tithes, except that it is not second tithe that is set aside but first tithe, and in the third year one gives second tithe and the poor man's tithe, as in the first assumption of the Sifrei, piska 109 there; for in this way too the language of the Sifrei in piska 72 is well explained. The second proof he brings is from Sifrei 302: "'In the third year' etc. — one might think that two tithes apply in it; I have only the poor man's tithe, of which Scripture speaks; whence to include the other tithes?" And the meaning according to him is: one might think that only two tithes apply, or only the poor man's tithe alone; Scripture says "all" etc. [Deuteronomy 26:12] — it included [them]. But I have not found anywhere in the Sifrei or the Sifra or the Mekhilta a passage that says "one might think" and "I have only" one right after the other; for these two expressions indicate that it means: from this verse I would have said such-and-such. Now if it says "one might think that it speaks of two tithes," how can it say at once "I have from the verse only one tithe"? Necessarily there is an omission here, and it should read as there, in piska 109.

v1_07_15:1) It is not our purpose to tell of the history of the Kutim, their character and their faith. R. v1_07_15:2) On the laws in which the Kutim are equal to Israel, see Berakhot 7:1; Demai 3:4, 5:9, 7:4; Terumot 3:9 (see Niddah 4:5). And there are mishnayot in which the Kutim are like gentiles; see Berakhot 8:8; Sheqalim 1:5; (Oholot 9:3?); Ṭohorot 5:8. All of these are anonymous, and they are old halakhot, from the time of Yoḥanan onward; and the former were taught afterward, when they made the law of the Kutim more lenient. In this way a resolution is found for a number of apparent contradictions. See Ḥullin (13a): "his bread (of a min) is the bread of a Kuti," and the Tosafot there; and there (4a), Tosafot s.v. matzat Kuti. Now in the name of R. Eliezer they said: "Whoever eats the bread of the Kutim is like one who eats pork"; and R. Akiva said: "Be silent, I will not tell you what R. Eliezer said," that is, that R. Eliezer was very lenient. If so, is there not a contradiction in what they received from R. Eliezer? But it is not so. For it is known that R. Eliezer is the master of old halakhot, and he received this tradition, that one who eats the bread of a Kuti is like one who eats pork, whose basis, in my opinion, lies in the time of Yoḥanan and in reliance on the story in Pirkei de-Rabbi Eliezer, except that there one must read Yoḥanan in place of Ezra. But what R. Akiva said in the name of R. Eliezer refers to R. Eliezer's own opinion about the Kutim of his time, whose law they had made lenient. Perhaps this was because, from the time of the war at the end of the days of the Temple, the Kutim joined the Jews against the Romans, so that the hatred between the two sides then ceased. See also *Karmei Shomron*, p. 10.

v1_07_17:1) The conclusion of the Gemara in Menaḥot is: "He has fulfilled [his vow] and is liable to karet." This is astonishing: how could it occur to anyone that a man fulfills his obligation with a sacrifice and is liable to karet for offering it? And they said that if he offered it in the house of Onias he has not fulfilled [his vow]; if he were liable to karet, why did they say only "he has not fulfilled"? Should they not have said also "and is liable to karet"? And the baraitot in the Tosefta ch. 12, where it is said explicitly "he has not fulfilled and is liable to karet; he has fulfilled and is liable to karet," are of no use to us, for anyone who has an eye to see will find that these baraitot are not ancient but were founded on the conclusion of the Gemara, as is the case with many baraitot in the Tosefta. This will be clarified further below when we speak of the Tosefta. In the Gemara R. Yoḥanan explained the Mishnah in this direction, and they try to bring proof for R. Yoḥanan's explanation and did not cite the Tosefta; and Rava too tried to reconcile the matter and did not know of the Tosefta. And from [the statement of] Rabbah bar bar Ḥana in the name of R. Yoḥanan, "One who says, 'I take upon myself a burnt offering that I will offer in the house of Onias,' and offered it in the Land of Israel, has fulfilled and is liable to karet," it is proved that if he offered it in the house of Onias he is not liable to karet; for otherwise, why did he not tell us a greater novelty, that even when he offered it in the house of Onias he is liable to karet? Nor is anything proved from the baraita in the Gemara, "I take upon myself a burnt offering that I will offer in the wilderness," and he offered it across the Jordan, "he has fulfilled and is liable to karet"; for this was said only of places where it was not permitted, but in the house of Onias it was permitted for the needs of the hour. And indeed they said explicitly (there, 109b) that they offered burnt offerings on it for the sake of Heaven, and this shows that they gave a justification for the matter and were not concerned about [the prohibition of] slaughtering outside [the Temple]. What the Tosafot wrote, that the altar was for the descendants of Noah, is contrary to the facts. In short, "he has fulfilled and is liable to karet" is only for the sake of dialectic.

v1_07_18:1) R. Zacharias Frankel, in *Darkhei ha-Mishnah*, p. 34, asked: what did the Sages answer him? Was Joshua not to be believed? And how did they decide that it is clean for all Israel? Let them ask those coming from Alexandria whether Joshua's words are correct! He explains that the water drawn by the antlia was at first desired for something attached [to the ground], and of the water much moisture remained and came onto the harvested wheat. Joshua considered that this falls under liquid whose beginning was desired, even though its end was not desired, which is under "if [water] be put" [Leviticus 11:38]; and the Sages disagree, since at the beginning it was for the need of something attached, and it is like "so that the wall may be rinsed" (Makhshirin 4:3). Now if this is by way of dialectic, we accept it; but if as law, there is an answer. For it still needs proof whether water drawn to soften the ground and water it, where one also wants to soften the clods of earth on it, falls under the need of something attached; and we have learned (there 3:4): "One who sprinkles his house and put wheat in it, and it became damp: if from the water, it is under 'if [water] be put.'" Further, if they disagreed on the law, there is a dispute here; why then did they say there was no dispute in Israel? Therefore it is certain that Joshua did not say "certainly unclean," but that there is a concern for impurity, lest they became moistened, as I wrote in the text. And the Sages answered: if he wants to be stringent with himself because of a concern, let him be stringent, but not for others. And there is proof from the Tosefta beside it: "Ḥalafta ben Kuyna says: the garlic of Baal Bekhi is unclean, because they sprinkle it with water and afterward braid it. The Sages said: If so, let it be unclean for Ḥalafta and clean for all Israel." Here the reason of the Sages is without doubt that we are not concerned at all lest water came on it; for if they agreed that water certainly came on it, there would be no doubt that it is unclean according to all. Therefore also in the Tosefta that speaks of Joshua, whose wording and form are exactly like the first, the meaning of the words is without doubt the same. And there, 3:5, we learned: "One who sprinkles his threshing floor need not be concerned lest he put wheat in it and it became damp." Whoever studies this mishnah will find that it is the very law of the Tosefta according to our explanation, and there is no need to go on about this further.

v1_07_19:1) From the wording of this story it appears that it is old but fragmentary. (a) The statement "and the sages of Israel departed in anger" is difficult. For if, as Rashi explains, the king was angry with them, it is not in its place here, but only afterward, when Eleazar incited him against the Pharisees. (b) Judah sinned, and the wrath went out against all the Pharisees? Surely the Pharisees too were angry with Judah, as Josephus tells. (c) The words of Eleazar too are not understood, when he said: "A commoner in Israel, such is his judgment, and you are king and high priest, such is your judgment"; Rashi explains: to bear his insult; but this is not the meaning of the word din. Therefore it seems to me that a whole statement is missing, and the meaning of the words is this: after the matter was investigated and found false, the sages of Israel departed in anger at Judah, as Josephus tells. And after the statement "and the sages of Israel departed in anger" a statement is missing whose content is that they sentenced Judah to lashes, as is also told in Josephus; and on this Eleazar said: "King Yannai, a commoner in Israel, such is his judgment, lashes, for one who spreads an evil name about his fellow; and you are king and high priest: is it not fitting that there be another punishment greater than this?" This agrees with the words of Josephus, who wrote that after they had passed the sentence of lashes, the Sadducee incited John against the Pharisees. From this event it follows for us that lashes for one who spreads an evil name was something the Sadducees agreed with, and necessarily the words "and they shall chastise him" [Deuteronomy 22:18], said of one who spreads an evil name, were understood as lashes, and this was not learned by a gezerah shavah (Ketubbot 46a); and in the Sifrei, Ki Teze, section 238, no gezerah shavah is mentioned. We further learn that in the view of the Sadducees the punishment of one who spreads an evil name follows the rank of the one shamed, while in the view of the Pharisees there is one law for every person. And see what I wrote in the journal *Ha-Shaḥar*, published by R. Peretz Smolenskin, year 2, issue 3.
