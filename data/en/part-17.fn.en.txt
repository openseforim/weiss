v1_09_02:1) In the Sifrei, at its end, we learn: Hillel came up from Babylonia at forty years of age, served [the sages] forty years, and led Israel forty years. And in Shabbat (15a) they said: Hillel held his patriarchate before the Temple [was destroyed] a hundred years. According to the Sifrei he became patriarch at eighty years of age, and was born in the year 3650 of the world. Now when he came up from Babylonia he was already a sage, for he expounded, reached agreement and received the halakhah (Sifra Tazria ch. 10; Tosefta Nega'im ch. 1; Yerushalmi Pesaḥim 6:1). If he had lived there forty years and served, how is it that the patriarchs did not know of him? And in Pesaḥim it is proved that they did not recognize him, and one of the disciples hinted at him and said: “There is a certain man, Hillel the Babylonian is his name.” But it is true that the number in the Sifrei is not exact. And it appears that in his youth he went up to the Land of Israel to learn Torah from Shemaiah and Avtalyon; and perhaps at the time that Herod persecuted the sages he returned to his country, and after the land was quiet he came back once to the Land of Israel for some reason, and then it came about that he became patriarch, as told in the text. And it is possible that he became patriarch at forty years of age and led Israel forty years; according to this all the days of his life were eighty years, as Graetz fixed his time, vol. 3, p. 207. Therefore, if he became patriarch a hundred years before the destruction, and lived eighty years and led Israel forty years, then the year of his birth falls a hundred and forty years before the destruction, which is the year 3690 of the world. But in truth there is no proof by which we can fix the year of his birth with complete exactness, for we do not know clearly the exact number of the days of his life; therefore I have estimated his time only approximately, not exactly.

Judah ben Bathyra said: What shall I do, for the sages said: A betrothed woman etc. does not eat terumah until she enters the bridal canopy — is this not the enactment of the last mishnah, after R. Tarfon and R. Akiva (see Ketubot 57), whereas R. Judah ben Bathyra lived a long time before this enactment? But he did not look at the baraita in the Yerushalmi, Ketubot 5:3, where it is proved that there were three mishnayot. The first mishnah: A betrothed daughter of Israel eats terumah at once, and by Torah law — and this is like the midrash of R. Judah ben Bathyra. They reversed themselves and said: after twelve months, when he becomes liable for her maintenance. And the court of the latter [time] said: A woman never eats terumah until she enters the bridal canopy. This baraita is the foundation of our Mishnah. What is called in our Mishnah "the first mishnah" is the middle one of the baraita. Its root is: If the time came and they were not married, she eats of his and eats terumah. R. Tarfon and R. Akiva dispute its conditions, but it cannot enter the mind that R. Tarfon and R. Akiva founded it. Therefore it is very possible that all three mishnayot are earlier than R. Tarfon and R. Akiva, and it is no wonder if the early R. Judah ben Bathyra knew of all three. It further appears that, although R. Tarfon explains the middle mishnah — from which it follows that in the time of R. Tarfon and R. Akiva they practiced according to the decision of the middle one — they nevertheless inclined away from it, to practice according to the first mishnah, that a betrothed woman eats terumah at once when she is betrothed; for in the Tosefta Ketubot ch. 5 they tell of R. Tarfon that he betrothed three hundred women in years of famine in order to feed them terumah; and of necessity, in a time of need, they set the matter on Torah law, as in the first mishnah and as in the midrash of R. Judah ben Bathyra.

v1_09_09:1) There a story is told of a man whose sons did not behave properly toward him, and he wrote his property over to Jonathan ben Uzziel. What did Jonathan ben Uzziel do? He sold a third, consecrated a third, and returned a third to the man's sons. Shammai came upon him with his staff and his bag. He said to him: Shammai, you cannot take back what I have sold, etc. And Shammai said: Ben Uzziel has rebuffed me. Rashbam explains “with his staff and his bag” as: to dispute with him. From this the scholar Graetz concluded that Shammai went out against him with a staff to compel him, and that this is a sign of his irascibility. But the intention of the baraita is not so. In my opinion one should judge the reverse: from the fact that Shammai went to him and did not summon him to come before him, as is the way of a master with a disciple, it is shown that he dealt with him in humility. And as for his coming with his staff and his bag, one should not explain that he came with a staff to chastise him and compel him, for if so, why his bag? Rather, the phrase “came with his staff and his bag” is a standing expression everywhere for describing setting out on a journey, as in Shabbat (31a): “This proselyte came with his staff and his bag”; and in Rosh Hashanah chapter 2, in the story of Rabban Gamaliel, we find “he took his staff and his money in his hand.” And as for the author of the statement using the word “upon him,” which implies that he came upon him with a staff to chastise him, as in “that you come to me with staves” [1 Samuel 17:43], said in the story of Goliath, such a fine point is not enough to take the phrase “came with his staff and his bag” out of its plain sense. But know that the whole story is told in the Yerushalmi, Nedarim end of chapter 4, in another manner and the very reverse of what is told in the Bavli; therefore it is hard to draw conclusions from it for the benefit of history.
