Chapter Twenty-Three

Beliefs and Opinions of the Jews.

The foundations of the beliefs and opinions on which the Jewish religion is built are the Torah and the other holy writings. Of the manner in which these foundations were understood, and how they lived within the nation from the day they came up from the exile until the destruction of the Temple, nothing complete and ordered has reached us; but from the midrashic remnants in the books of the Oral Torah and the other books of the Jews of that time we shall recognize well the opinions of the Pharisaic Jews and come to know the cornerstones of their beliefs, which were sealed with the seal of the early time.

The attributes of God's essence in the holy writings are mostly corporeal attributes. But it was already hinted in the Torah itself that these attributes are not to be taken in their plain sense. When it is said: 'for you saw no manner of form' [Deuteronomy 4:15], or: 'for man shall not see Me and live' [Exodus 33:20], or 'Is the Lord's hand shortened?' [Numbers 11:23], this was a compelling proof for one who reflects that the essence of God has no form, and that the concept of seeing God and the attribute 'the hand of the Lord' are nowhere to be understood according to their literal meaning. It is true that in the most ancient times they did not enter into subtle inquiries on the essence of God, and did not attend to the consequences that follow from believing in the existence of God in this way or that. But from the time that enlightenment increased, and especially from the time that the opinions of the sages of the nations among whom they dwelt became known to them, and they grew accustomed to inquiries into metaphysical matters, it was impossible that this should not be for them a great stimulus to reflect in speculative inquiry on the lofty cornerstone of the attributes with which the essence of God is described in Scripture. In the Septuagint we already find the effort of the sages of Israel to soften⚠1 the attributes of corporeality. And although that translation is not the work of the sages of the Land of Israel, at any rate it was made with their approval. And the Aramaic translations, whose root is ancient, made this effort their cornerstone. Some of the emendations of the Scribes also show their desire to remove corporeality, as was already explained above. And a Jewish sage of Alexandria — Aristobulus — said to Ptolemy Philometor: 'As in common speech we use the word "hand" to signify power ... so it is in the words of the Torah' (Eusebius, *P[raeparatio] E[vangelica]* VIII, 10). In these words we find, almost word for word, a saying current in the mouth of the sages of the Land of Israel: 'The Torah spoke in the language of men.' And although this saying is found only regarding the interpretation of doubled expressions in the Torah, nevertheless the example in the words of Aristobulus shows us that it was said also about the attributes of corporeality. And in different words this saying is found explicitly on this matter: 'We describe Him so as to soothe the ear with what it is able to hear; we describe Him by His creatures' (Mekhilta Ba-Ḥodesh ch. 4). It is clear that the sages of the Land of Israel strove to remove corporeality (R. Ḥayyim Oppenheim in my journal *Beit ha-Midrash*, p. 65). But they did not enter into philosophical speculations like the Alexandrians; they went in their simplicity, and with the help of their midrash on the Torah they interpreted and decided. One idea runs through all the midrashim of the sages: that the essence of God is simple, is not a body, and is exalted above the thought of man; and because He is simple they also called Him by abstract names and called Him 'Might' (Gevurah) and 'Shekhinah.' But God reveals Himself to men according to the measure of their human apprehension, and the vision that they see is not so in essence; rather, the Holy One, blessed be He, shows it to them so by way of miracle. And since, according to their belief, God reveals Himself to men in a corporeal vision, therefore it is permitted also to speak of Him in corporeal attributes. This idea goes round through all the midrashim of the sages. In the simplicity of their faith they found the source of this idea in the Torah and the Prophets, saying that the Torah's words 'This is my God and I will glorify Him' [Exodus 15:2] teach that God was revealed to Israel in a sensible vision by way of miracle, so that the maidservant at the sea saw a vision of God and pointed at Him with her finger (Mekhilta Shirah ch. 3). And it was easy for them to explain the saying 'The Lord is a man of war' [Exodus 15:3], which is corporealization with respect to God, who fills heaven and earth; for they believed that the Holy One, blessed be He, out of His love for Israel, was revealed to them in various visions: once as a man of war, in order to sanctify His name (ibid. ch. 4), and once as an old man full of mercy (ibid. and Ba-Ḥodesh ch. 5). And they said well that these modes of the revelation of the Shekhinah depend on the deeds of Israel: that they make His right hand left if they do not do His will, and His left hand right if they do His will; and He neither slumbers nor sleeps if they do His will, and, as it were, there is sleep before Him if they do not do His will. Already the addition of the words 'as it were' shows that it means to say that the action of Israel causes them to imagine a change in His actions. And one of the Tannaim who lived in the first century after the destruction said: 'At times the world and its fullness cannot contain His divinity, and at times He speaks with a man from between the hairs of his head' (Genesis Rabbah ch. 4). And another Tanna explains the Torah's words 'and when I see the blood' [Exodus 12:13], which is a corporeal attribute, and interprets that the meaning is that as the reward for a commandment that Israel performs, the Holy One, blessed be He, reveals Himself and has pity on them (Mekhilta Bo ch. 7). We have no clearer effort to remove corporeality than their saying that if it is written in the Torah 'a jealous God' [Exodus 20:5], the intention is not to describe God with the attribute of jealousy, for jealousy does not rule over Him, but He rules over jealousy (ibid. Ba-Ḥodesh ch. 6). There is no counting such traces in the books of the Oral Torah. Thus we have seen that their belief in the essence of God was not in a corporeal manner. And if their words about God are corporeal words, this is no proof about their concepts and their belief, for so it was of old and so it will be forever, that men speak of God in human attributes. But although their apprehension of the essence of God was spiritual, nevertheless they did not enter into philosophical speculations and did not interpret according to scientific premises and abstract inquiries; rather, they took their premises only from the holy writings, and in relation to Scripture they interpreted this cornerstone, and even this only by a slight hint.

The most anthropomorphic passages of Scripture are the visions in the book of Ezekiel, which are known by the name Ma'aseh Merkavah [the Work of the Chariot]. Expounding those visions was a matter with which the sages occupied themselves. But they did not expound them before the multitude, nor in a gathering of colleagues and disciples, and it was a rule that one does not expound the Chariot unless one is a sage who understands of his own knowledge (Ḥagigah 2:1). For they held that a corruption of belief is not as dangerous when people believe the words as they are written as it is dangerous if such sublime matters are handed over to those of short understanding. For anthropomorphism is dangerous because it leads to idolatry, and this danger had already been overcome since the days of the exile, for since then the belief in the God of Israel had taken root in the nation, and they recognized His unity and His special providence; but speculative inquiry could bring them to the opinions of the Greek philosophers, which were dangerous to the belief in the essence of God, and to believe, as many of them believed, that God and the world are one. Of the midrash of Ma'aseh Merkavah we have only broken remnants. It was called "the secrets of the Holy One, blessed be He" and also "the secrets of the Torah" (Pesaḥim 119a). By the name "secrets of the Holy One, blessed be He" they also called the Targum of Jonathan ben Uzziel (Megillah 3a), and there is no other reason to call it by this name except that Jonathan in his Targum expounds the Work of the Chariot; therefore his Targum will serve us as a partial example of the character of the midrash of the Chariot. Now Jonathan hints that the vision of the Chariot is not to be taken in a material way, as a real sight, but that the prophet's seeing was in a prophetic vision, and the sight is a vision of the glory of the Shekhinah (Ezekiel 1:1); and when it is God's will to let His prophets hear the word of prophecy, a voice goes forth from heaven (ibid. 25); and all the images that were shown to him in his vision were formed for the moment and engraved in his imagination. And where he sees the hands of a man under the wings of the living creatures, and the face of a lion on the right and the face of the ox on the left, the Targum hints deliberately that these images were made for living creatures that he imagined he saw (ibid. 8, 10). With these small hints the Targum strips the garment of anthropomorphism from the Work of the Chariot, and holds that in this vision the Holy One, blessed be He, revealed to him sublime spiritual matters in sensory images to which man is accustomed in the material world. And Ben Sira said well (49:8) of the visions of God that Ezekiel saw, that the Holy One, blessed be He, showed him His glory in the Chariot; and accordingly the removal of anthropomorphism is part of the midrash of the Chariot. Despite all the warnings they gave not to expound the Chariot, there were at all times sages who occupied themselves with expounding it, and so the images in the prophet's words became matter for the imagination to set up towers flying in the air. And that, despite all this, only small traces remained in the books of the Oral Torah was because most of the masters of halakhah had no satisfaction in the masters of secret lore and did not occupy themselves with the secrets of the Torah. But the masters of secret lore, who "please themselves in the children" [Isaiah 2:6] of the soul's fancies, were at that time chiefly the sect of the Essenes. Already then they had secret writings, and they occupied themselves with the study of the angels, prophesied and interpreted dreams (Josephus), and expounded on the existence of God and on the creation of the world (Philo). From this it appears that the core of their occupation was the Work of the Chariot and the Work of Creation. Some remnants of their teaching were brought up in the book of Enoch; and after this sect came to an end, and some of its members joined others and their remnant went up among the Pharisees from whom they had come out, then too some of their secrets came out into the light, and a few remnants of them have survived for us in the Talmud (Jellinek, introduction to *Beit ha-Midrash*, part 3, XX, XXI). But inquiry into such subtle speculations brought about the opposite of what they wanted. For first and foremost they wanted to understand the spiritual matter hidden in the secret of the images, and instead the imagination bore in them new images by the thousand, and beliefs came forth that are hard to reconcile with the opinions of the Torah and the Prophets.

The four-letter name of God, YHWH, which is also called the Shem ha-Meforash [the Explicit Name],{v1_12_03:1} they held holy and did not pronounce by its letters. From the death of Simeon the Just even the priests refrained from blessing with the Name. Whoever pronounces the Name has no share in the World to Come (Sanhedrin ch. 10), and only for a great purpose did they pronounce it (Avodah Zarah 18a); therefore they gave it various substitute names. The usual substitute is "the Holy One," and they added to it the phrase "blessed be He." The epithet "Holy" as a special substitute name is found in Scripture (Job 6:10; Habakkuk 3:3). The author of the book of Enoch uses this substitute name most often. The abstract name Shekhinah is a substitute name signifying the nearness of God to a place, a person or a nation. Therefore, insofar as He watches over, protects, or pours out His spirit upon something, He is called Shekhinah. Hence they translated "the eyes of the Lord" as "the face of the Shekhinah" (Isaiah 1:15), and the Temple was translated "the house of His Shekhinah" (ibid. 5:5); and the Shekhinah protects (ibid.), and it rests [upon them], and it went into exile with Israel, and people enter under its wings. Insofar as He is the master of strength and power, He is called Gevurah [the Might]: "the hand of the Lord" is translated "the might of the Lord"; and they said "the right hand of the Might," "the mouth of the Might." God is called Makom [the Place] because His existence is in every place (Philo); and the later sages of the midrash said: because the Holy One, blessed be He, is the place of the world (Genesis Rabbah 69), or because in every place where the righteous are, He is found with them (Pirkei de-Rabbi Eliezer 35). The early sages used this substitute name mostly when they spoke of the fulfilment of His will or of its opposite, as: "the will of the Place," "the anger of the Place," "they angered the Place," "asks for reward from before the Place," "punished before the Place," "the Place desires," "those known to the Place." Shamayim [Heaven] is also an old substitute name, and it is found with Antigonus of Sokho. It is usual when God is described in His kingship, whose yoke, fear and kingdom are upon man; so we find "the kingdom of Heaven," "the fear of Heaven," "the awe of Heaven." When they spoke of His goodness towards Israel, they call Him by the name "Father in Heaven"; and they lift their eyes to their Father in Heaven. The relation of God to Israel has been described from of old as the relation of a father to his son. And since God is called Lord, as against His people, who are called servants of the Lord, He is therefore described by the substitute name Ribbona Alma [Lord of the World] (Targum Jonathan, Isaiah 3:1), and in common speech Ribbono shel Olam. And when they spoke of God as the Former and Creator of all, they called Him by the epithet "He who spoke and the world came into being." These substitute names give a picture of their belief in a spiritual unity far from anthropomorphism, and they were founded on the belief in the creation of the world by the word of God, in His providence, His power and His goodness, and that He rules over all, and in reward and punishment. These beliefs are the roots of the substitute names mentioned, but in the course of time they became like proper names of God.

The creation of the world is called Ma'aseh Bereshit [the Work of Creation]. Inquiry into it was in their eyes a dangerous thing, and many sages refrained from entering into it (Ḥagigah 2:1), and they warned that one must not expound what is above and what is below, what is before and what is after. Nevertheless, midrashim on this subject have survived, and from them we learn their opinions on the creation of the world. They believed in the creation of something from nothing. A certain philosopher said to Rabban Gamaliel (the Second): "Your God is a great artist, but He found good materials" — namely tohu and bohu, darkness, wind, water and the deeps; and Rabban Gamaliel answered him with a rebuke and said to him: of all of them "creation" is said (Genesis Rabbah 1). Had one of the sages of Israel thought so, he would not have rebuked him so sharply; surely they believed from of old in creation of something from nothing. The images of the Scriptures they took literally, and from this arose astonishing opinions, and they thought that the world is built on a material foundation. The later sages said that the Foundation Stone was at the site of the Temple (Yoma ch. 5, Bavli and Yerushalmi, and Tosefta ch. 2). In the book of Enoch he calls the foundation "a corner stone," and Enoch reached the foundation and saw the corner stone (18:2). Light is a spark of the radiance of the splendor of the Holy One, blessed be He (Ben Sira 43:2), and this is not a poetic figure but words meant literally; and one of the masters of aggadah testifies that he received this from his predecessors and that it was handed to him in a whisper (Genesis Rabbah 3). For most natural phenomena they gave astonishing causes that have no natural relation to the effect. Examples of the lore of the Work of Creation of the early sages, the masters of the Mishnah, the Talmuds and the midrashim, are found in scattered places in the two Talmuds and in the books of midrash;{v1_12_04:1} but they are not many, because they were careful about expounding it and publishing it in public. From the remnants that are in our hands we understand that it is the inquiry into the creation of the world and of the things that exist, according to what they drew out of the section of the Work of Creation or out of verses in the rest of Scripture that speak of God's works in the nature of the creatures. The ancients had a complete book of this kind, called *Sefer Yetzirah* and also *Hilkhot Yetzirah*. We also have a *Sefer Yetzirah*, which is not the *Sefer Yetzirah* of the early masters of the Talmud (see *Dor Dor ve-Dorshav*, part 4, pp. 150, 218); but it too is a midrash of the Work of Creation. The later authorities disagreed on the matter. Maimonides said in his books that the matter of the Work of Creation is natural science, and most of the sages of Spain agreed with him; but Rabbenu Nissim said in his *Derashot* (sermon 1) that the Work of Creation is not natural science, for if it were, why did our sages command that it be concealed, so as not to expound it in public? Therefore he determined that besides the natural science that is acquired by the inquiry of the intellect, there is a natural science that is known only by divine influx, and that is the lore of the Work of Creation of the ancients (ibid., part 5, pp. 27, 139); and such was the opinion of the Kabbalists. Creation exists for the sake of the righteous man, etc. (Berakhot 6b; Genesis Rabbah and Tanḥuma; the book of Enoch; and 4 Ezra ch. 4); for the Holy One, blessed be He, wished His name to be magnified, and therefore He created the world (Yoma 38a), and this is fulfilled through those who do His will. The Torah was His instrument of craft in creation (Ḥagigah 12a; Genesis Rabbah 1), and it preceded the creation of the world (Pesaḥim 54a; Ben Sira ch. 1 and ch. 24); and wisdom is the Torah. Philo describes the supreme wisdom by the name "word" (logos), which was His instrument of craft in creation, and it is the spirit of the divine Torah (in the books *On Flight* 458 and *On the Cherubim* 129). The word, which is the supreme wisdom, was regarded by the Greek philosophers as a separate substance: God and the word are not one, but the word is like an emanation from the essence of God, and exists by itself in order to be the intermediary that carries out the will of God, to give life, order and motion in the material world. This is the doctrine of the logos at its root; but in the secondary notions the Greek sages differ from one another. Philo described the word as a distinct being, as though it were second to the God of the world; yet he is not like one who believes in two powers, but it is rather like a messenger carrying out his mission. Now the doctrine of the logos found those who honored it also among the Jews in the Land of Israel; but they did not enter into philosophical speculations, and in this too they relied on the holy writings. Behold, Solomon in his Proverbs describes wisdom as a being in its own right, and this wisdom is the Torah, for to them it alone is the true wisdom. So too Ben Sira describes wisdom (ch. 24): its seat is on high, it pours forth the spirit of prophecy, and it comes forth from the mouth of God Most High; and the source of wisdom is the Torah (ch. 1). From this it appears that wisdom is divine, and the word is wisdom, which is the Torah. Besides this, they regarded the divine word as a substance in its own right. From their saying that from every single word that goes forth from the mouth of the Holy One, blessed be He, an angel is created (Ḥagigah 14a), we learn that they believed in the substantiality of the word; and elsewhere they said that where the Lord spoke with Moses "in a vision," this is a vision of speech (Sifrei Be-ha'alotkha, pisqa 103). Had this belief not been old, the later sages would not have invented such a new thing out of their own hearts. The Aramaic translators too used the word *memra* in an abstract way, as though it were a being in its own right, for in truth such was the belief. From the time when they began to strive to remove anthropomorphism, this notion of the word grew stronger, and that time preceded their knowledge of Greek wisdom; for most of the emendations of the Scribes, known and unknown, are products of this striving, and they are very old. Yet the opinion about the word was no danger to the belief in unity, just as the belief in angels was not dangerous to unity. For we have already seen above that they believed that the Holy One, blessed be He, shows the revelation of His Shekhinah in sensory images, and this by a new creation at each time of need. Therefore all the acts that depend on accidental qualities and cause a change in the agent are performed by the angels whom the Lord created for this purpose. Of this kind are the angel of the presence, the savior, of the Second Isaiah, the destroying angel of the book of Samuel, and the angel of the covenant of Malachi. And it is very likely that the doctrine of angels, which spread in the exile and afterwards, was founded at its core on the striving to remove anthropomorphism in every possible way. Now the matter is likewise with regard to the word. For if the level of their understanding was not fit to grasp abstract and philosophical notions, their belief came to their aid: they believed that all the acts and qualities ascribed to God are His angels, who do His will, and in this category is also the word. For this reason the philosophical notion of the Alexandrians about the word found favor in the eyes of the sages of the Land of Israel, because it agreed with their belief. But they did not turn to the speculations and the consequences that were attached to it; it was enough for them to know that Greek wisdom agrees with their belief. Now as for the translators' use of the word *memra* in an abstract way, as though it were a being in its own right, this is not an opinion taken from the Greeks; it is an opinion native to the Jews, and the translators did not originate it in order to teach the people the removal of anthropomorphism. On the contrary: long before, the belief that God is not corporeal had taken root in the people, and in agreement with this Jewish belief the translators made their translations. However, there is a great difference between the notion of the word among the Jews in the Land of Israel and the notion of it among the Greeks. For to the Greeks the word was at the rank of divinity, and in Philo's opinion it is second to the God of the world and something like an intermediary between God and the material world; but the word among the Jews was a created thing, at the rank of an angel doing His will, and it is the divine wisdom, which in their opinion is the Torah; therefore the word is the Torah, and in this there is agreement between the Pharisees in the Land of Israel and Philo.

In the manner of creation there is a likeness between the images of Philo and those of the sages of the Land of Israel. In Philo's opinion the world was first created in thought, and according to this thought — which is the spiritual image formed in the soul of the thinker — He created the material world (in the book *On the Creation of the World* 3). Close to this is also the opinion of the sages of the Oral Torah, who compare creation to the building of a palace: as the craftsman looks at the plans he has, to know how he is to make the rooms, so the Holy One, blessed be He, looks into the Torah and builds the world (Genesis Rabbah 1; ⚠2 4). The root of this midrash is hinted at in the dispute of the House of Shammai and the House of Hillel: the House of Shammai say, the thought was at night and the deed by day, and the House of Hillel say, both by day (Genesis Rabbah 12); and according to both of them, thought came first. But on this matter there are only obscure hints, for the sages of the Land of Israel guarded themselves from entering into speculation on "what is before." With regard to time, the opinions of the Greeks are known: that time is eternal, or that it came into being with being and existence, without which the notion of time cannot be pictured, and that the six days of creation in the Torah are not to be taken literally (Philo); but for the sages of the Land of Israel time was one of the created things (4 Ezra 5:49), and the aggadot about the things that were created before the creation of the world point to this opinion, for these aggadot necessarily presuppose the existence of time. And one of the later sages said that there was an order of times before (Genesis Rabbah 3), and one must not take his words out of their plain meaning, as his commentators did, for we have seen that such was the prevailing opinion in the Land of Israel. The noble part of creation is man. They believed that for his sake the world was created, and that he was created in the image of God. And the expression "image of God" was not understood in a corporeal way. For the aggadah tells that the Seventy changed [the text] and wrote: "I will make man in an image and in a likeness" instead of "Let us make man in our image, after our likeness" (Mekhilta Bo 14 and in many places); from this it appears that "in our image, after our likeness" was understood only of the spiritual quality. Therefore the later sages said that there are people who are in His image and likeness and there are those who are not in His image and likeness (Genesis Rabbah 8); and their meaning is that image and likeness were said of the spiritual quality, and the righteous possess the image of God and the wicked do not. Therefore the image of God is a figure for the soul. And they believed that the soul is a spiritual being, existing by itself in a special place in heaven (Ḥagigah 12b); and when the body is formed in the mother's womb, the Holy One, blessed be He, puts a soul into it (Niddah 31a). In its first place it is pure (Berakhot 60b); in its joining with the body it becomes soiled (Shabbat 152b). Therefore it would have been better for man not to have been created than to have been created (Eruvin 13b), for by his creation the soul was turned away from its purity because of sin; therefore let a man take care to keep his soul pure (ibid.). This keeping lies in man's will, and to him the freedom is given to follow his good inclination or his evil one (Ben Sira 15:14; Sifrei Re'eh, pisqa 53; and in the Gemara in many places). The moral good that a man should choose is the fear of Heaven in general (Avot 1:3). Fear is the beginning of love (Ben Sira ch. 25), and the fruit of the fear of Heaven is the fear of sin (Berakhot 19a; Avot ch. 2, ch. 3, and in many places). The greatest of the merits is the study of Torah (Pe'ah 1:1; Ben Sira 6:36–38), and second to it are the Temple service and acts of loving-kindness; these three were counted as the pillars of the world (Avot ch. 1).

The value of Torah study is great if it leads to deeds (Avot ch. 1), and meditating on it is not enough; rather, keeping it is the main thing (Ben Sira 6:38). But the performance of the commandments is not acceptable when it is done without intention (Berakhot 2:1), and a man's deeds in general should be for the sake of Heaven (Avot ch. 2), whether eating and drinking (Beitzah 16a) or his other affairs (Tobit, in many places); and the condition of the deed is faith: faith that the Torah is from Heaven (Sanhedrin 10:1), faith in the faithful shepherd, who is Moses (Mekhilta Beshallaḥ 6), and in the prophets of the Lord (2 Chronicles 20). Whoever performs a commandment in faith, great is his reward (Mekhilta ibid.); and as a reward for faith in Him who commanded Moses to perform the signs and wonders, the Holy One, blessed be He, performs miracles and mighty deeds for them (Mekhilta de-Amalek 1; Rosh Hashanah 29a; ⚠3 16); by the merit of faith they were redeemed from Egypt (Mekhilta Beshallaḥ 3), and by its merit the exiles are gathered in (ibid. 6). The commandments were divided into two parts: commandments between man and God, and commandments between man and his fellow. The first principle of the first part is the acceptance of the yoke of the kingdom of Heaven (in many places), and in it are included the virtue of giving thanks both for the good and for the bad (Berakhot ch. 9); the justification of Heaven's judgment (Sifra, the opening of Millu'im; Ta'anit 8a); serving the Lord out of love (Sotah 24a;⚠4 Sifrei Va-etḥanan, pisqa 32), and awakening the love of all who come into the world for the Holy One, blessed be He (Sifrei ibid.); and the love must not be for the sake of receiving reward (ibid., pisqa 41), but, like the service of the Lord in general, it should be only for the sake of its own working (ibid., Ekev, pisqa 48), not on condition of receiving reward (Avot ch. 1). Love is carried into deed by cleaving to the ways of the Lord (Sifrei, pisqa 49) and by being like Him: as He is merciful and gracious, so shall man be (ibid., and Mekhilta de-Shirah 3; Yerushalmi Pe'ah ch. 1; and Shabbat 133b); and by keeping His commandments, without distinguishing between a light commandment and a grave one (Avot ch. 2; Sifrei Re'eh, pisqa 79 and 82). The greatest in this part of the commandments is the commandment of the sanctification of the Name: it is a man's duty to give up his life and his means to sanctify the Name (Sifrei Va-etḥanan, pisqa 32). The principle "Give yourself up and sanctify My Name!" (Sifra Emor 9) was held fast by the Jews at all times. But before the destruction it was kept with greater strictness than after it. Formerly they gave up their lives for every light transgression, but in the upper chamber of the house of Nitzah in Lydda they were somewhat more lenient. The commandments between man and his fellow are all the commandments of justice and uprightness, mercy and grace, and the foundation of them all is the love of man for man, which includes acts of loving-kindness, which are a pillar of the world. The root of this foundation is: "What is hateful to you, do not do to your fellow" (Tobit 4:16); this is the whole of the Torah, and all the rest is only like its commentary (Shabbat 31a); and they called this rule "a great principle in the Torah" (Sifra Kedoshim 4). One of the great merits is the giving of charity, and it was counted as part of acts of loving-kindness; and the two together are weighed against all the commandments (Yerushalmi Pe'ah ch. 1); and whoever does much loving-kindness is praiseworthy (the book of Tobit, in many places). It is a ransom for the sinning soul (Daniel 4:24), and its reward is in this world and in the World to Come (Pe'ah ch. 1), and it is one of the marks by which Israel are distinguished (Yevamot 79a). Acts of loving-kindness are for the living, healthy and sick, and also for the dead, to bury them according to their honor (Tobit, in many places; Mekhilta de-Amalek 2; and in the Gemara in many places). Belonging to love and to acts of loving-kindness is also the duty of rebuke, for they believed that all Israel are sureties for one another (Shevuot 39a). Like the Torah and acts of loving-kindness, the Temple service too is one of the pillars of the world. Therefore the early pietists longed to bring a sacrifice (Nedarim 10a), and Judah ben Bava brought a suspensive guilt-offering every day (Keritot 25a); and Rabban Yoḥanan ben Zakkai praises the generation whose prince brings an offering for his sin (Horayot 10a; Sifra). But they did not shut their eyes to the fact that this service is below the dignity of God and close to anthropomorphism. Therefore they said that its purpose is only to give good reward to those who perform it (Mekhilta Bo 15); and where it is said that the sacrifices are "a sweet savour," this is only a figure: that it is a satisfaction before Him that His will is done (Sifra in many places; Sifrei Pinḥas, pisqa 143). But a sacrifice without purity of thought does not reach its purpose (Ben Sira ch. 34; Judith ch. 16). The belief in the preciousness of the Temple service had taken root in the nation, and the enlightened, although they lessened its value relative to the Torah and acts of loving-kindness, nevertheless guarded themselves from speaking against it outright. On the contrary, they said expressly that one should not withdraw one's hand from it, for so God commanded (Ben Sira ch. 35); and where they condemn the sacrifices of the wicked (Ecclesiastes 4:17; Ben Sira 34:5), they do not condemn the service in general, but admit that it is acceptable (Ecclesiastes 5:3; Ben Sira 35:4). Included in the service of the sacrifices are the vow and the Nazirite vow. Although these too were not acceptable in the eyes of the enlightened (Ecclesiastes 5:4; Ben Sira 18:22; Nedarim 9a and the places cited there), they could not oppose them, since they had taken root in the people. Many practiced the Nazirite vow (Yerushalmi Berakhot), especially in a time of trouble (Nazir 5:1). Likewise fasting is a great merit (Tobit 11, and in the Mishnah and Talmud in many places), and in all the troubles of the community they decreed a fast (Tractate Ta'anit; 1 Maccabees 3:47; Judith 4:17). For every affliction of the soul was to them like the sacrifices and even dearer than they, since sufferings win favor more than sacrifices (Sifrei Va-etḥanan 39;⚠5 Mekhilta de-Ba-Ḥodesh 10), for sufferings scour the soul and atone (Ta'anit 21a), like death, which scours (Shevuot 8a) and atones (Sifrei Shelaḥ 112, Naso 4; Sanhedrin 43a). Yet although death atones, they were nevertheless accustomed to bring an offering to atone for the souls of the dead (2 Maccabees 12:58).⚠6

God watches over man, the choicest of creatures, and His eyes run to and fro through the whole earth (Zechariah 4; 2 Chronicles 15⚠7). Divine providence extends over all the details of deeds, and the events of the world, in general and in particular, are in the hand of God, and nothing is in the will of man except the fear of Heaven (*Antiquities* 13); and it is an ancient proverb: everything is in the hands of Heaven except the fear of Heaven. The saying "All is foreseen, and freedom is given" (Avot 3), which is attributed to R. Akiva, was not invented by him; it was a foundation of the faith of Israel from of old. They did not enter into speculations to reconcile the concept of foreseeing, or divine knowledge, with the concept of freedom, which contradict each other. Rather, both were established beliefs, and they did not question them. Therefore they believed that man has free will and that there is no decree of God upon him to do evil or to do good (Ben Sira 15:20); and they believed that the deeds of man are revealed before the Lord before they are done (ibid. ch. 23 and in many places); and they believed in divine decree (*Antiquities* 13) — in agreement with the Essenes — that a man does not hurt his finger below unless it is proclaimed concerning him above (Ḥullin 7b); and they believed that the inclination to evil was planted in the heart of the first man (4 Ezra 4:30), and this is the root of the aggadah about the filth that the serpent cast into Eve, which clings to human beings (Shabbat 146a); and they believed that Satan acts upon the will of man and incites him (1 Chronicles 21). Satan is also described so in the prophecy of Zechariah (3:1), and with a small departure from what is described in Job, where Satan acts as the messenger of the Lord. But the later [writers] describe Satan as inciting: he goes down and leads astray (Bava Batra 15a⚠8), as in Zechariah and Chronicles, and he goes up and accuses, as described in Job. Satan is the evil inclination, and he is the angel of death (ibid.); and this is founded on the belief that the sin of the first man, through the counsel of the serpent, brought death upon the generations. These beliefs — in providence, in divine knowledge, in the freedom of man, and in the inclination to sin — contradict each other, and yet they stood firm in the nation, for they were believers, sons of believers, and did not enter into speculations. The Lord who watches is the King of the world, and His kingship is called the kingdom of Heaven. He rules over the nations as over Israel, but upon Israel His name rests most of all (Sifre Va-etḥannan, sec. 31). To the peoples He allotted kings of flesh and blood, but upon Israel He set the yoke of the kingdom of Heaven (Ben Sira 17:13). This belief was strengthened by the Zealots, who led the revolt before the destruction, and who fixed it firmly (*Antiquities* 17; Avot de-Rabbi Natan ch. 20). All the peoples are His, but He has placed them under the charge of His angels, while over Israel He Himself rules and watches (*Book of Jubilees* ch. 15; Pirkei de-Rabbi Eliezer ch. 24). Out of His love for Israel He made known to the nations that He Himself guides them, so that they would treat them with honor (Mekhilta Be-shallaḥ ch. 1). To set apart the deity of the one Lord, as if He were only the God of the Jews and not the God of the nations, was not among the foundations of their faith; rather, they believed that the Lord saves all who come into the world, and Israel more, because of their righteousness and their faith (ibid. ch. 3). God glories in Israel because He too is the glory of their strength (ג"ו⚠9 ibid.). He makes them one unique entity in the world because they too made Him one unique entity in the world (Ḥagigah 3a; Ben Sira in many places; Psalms of Solomon⚠10 ch. 2, 3, 10). And therefore Israel are beloved and are called children of the Omnipresent (Avot 3 and in many places), and He deals with His children by the measure of mercy (Berakhot 7a) and inclines their judgment toward lovingkindness (Rosh Hashanah 17a). Even if the judgment has been decreed, it is in the power of man to annul it through charity, prayer and repentance (Rosh Hashanah 16b; Yerushalmi Ta'anit 2; Tobit 12); for one who does charity is like one who offers a sacrifice (Ben Sira 35), and it is reckoned as a ransom and redemption of the soul (Daniel 4:24). Likewise prayer is service of the heart and is esteemed like the service of the altar (Sifre Ekev, sec. 81⚠11), and the Holy One, blessed be He, longs for it (Yevamot 94a⚠12), when one prays in purity of heart and with gravity (Berakhot 5:1), and with mercy and supplication before the Omnipresent (Avot 2); and then the Holy One, blessed be He, annuls His decree because of the decree of the righteous man (Yerushalmi Ta'anit 3:12). Likewise repentance atones (Mekhilta Ba-ḥodesh ch. 6; Bavli and Yerushalmi Yoma ch. 8; and in many places), and the hand of the Holy One, blessed be He, is stretched out to receive penitents (Pesaḥim 119a), on condition that the repentance is complete; for one who says "I will repent and sin [again]" is like one who immerses with a creeping thing in his hand (Ben Sira 34; Yoma 86a).

The princes of the kingdom of Heaven are the ministering angels. The names of the angels came up with them from Babylon (Yerushalmi Rosh Hashanah 1:1), and therefore they are not mentioned by their names in the early Holy Scriptures, but only in the late ones (Daniel 8, 10). There they are called princes, and every kingdom of the kingdoms of the earth has a prince. Michael is the prince of Israel (ibid. 10:21), and he is one of the chief princes (ibid. 10:13). Seven princes of the Presence are mentioned (Tobit 12), corresponding to the seven princes of the Persians, who are called Amshaspands. The angels of the Presence are a special kind (*Book of Jubilees* ch. 2), and they are Metatron, prince of the Presence (Sanhedrin 38b), and Suriel, prince of the Presence (Berakhot 51a). There are also angels of holiness (*Book of Jubilees*, ibid.), and they are called holy ones (Daniel 4:14) and also watchers (Daniel; *Jubilees*, ibid.; and the *Book of Enoch*). Gabriel is distinguished by the name "holy one" (Daniel 8:13); he is counted in the company of Michael, Raphael (Bava Metzia 86b), Uriel, Suriel and Penuel (*Book of Enoch* ch. 9). The greatest of them all is Michael (Yoma 37a). Gabriel is also called Ruaḥ Pisqonit, and he has three names: Pisqon, Itmon, Sigron (Sanhedrin 44a). To all the phenomena of nature they gave princes, spirits and angels (*Book of Enoch* ch. 55). Rahab is the prince of the sea (Bava Batra 74b); Dumah is the angel of the spirits (ibid. 94b); Yurkemo is the prince of hail (Pesaḥim 118a); Lailah is the angel of conception (Niddah 15b). There were also angels who pray on behalf of man; one of them is Sandalfon (Ḥagigah 13b). Metatron writes the deeds of men in a book (ibid. 15a), and he is Enoch son of Jared, whom the Holy One, blessed be He, hid away (Yalkut Isaiah, sec. 315). For all this there are parallels in the *Book of Enoch* and *Jubilees*. Besides the princes appointed over natural matters there are seventy princes, one prince for each people, and angels and evil spirits without end. And they said: "Were the eye given leave to see, no creature could stand before the demons" (Berakhot 6a; Philo, in the book *On Dreams*, 636⚠13). Every derangement of the mind they ascribed to an evil spirit (*Antiquities* 6:8). Samael is the inciter and Satan (Sotah 10b). Azael, one of the angels who came down to earth, also incites to transgression (Yalkut, ibid. 44; Yoma 66b). One kind of the evil spirits are the demons. In their existence they believed with all their heart, so that they became the cause for the foundation of a number of laws and rules (Shabbat 2:5; Gittin 66a; Yevamot 122a; Sanhedrin 44a). They are also mentioned by name, such as Ashmedai, the prince of them all (Gittin 68a); Lilith, a demon that has wings (Niddah 24a) and is the prince of the night (Shabbat 152b); Hurmiz son of Lilith (Bava Batra 73a); and Ben Temalion (Me'ilah 17b). They said that there were three hundred kinds of demons in Shiḥin (Gittin 68b). One kind of evil spirits is called angels of destruction, and their number is great (Shabbat 88a; Pesaḥim 112b), and they too are designated by names. The mouth cannot tell the multitude of inventions that the imagination devised in these matters, and all of them found believers.

The angels reveal themselves to the righteous. Elijah the prophet, who is in the rank of an angel, goes about from city to city (Bava Kamma 60b), guards the ways of men, makes known the secret of the Lord to those who fear Him (Berakhot 3a), and resolves their doubts (Pesaḥim 70a). The righteousness of the righteous man can bring him to the holy spirit. Although the holy spirit ceased with the prophets (end of Sotah, Bavli and Yerushalmi), nevertheless they believed that the holy spirit was revealed by chance to individuals, or that they heard a heavenly voice (ibid. 33a; מ"ת⚠14 ch. 11; Sanhedrin 11a; and in many places). And since the righteous are near to God, they believed that they could do good to others through the prayer that they pray for them; and in every time of trouble they sent to the famous righteous men to pray on behalf of the people (Berakhot 34b; Ta'anit ch. 2, Bavli and Yerushalmi). Likewise they believed that it was in the power of the righteous to act by means of whispered charms. For we have seen that in the last years of the Temple this was a craft for some of them, who boasted of this power; and this was not a new invention, but such was the belief among the people, and therefore they opposed with all their strength those who whisper over a wound (Sanhedrin 10:1). Ben Dama, the son of R. Ishmael's sister, wanted to be healed by Jacob of Kefar Sekhaniah in the name of his master (Yerushalmi Avodah Zarah 2, and the Bavli there, and Tosefta Ḥullin 2). Without doubt the matter of the whispered charms was the use of the names of the Holy One, blessed be He, and adjurations, which they made in a whisper; and the charms are already hinted at in the early Holy Scriptures. They also believed that there was power to act by whispering the names of impurity (Sanhedrin 91a), and these are the names of the demons, and especially the name of Satan; whisperings by the name of Satan and sorcery are one and the same. The belief in sorcery is old in the nation. The women were given over to sorcery (Avot 2:5⚠15; Eruvin 64b), and in the days of Simeon ben Shetaḥ it was the sickness of the time (Sanhedrin 45b); and from ancient days sorcery was the craft of women, for the Torah itself speaks of a sorceress [Exodus 22:17]. The aggadah relates that the angels who came to the daughters of men taught them sorcery and adjurations (*Book of Enoch* 7:10, and Yalkut, sec. 44). Yet the practice of sorcery was an abomination in their eyes, and although they believed in its effect, nevertheless those who practised it were persecuted. But the adjurations and charms — to remove illnesses by a charm, and evil spirits by adjurations — even the enlightened did not oppose those who practised them, since they believed in them with complete faith. They ascribed the scrolls of adjurations and charms to King Solomon (*Antiquities* 8:2). All these were reckoned as channels that join the spiritual world with the material world. One special channel among them was the dream. The enlightened of the people endeavoured to teach that there is nothing in dreams and that they come only "through much business" (Ecclesiastes 5:2); visions of the night are vain images (ibid. 5:6; Ben Sira 34), and we should not fear them, but only fear God (ibid.). But they were of no avail against the belief of the people, which was very strong; and it was almost a special craft in Jerusalem to interpret dreams (Berakhot 55a). These opinions, which spread in those days, frightened them at every single step of their way, so that they were afraid to utter a bad word from their mouths, lest they bring Satan upon them (Berakhot 19a). This became a principle: "A man should not open his mouth to Satan." When they spoke of an evil that would come upon Israel, they said "upon the enemies of Israel," out of fear lest they arouse Satan by their speech. And they believed in omens for good or for evil (Berakhot 34b). For many generations more they could not separate themselves from divinations (Ḥullin 95b) and from signs and portents (Mekhilta Bo ch. 1; Sukkah 29a), but the greatest of the sages fought them with all their might (Shabbat 66a; Sanhedrin 65b; and Mekhilta, ibid.). It is indeed so that not all these opinions are rational and in agreement with the opinions of the Torah and the Prophets; but their source is pure, for all of them were hewn from the belief in divine providence. As one mother bears sons who differ from one another in appearance, in stature and in beauty, so from this belief came forth different offspring: some of them good and healthy, and some of them thin and of evil spirit; but in all of them a little of the spirit of their mother still clings.

The choicest offspring that came forth from the belief in providence is the belief in reward and punishment. Their belief was firm that everything that comes to a man, of good or of evil, in temporal life is the outcome of his deeds and their fruits (Pe'ah 1; Menaḥot 44a). Whether for good or for evil, the Holy One, blessed be He, repays measure for measure (Sotah 8b and in many places); but the measure of good is greater than the measure of punishment (Sanhedrin 100a and in many places; Ben Sira 35). If evil comes upon the righteous, it is for a trial or for atonement, and so that their reward may be complete in the world to come (Ben Sira 2; Tobit 2; Psalms of Solomon in many places; Ta'anit 11a; Bava Batra 15b; Horayot 10b); and He does good to the wicked man in this world in order to drive him out of the world to come (Bava Batra, ibid.; Sifre Ha'azinu, sec. 306). Sometimes the righteous die for the sin of the generation, so that they should not learn from the deeds of the generation (Psalms of Solomon ch. 4, and in the Gemara in many places), or to atone for their generation (Sifre Ve-zot ha-Berakhah, sec. 344), or because of the punishment that is ready to come (Mekhilta Mishpatim ch. 15). Good comes to Israel by the merit of the fathers, and the Holy One, blessed be He, remembers for the children the kindnesses of their fathers and the wickedness of their fathers. Ezekiel (18) already decided that this holds only when they hold the deeds of their fathers in their hands; and this was a principle for them at all times (Ben Sira in many places; Sanhedrin 27b). A reward is also appointed after death. When a man dies his soul does not perish; rather, it remains for eternal life. The souls of the righteous are placed in a treasury (Sifre Pinḥas 139; Ve-zot ha-Berakhah 344) and are stored away beneath the Throne of Glory (Ḥagigah 12b). When a man dies there are two ways before him, one to the Garden of Eden and one to Gehinnom (Berakhot 28b), and he gives account of his deeds before God (Avot 3). Of the nature of the reward and the punishment they spoke only by hint. In the book of 4 Ezra the matters are precise to a hair's breadth (ch. 6), and in a number of things there is agreement between it and the books of the Oral Torah. For example: the righteous are placed in a treasury where the eternal light is (Genesis Rabbah 3); the souls of the wicked are handed over to angels of destruction (Sifre Ve-zot ha-Berakhah 357), and these angels come to meet them and receive them, while the righteous are received by the ministering angels (Ketubbot 104a); the righteous enjoy the radiance of the Shekhinah (Berakhot 17a); the deeds of man, written in a book (Avot 3), are spelled out before him, and he is judged for them and acknowledges the justice of the judgment (Sifre Ha'azinu 306; Ta'anit 11a); and there are seven grades for the righteous in the Garden of Eden (Sifre Deuteronomy 10). For all these there are parallels in 4 Ezra. These images lived in the belief of the people, and the imagination invented a thousand like them; but the basis of the matter was agreed by all: that the soul does not wither after death. Ecclesiastes, with his doubts in the reckoning of the soul after death (9:10), decides that the spirit returns to God (12:7). In most of the Apocrypha the belief in the survival of the soul is mentioned, either by hint (Ben Sira 45:20 and 48:5, 15) or explicitly (Tobit 2:18; Psalms of Solomon in many places; 2 Maccabees ch. 11). The concept of the survival of the soul is described in terms of the storing away of the soul, because they believed that the souls are kept for the last day of judgment (Psalms of Solomon in many places; Enoch; Ezra; Jubilees; *Antiquities* 18; Sanhedrin 108a). The souls of the wicked are bound and kept in Sheol (Enoch, Ezra and Jubilees), and they have no permission to return among the living; but the righteous have permission to return (*Antiquities*, ibid.; Berakhot 18b), and therefore they called the righteous "living" even in their death (Yerushalmi, ibid. 2:8; Matthew 22:32). Of the belief in the transmigration of souls there is no hint in the books of the Oral Torah, but in other books of the ancient Jews hints are found. According to the testimony of Josephus (*Wars* 2:8), they believed that the souls of the righteous come into another body. 4 Ezra (ch. 6) asks whether the souls will be revealed in new bodies, and this is a sign that in the days of the writer they believed so. Also in the writings of the Evangelists (Matthew 14:2) there is some hint; but one cannot decide clearly that this belief was widespread among the people.

The chief reward and punishment is on the last day of judgment, before whose coming the Messiah will be revealed. The belief in the coming of the Messiah took root in the nation from of old (above, ch. 3). From the time that the Temple was destroyed the first time, it branched out further with the opinions of the people. As the troubles grew, the hope for redemption also grew stronger. In its root this hope was shared by the whole nation, but the description of its outward form was not shared by all. For the images of the character of the redeemer changed according to the political situation, according to the abundance or scarcity of their troubles, and according to the level of enlightenment. Just as the earlier prophets differed from the later ones in their images of the Messiah, so that the stamp of each time is seen impressed upon them, so it was in the days of the Second Temple. In the days of their going up from the exile their sun shone and salvation was near; then, when their heart still sought, they held fast⚠16 in their hope to the completeness of the redemption. Nehemiah hopes that a priest will stand up with Urim and Thummim (Ezra 3:66⚠17), and that the Lord will bring them to the place that He chose and gather the dispersed of Israel (Nehemiah 1:9). One of the prophets of the exile (Isaiah II), who prophesied in the days of Cyrus, hopes for a complete redemption and an everlasting salvation: for the ingathering of the exiles and for the payment of recompense to the enemies of the Lord, so that they may fear Him; the light of the Lord will shine upon Israel, and the nations will walk by its light, and all will see the glory of the Lord, and, like Israel, will bring an offering to His house in Jerusalem; and then the wolf and the lamb will feed together, and there will be no destroyer in all His holy mountain, and the wicked who turned away from the Lord will be judged and go to perdition, and they will be an abhorrence to all flesh. Haggai, Zechariah and Malachi also described the redemption in this way. At the end of the prophecies of Malachi a new matter is found: that the Lord sends them Elijah the prophet before the coming of the great day of the Lord [Malachi 3:23]. But he concealed from us what that great day is: is it the coming of the Messiah or the last day of judgment? Is he the herald of the coming of the Messiah, or is he himself the redeemer? But I have already shown above that these last verses in Malachi are not part of the book, but were appended to serve as the seal of the collection of the Torah and the Prophets; and that the name "Elijah the prophet" does not refer to the ancient Elijah, but refers to prophecy in general: that God will return and raise up a prophet for them as at first (see above, end of ch. 3, in the note). None of them speaks of the coming of the son of David. In the book of Baruch (chs. 4, 5) he describes the redemption as the ingathering of the exiles, temporal and moral happiness, and the subjugation of their enemies. So too it is described in the book of Tobit, and it adds further (ch. 13) that nations will gather to the Temple of the Lord in Jerusalem and serve the God of Israel, and forsake their idols and rejoice with Israel (ch. 14), and the kingdom of the Almighty will rise over them forever (end of ch. 13). From the writings of the Alexandrian Jews too (2 Maccabees 1:34–36 and 2:18, 19) these longings and hopes sparkle. Ben Sira (ch. 36) also speaks of the punishment of the nations, so that they may know the Lord as Israel His people, and he desires that the Lord raise up prophets for them as of old; and in all other matters his hopes do not deviate from the opinions of the prophets and writers mentioned. One cannot believe that it is only by chance that the establishing⚠18 of the kingdom of the house of David is not mentioned even in a single word; and likewise one cannot believe that they did not hope that the kingdom of David would return as of old. But their political situation forced them, in all those times, not to reveal the secrets of their heart, for the sake of the peace of the government. This is seen also in the writings of Josephus. Is not the subject of all his books the history of the Jews, their religion and their faith? Yet there is not a word in his speech about their hope for the coming of the Messiah and for the restoration of the kingdom of the house of David. This can only be because of his wish to keep the peace with the Romans, for whom especially his books were intended. In the book of Daniel the teaching of the Messiah is found more explicit and more extended than in all the late writings that were written up to the end of the days of the Maccabees. This book was attributed to Daniel, who was one of the exiles and famous for his righteousness (Ezekiel 14) and became a byword for his wisdom (ibid. 28); a man wise and righteous like him was the fitting man to whom the prophecies written in the book could be attributed. But in truth it was composed in the days of the persecution of the Jews by Antiochus Epiphanes; and therefore all the events in the days of the Persian rule are coloured with legendary images, and some of them are contradicted by the facts, whereas all its reports of the events in the days of the Greek kings agree with what happened to a hair. Speaking in all innocence, the book itself testifies to its own time, when it says that he was commanded to shut up the vision for many days (8:26⚠19) and to seal the book until the time of the end (12:4). Is this not as though it excuses itself for why it was not known in former times, through all the days of the Persians and the first Greek kings, and did not come to light until the time of the end, and says that so Daniel was commanded? Indeed, for us today this is clear testimony that the beginning of its coming to light, which was in the days of the persecutions, is also the time of its composition.{v1_12_11:1} And it is clear that there was no memory of these prophecies before that time; for otherwise, why did Ben Sira not mention Daniel among the men of wonder and the prophets, to all of whom he gave a memorial? Now if we consider the book where it speaks of the Messiah, we find that in the essence of the matter it did not depart from the opinions in the other writings of that time; only it describes explicitly what others said only by hint. The first image it gives is in the dream of Nebuchadnezzar. In his dream, behold, a great image: its head of gold, its breast and arms of silver, its belly and thighs of bronze, its legs of iron, and the feet partly of iron and partly of clay. Then a stone was cut out not by hands, and it smashed the feet of the image, and the stone became a great mountain that filled the whole earth. These parts of the image are four kingdoms (Babylon, Media, Persia, Greece), and the stone that smashes the fourth kingdom is the kingdom of Israel. At that time the God of heaven will set up a kingdom that shall never be destroyed, nor shall the sovereignty be left to another people; it shall break in pieces and consume all these kingdoms, and it shall stand forever (2:44, 45). And Daniel sees in a dream of the night four beasts coming up from the sea, the fourth very terrible, and its mouth speaking great things; and the Ancient of Days sits upon a throne, and the fourth beast is given over to destruction in a stream of fire, and from the other beasts too the kingdom is taken away. And behold, with the clouds of heaven there came one like a son of man; he reached the Ancient of Days, and to him were given dominion and glory and kingdom, and all the nations and tongues shall bow down to him; his dominion is an everlasting dominion that shall not pass away, and his kingdom shall not be destroyed (4:13, 14⚠20). And Daniel receives the interpretation from the angel: the four beasts are four kingdoms (Babylon, Media, Persia, Greece), all of which will perish; and the one like a son of man is the people of the saints of the Most High, to whom the kingdom under all the heavens is given, which will be an everlasting kingdom, and all the rulers shall obey them and bow down to them (7:26). Now just as the beasts are images of four kingdoms, so the likeness of a son of man is an image of Israel; and therefore "son of man" does not refer to a Messiah, but to the kingdom of Israel, and that is the kingdom of the Messiah. In these two visions it is not said who will be the means of attaining world dominion; it is only said that the Lord will set up the kingdom, and not through whom. But in the prophecies in the Hebrew part of the book the end of the four kingdoms and the history of the fourth are told in great detail, and with wonderful art every point of the annals of Antiochus the wicked is described in its bearing on the Jews. And there shall be a time of trouble for Israel such as never was, and at that time Michael the great prince shall stand up for Israel, and at that time they shall be delivered according to all{v1_12_12:1} that is found written in the book (12:1). This refers to the fulfilment of the prophecies written in the Aramaic part, which he called "the book": that God will set up the kingdom of Israel over all the earth, and after He sets this up, then the resurrection of the dead will take place. From this it is seen that the images of the kingdom of the Messiah in the book of Daniel are the images that all the writers of his time held: the eternity of the nation, the spreading of its kingdom over all the peoples, and its endurance for ever and ever. Who will be the king in this kingdom? Of this the book is silent. Perhaps the matter was understood of itself: if we speak of an enduring kingdom in Israel, its king will be of the house of David. Therefore in the days of the book of Daniel the teaching of the Messiah did not reach a new stage; but those who studied the book and its expounders who came after it gave its words an intention other than what the writer intended, and this gave birth to new opinions on the matter of the Messiah and his kingdom.

Those who expounded the prophecies of the book took the parable for the thing it stood for, and there came out a picture of the Messiah that the prophets had not imagined: they thought that the Messiah is a son of man who will be revealed from among the clouds, as in the parable of the book, and they gave him a nature close to the divine. Fourth Ezra (ch. 13), in his visions, invents visions whose source, as all who see them will recognize, is the prophecies of Daniel; but he erred in their sense and took the parable for the thing signified, and the Messiah appeared to him as a heavenly being whom God had hidden for a long time (13:26), and he calls him son of God (ibid. 32). And the Book of Enoch (ch. 48 and in many places) calls him son of man, as in Daniel's parable, who sits in heaven and was named by name before the Ancient of Days, and who existed before the creation of the world. And although there is clear evidence that the hands of other editors have touched these two books, it is nevertheless clear that the mistaken sense given to the book of Daniel gave birth to all the strange views in the beliefs about the Messiah, all that is written in the two books mentioned; and these were planted among the Jews, and many even of the sages, early and late, could not part from them. They believed that the name of the Messiah was created before the world was created (Pesaḥim 54a), and that he is hidden and concealed because of the sins of Israel (Targum Jonathan Micah 4:8); and in the opinion of many he will be from the dead, and David himself will be the Messiah (Yerushalmi Berakhot ch. 2; Sanhedrin 98b); and he will be revealed and concealed and revealed again, and whoever believes in him will live, and whoever does not believe goes over to the nations of the world and is killed by them (Midrash Song of Songs and Ruth); and he suffers in every generation according to the sins of the generation (Pesikta Rabbati); the holy spirit will rest on him (Targum Jonathan Isaiah 42:2⚠21), and the radiance of holiness shines from the radiance of his face (ibid. 53:2); he will pray for mercy on Israel and by his merit their sins will be atoned, and he will deliver the wicked to Gehinnom (ibid. 53:4, 19⚠22); and he will build the Temple that was profaned through sins (ibid. 5), not by human hands but by the hands of Heaven (Mekhilta Shirah ch. 10; Ketubbot 5a; Ezra 13:36); and he will be sharp toward the nations (Sifre Deuteronomy piska 1), and will accuse them before the Holy One, blessed be He, and will judge them and Samael (Pesikta Rabbati; Ezra ibid.; Enoch ch. 54); but he is gentle toward Israel (Sifre ibid.), and will gather the dispersed, even the Ten Tribes, who were exiled beyond the Sambatyon (Ezra 13:40; Sanhedrin ch. 11), and the waters of the Euphrates will be cut off so that they can return (Ezra ibid. 47); and for those sunk in Riblah the Holy One, blessed be He, makes tunnels, and they go as far as the Mount of Olives, and it splits and they come up out of it (Pesikta Rabbati; and see Yerushalmi Sanhedrin end of ch. 10). And not only his nature and his deeds, but also the name of the Messiah they expounded (Sanhedrin 98b and in many places). Such expounding was already customary among the ancients. And the name Bar Kokhba was perhaps also a title for the Messiah, because they took the Torah's saying "there shall step forth a star out of Jacob" [Numbers 24:17] as a hint at the Messiah (see above, ch. 3); and there is found in the Gospel a story that wise men of the East came to Jerusalem to bow down before their Messiah because they had seen his star in the east, and this shows that a star was a figure for the Messiah at that time. And perhaps for this reason he was called in Babylonia Bar Nafle (Sanhedrin 96b), for nafle in Aramaic is a term for a star (R. Naḥum Brüll in the journal *Beit ha-Midrash*, 148). The pen cannot write all the inventions that the imagination devised to portray the Messiah and the conditions of his coming and his office (Pesikta Rabbati piska 35, 36 and in many places), mixing together husk and kernel, the possible and the impossible. Let us not think that these are late inventions, for their roots agree with the pictures of the books of Ezra and Enoch, which were composed in the days of the Temple. But although these views found believers among the common people and among some of the sages, they were nevertheless not principles of faith; they were only secondary pictures and offspring of aggadic midrashim, according to what they understood, or rather distorted, in the Scriptures, putting into them things that the prophets never thought of and that never came into their minds. Such expositions were like the degenerate shoots of the vine [Jeremiah 2:21] in the vineyard of Israel; they were not the views of the nation as a whole nor agreed beliefs. And just as even in our time, which is a time of enlightenment, there are still simpletons who believe every word [Proverbs 14:15], and yet we do not say on that account that their foolishness is the general, ruling belief, so it was in those days: even if many abounded in the children of vanity in the matter of the pictures of the Messiah, we do not say that their vanities were the general belief; for the enlightened did not abound in these, and their belief in the Messiah was pure of dross, and in the essence of the matter they did not stray far from the prophecies of the prophets and from the words of the sages down to their time. Now the principal beliefs were these: that close to the revelation of the Messiah troubles come, which are called the birth pangs of the Messiah (Shabbat 118a and in many places); and it would be, for example: "In the footsteps of the Messiah insolence will increase" (Sotah 49b). The insolence will show especially in that the youth will behave insolently against the elder and the base against the honorable [Isaiah 3:5], the son dishonors the father, the daughter rises up against her mother, the daughter-in-law against her mother-in-law, a man's enemies are the men of his own house [Micah 7:6], and the ways of morals are corrupted (ibid. and Sanhedrin 97a). The period of preparation is seven years. The first three are evil and full of distress, and the Torah is forgotten by those who study it. In the fourth, plenty and not plenty; in the fifth, great plenty and joy, and the Torah returns to those who study it;{v1_12_13:1} in the sixth, voices; and in the seventh, wars (all this ibid.). And it is not the office of the Messiah to innovate anything in matters of religion (Eduyot 8:6⚠23), and the Torah is found with all Israel; they study it and do not forget it (Yalkut Exodus 273). After the years of preparation, the time has come for the kingdom of the nations to end and the time for the kingdom of Heaven to be revealed (Midrash Song of Songs on "the fig tree"), and then the redeemer will come. Although they believed that the Holy One, blessed be He, is the redeemer, and that the redeemed are the redeemed of the Lord, not the redeemed of the King Messiah (Midrash Psalms 107), and they believed from of old that the gathering of the exiles and the redemption would be completed and after that the Messiah would be revealed (Megillah 17b; Yerushalmi Berakhot 2:4), in accordance with the sense of the words of the prophets, they nevertheless gave the redemption the name "the coming of the son of David," and the Messiah is called "redeemer," whom God brings (the Eighteen Benedictions). This redeemer will be of the root of David, and his birthplace is Bethlehem of Judah (Targum Jonathan Micah 5; Yerushalmi Berakhot ch. 2); his office is to lead people to repentance toward the Lord (Song of Songs Rabbah on "your neck"), for the redemption depends on repentance (Sanhedrin 97b). He will bring many back to the Torah and pray for the sins of Israel (Targum Jonathan Isaiah 53:11), and the good God will forgive (ibid. 5); all idolatry will be uprooted, and the Lord will be alone in the world and His kingdom forever and ever (Mekhilta de-Amalek ch. 1); and this kingdom is guided by the Messiah, and there is no end to all the material and moral good, according to all the promises of the prophets (Targum Jonathan Isaiah 11, and in the Gemara and the midrashim in many places). These refined beliefs found their place in ancient prayers. Whoever considers the Eighteen Benedictions, to know what they asked for, will also understand what their hope was and recognize their picture of the redemption. Therefore, if we see in them that all their request was the attainment of understanding, repentance, purification from sin, deliverance from trouble, temporal good, the gathering of the exiles, the restoration of their political state, the subduing of the wicked, the destruction of wickedness from the earth, the reward of the righteous, the raising of the horn of Jerusalem, and the restoration of the kingdom of the house of David — is not the picture of the things that are fit to found and establish the happiness of the nation laid down in this request? From this we can judge the general belief, clean of any admixture of strange views. But the nature of the Messiah and all the particular secondary pictures were for them among the things hidden from human beings (Mekhilta Va-yassa ch. 4 and 5; Pesaḥim 54b).

Just as the enlightened thought it wrong to expound on the "how," so they thought it wrong to expound on the "when"; for the end, the time of the Messiah's revelation, was also among the things hidden from human beings (Mekhilta and Gemara ibid.). But if, all the same, at the end of the days of the Temple the calculation of the end was a matter with which the sages occupied themselves, they did this only to revive the heart of the crushed and to give them hope that salvation was near to come. The prophecy in the book of Daniel they interpreted as referring to their own time, and they expounded the saying "a time, times, and half a time" [Daniel 7:25] as referring to the last period of the days of the Temple (Sanhedrin 97a, and Rashi there). But for the honor of the sages of the Oral Torah we will say that they intended only to strengthen the hope of the people and rested their consolations on the words of the book; it is impossible that they thought that this was the meaning of the book, for whoever considers it with a settled mind will see that the book speaks of the end of the reign of Antiochus Epiphanes. Now this calculation proved false. The time passed, the calculation ran out, and the hoped-for Messiah did not come; instead came double trouble. Yet the gates of hope were not locked. They quickly found a new opening: R. Akiva came in his time, expounded the Scriptures and made a calculation suited to his time (97 ibid.). And as the troubles multiplied, so the calculations multiplied, and none of them proved true, until the later sages returned to the principal belief: that the end is hidden from the eye of every living thing, and the Lord knows. And they saw reason to hold to this with even greater force, since they saw that many calculators of the end, when they saw that their calculation did not come to pass, despaired and said: "Since the end has arrived and he has not come, he will no longer come." Therefore all people of settled mind loathed it⚠24 and took upon themselves not to make calculations, for the Messiah comes only when the mind is diverted (ibid. 97a). Likewise there was no agreed belief about the measure of the time of the days of the Messiah; there were various views on this, all of them results of their midrash or children of the imagination (Sanhedrin 99a and in many places, and in the books of Ezra and Enoch). And now we see that the belief in the redemption shared by the whole nation was only in the principal foundations and not in the secondary matters. R. Akiva saw that Bar Kokhba was doing valiantly against the Roman legions; therefore he held him to be the Messiah. And have we heard that he examined whether the secondary matters that were ascribed to the Messiah were fulfilled in him? Everyone knows that he was like any other man: he did not come down from heaven and did not trace his descent to the house of David; and nevertheless R. Akiva and his generation held him to be the Messiah. Some time later a sage of the Pharisees named R. Hillel arose and said: "There is no Messiah for Israel" (Sanhedrin 98b). He certainly did not deny the redemption. Perhaps he even admitted that when it is the will of the Lord to redeem Israel, the hand of one of the men of high rank will be in the midst, who with the help of God will work salvation for His people; but he denied the secondary attributes that were attached to the time of redemption, together with a human redeemer who stands above nature, and this is what he meant in saying "There is no Messiah for Israel." And one of the sages of the aggadah said that those who return in the gathering of the exiles will be the redeemed of the Lord, not the redeemed of Elijah nor the redeemed of the King Messiah (Midrash Psalms 107); from this saying too the idea flashes forth not to ascribe the redemption to the Messiah: rather God will redeem His people and raise up His kingdom, and it is the kingdom of Heaven, and in this kingdom a king will reign, and he is called the King Messiah. And one of the sages of the Talmud said: "There is no difference between this world and the days of the Messiah except subjugation to the kingdoms alone" (Berakhot 34b). How could he say so if he had believed in all the strange inventions that were attached to the King Messiah and to his kingdom? Indeed, from the time when those secondary views became the work of man and harmed the faith of Israel, it was a need of the hour to teach the opposite and to strip from the belief in the Messiah the husk that the imagination had attached to it. And if the attributes that we consider the husk of the kernel had been a principle of their faith from ancient days, how would all these sages have dared to deny what their predecessors held as a principle of faith? But so it is: the principal foundations of the belief in the Messiah were shared by all, but in the secondary matters each one had his own teaching in his hand.

After the kingdom of the Messiah is revealed there will be the resurrection of the dead. Both the righteous and the wicked will stand up at the resurrection, these to everlasting life and those to reproaches and everlasting abhorrence (Daniel 12:2; Sanhedrin 108a); and both Israel and the nations of the world have their share in the resurrection, except the generation of the Flood, the generation of the Dispersion and the people of Sodom (ibid. ch. 10), and their pious ones have a share in the world to come. The book of Ezra assigns to the nations of the world a terrible portion in the world of the resurrection (ch. 7). The resurrection comes together with the great day of judgment, when God will bring into judgment all the creatures of the worlds. The present world will pass away and wear out, and the Holy One, blessed be He, will create a new world (Sanhedrin 92b). These are the principal beliefs about the world of the resurrection and the day of judgment, which will come after the days of the Messiah; on them there was agreement among all the Pharisees, and they are compressed into short phrases: the day of Gog and Magog, the birth pangs of the Messiah, the day of consolation, the kingdom of the house of David, the days of the Messiah, the time to come, the resurrection of the dead, the depth of judgment, the great day, the world to come, the new world (Mekhilta Va-yassa ch. 4 and 5, and in the Gemara and the Midrash in many places). But these two principles too, the resurrection of the dead and the great day of judgment, were a matter with which the spirit of the expounders occupied itself in all generations; they devised devices in the Holy Scriptures to stand in the counsel of the Lord, and from every fine and small hint they drew out great and wonderful things and produced mountains hanging by a hair. The lofty idea of reward and punishment in the world to come was profaned by material pictures. There came forth the aggadah of the Leviathan whom the Lord killed and salted for the righteous in the time to come; of the beasts on a thousand hills [Psalms 50:10], which are also kept for the righteous (Bava Batra 74b); of the wine kept in its grapes since the six days of creation (Berakhot 34b); of the dew with which the Holy One, blessed be He, will revive the dead (Ḥagigah 12b; Yerushalmi Berakhot ch. 5); of the women who will give birth every day, and the trees that will bring forth fruit every day, and of the cakes and garments of fine wool that the Land of Israel will bring forth (Shabbat 30b); that the righteous will rise in their clothes (Ketubbot 111b); in what manner man will be formed in the world to come and which part of the body will be renewed first (Genesis Rabbah ch. 14); of the Jerusalem of the world to come (Bava Batra 75b and in many places); and hundreds of such aggadot, all of which were flying in the air at that time. And although most of them became known only from the mouths of the later sages, they were nevertheless ancient, and their counterparts are in the books of Fourth Ezra and Enoch; but they were not among the principles of their faith. Rather, the custom of expounding every slight hint in the Scriptures gave birth to them. We can only lament that these views did harm from the day of their birth through many generations to come, and darkened the pure beliefs in Israel. But so it was from of old and so it will be forever: the greater the worth and the preciousness of some man or thing, so much the more, in proportion, will various aggadot attach to it in the course of time. Therefore we will also not wonder when we see that the very principle of the belief in the resurrection of the dead became for the aggadah a beloved and dear matter to delight in. How great the worth of the belief in the resurrection of the dead was for them, we can estimate if we consider their great efforts to uphold and preserve it. Already in the days of the first Scribes they strove to make it known and enacted that it should have a mention in the text of the prayer. They also enacted that all who concluded benedictions in the Temple should say "from everlasting to everlasting" (Berakhot ch. 9), deliberately against those who denied this principle. For already the ancient Kutim [Samaritans] did not believe in the resurrection of the dead. After that came the Sadducees and denied it, casting doubt on the antiquity of the tradition and thinking that this view had not grown in the field of the religion of the Torah, but that they had brought it up with them from Babylonia, since among the Persians the belief in the resurrection of the dead was rooted. And they paid no attention to the proofs from Scripture that the Pharisees brought them (Sanhedrin 90b).{v1_12_16:1} The principle of the belief in the resurrection of the dead was almost of equal weight with the principle of the belief in Torah from Heaven, for of both they said that whoever denies them has no share in the world to come. These beliefs and views struck root in the vineyard of Israel and became a mighty tree that bore branches and made fruit [Ezekiel 17:23]; the Torah of Moses and the prophets was its soil, and by the springs of wisdom it sent out its roots; and the sages of every generation guarded it and raised it up, and it prospered, multiplying branches and lengthening its boughs. Time after time they grafted foreign shoots onto it, but through the wisdom of its keepers the foreign shoots changed their nature and became like all the boughs of the tree, resembling them in their beauty, until these and those alike were holy, and all who saw them recognized that they are the seed that the Lord has blessed [Isaiah 61:9]. For the wise of our nation, the heads of the people and its leaders, who were called to keep the way of the tree of life [Genesis 3:24], the Torah and the religion of Israel, were not grudging toward the views and the laws of other peoples; rather, whatever was right in their eyes and fit to add learning and moral instruction and to increase knowledge, they chose and brought near, and they accepted the truth from whoever said it; and the certainty of these words of truth [Proverbs 22:21] was holy to them as if its root were in their own soil, and they strove with all their might to show that these truths were founded in the Torah of the Jews and were given to them from their own. But when a man rose up from among them, or even sects, who devised devices to plant a stranger's slips [Isaiah 17:10] in the vineyard of their Torah, which had neither the fragrance of Lebanon nor the glory of Sinai — as indeed happened at various times — then they too stood firm for our people and gave their lives for their Torah, and with doubled strength the faithful keepers guarded their vineyard and were a shield for the Torah, and did not let it be corrupted or cease from its purity. And if many say to themselves: "See, the tree is dead, its stock is dead in the ground [Job 14:8]; is it not far better for us to exchange the stock of the tree and plant it a choice vine, a faithful planting, that will bud and blossom and bear fruit, whose glory will be greater than the first, and that will live forever?" — then they too will answer and say to them: "In vain do you murmur against the Torah of the living God, given as an everlasting statute, whose foundations will never be moved. See, this tree that you thought dead, its stock in the ground, is still alive and will live forever; it has not lost its strength and its scent is not changed [Jeremiah 48:11]; as on the day it was planted it flourishes and will not cease from yielding fruit [Jeremiah 17:8]; and as then, so now too it will bud and blossom and give its fruit for praise and for glory in the earth."
