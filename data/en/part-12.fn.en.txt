v1_06_12:1) In *Darkhei ha-Mishnah*, p. 32, he explains this halakhah: the people of Yose's generation wanted to be stringent with one who touches a corpse, a "father of fathers" of impurity, [treating him] like the corpse itself, so that he would be masav (for in his view the source of impurity, such as a corpse, a zav, etc., is called masav); and on this Yose testified dikrav be-mita mistaav, that is, mistaav but not masav (in his view mistaav indicates a lower degree than masav), for he is at a lower degree than the masav, that is, the corpse, and becomes only a "father" of impurity. Thus far his words. But this needs proof that the reading "mistaav" is the original one; for in the Gemara the reading is masav, and so was the reading in the Mishnah before Maimonides, and R. Obadiah of Bertinoro followed him, and so is the reading of the Tosafot in Nazir (42a). And even if it were so, the explanation in itself is open to doubt. For his intention is evidently that the Ḥasidim wanted to decree that one who touches one who touched should be impure like one who touches the corpse itself, and Yose came and testified that it is not so: one who touches a corpse is not masav but mistaav, that is, he is not like the corpse itself, and therefore one who touches one who touched is not like one who touches a corpse. But these are exactly the words of the Gemara, which says: "They decreed dikrav be-dikrav," that is, one who touches one who touched shall be impure like one who touches a corpse, that is, with seven-day impurity; "and he came and said: dikrav is impure, but dikrav be-dikrav is not impure" with seven-day impurity, except when they are joined. And Rava in the Gemara refuted this explanation. Therefore I have withdrawn my hand and taken hold of the explanation of Rav Naḥman, which Rava brought in his name. One should also remark on the incident that R. Judah told there, that Yose ben Yoezer set up beams for them and said: "Up to here is the public domain, up to here the private domain." We cannot decide that this incident has any relation to the halakhah of Yose, but this much is clear: that this incident prompted Rav Naḥman to his explanation.

v1_06_13:1) See the book *Urschrift* of R. A. Geiger, p. 116 and on, who wrote that the word eshkol is compounded of ish kol, as Rav said in the Bavli, and that it is the exact translation of the Greek word katholikos; and if so, it is right to call the leaders of the community, who are appointed over all matters of administration, eshkolot. And this is what they said in Yerushalmi Shekalim 5:3: "There were two katholikin." This is the first office after the Nasi and the high priest. But what he wrote there, that they are the Pairs, still needs proof; for before Yose no Pairs are mentioned, and after him there were no leaders for all matters, but only in matters of religion and Torah. In a baraita in Yerushalmi Sotah it is taught: "All the Pairs who arose from the death of Moses until Yose ben Yoezer arose, one can find fault in them; until Judah ben Bava arose, one can find fault in them." And on this the rabbi relied [to say] that eshkolot are the same as Pairs. But the baraita is corrupt from beginning to end and truncated, and one cannot rely on it. The main reading is as it is in Tosefta Bava Kamma ch. 8, and there they read "all the eshkolot," and they also read "one cannot find fault in them"; but the Tosefta too is corrupt, in that it reads "from the death of Yose" etc., and what was [said] "from the death" is missing there, and one must correct it according to the Yerushalmi, that is: "from the death of Yose one can find fault in them." Thus from the one and from the other the correct reading is supported, and so without doubt was the reading before the Bavli (Temurah 15b). And see vol. 3⚠10, ch. 4, p. 34, what I conjectured on the explanation of the word eshkolot.

v1_06_15:1) The whole of Psalm 118 fits very well the time of the victory of the Maccabees. And the baraita that says "in the following year they fixed them and made them festival days with hallel and thanksgiving" refers to the two parts of the hallel: the first part begins and ends with halleluyah, and the second part is the one that begins and ends with hodu. This baraita is very old, and whoever composed the praise that is said on Ḥanukkah, "In the days of Mattathias" etc., already had the baraita before his eyes, for there it says: "and they established these eight days of Ḥanukkah to give thanks and to praise Your great name." This corresponds to the words of the baraita, "they fixed them and made them festival days with hallel and thanksgiving." But the wording of the baraita is more exact: first hallel and afterwards thanksgiving, in the order of the two parts of the hallel. The composer of this prayer was not exact in this, and perhaps he did not understand that the baraita said "with hallel and thanksgiving" deliberately, to hint at the two parts of the hallel.

v1_06_16:1) Although according to the reading before us in the Mishnah the latter clause, "even if the king greets him etc.," does not refer back to the first clause but is a separate saying, nevertheless it seems to me that this saying in the latter clause originally came to tell and to teach the character of the Ḥasidim, as I have rendered it in the text; but the author of the Mishnah gave this saying the form of a halakhah, whereas originally and first of all it had the form of a narrative. And in the substance of the matter there is great cause for astonishment here. Certainly it is not surprising of the early Ḥasidim that they would not interrupt even where they were in mortal danger, for indeed that was the character of the Ḥasidim; but it is surprising of the later ones that they fixed it as halakhah, while we see that everywhere they would set aside even the Sabbath for a doubtful danger to life, and for this very reason they opposed the Ḥasidim in the matter of killing snakes and scorpions on the Sabbath. True, Rav Joseph and Rav Sheshet (Berakhot 33a) noticed this, but their words there do not follow the intent of the plain sense of the Mishnah. And from the story of a certain Ḥasid it appears that it was the way of the Ḥasidim not to interrupt under any circumstance, and this is a proof of my words.

v1_06_18:1) R. Ishmael did not expound the doubled forms (Yerushalmi Nedarim 1:1, Sotah 7:5, and in many places), nor did he expound the rule of extension (Shevu'ot 26b and in many places). On the matter of the doubled forms see Tosafot on Sotah (24a), s.v. R. Yonatan, and on Menaḥot (17b), s.v. Mai. This rule of midrash developed in several directions. a) That the doubling indicates that the action is done again at another time besides the present time, without delay between them; and this is the force of the action with respect to its continuance in time. Of this kind is the midrash of the early Elders: maḥoh timḥeh⚠11 — for the world to come. So too hikkaret tikkaret — in the world to come (Sanhedrin 64b; Sifre Shelaḥ 112); pakod tifkod — in the world to come (Mekhilta Beshallaḥ ch. 9⚠12). b) Close to this: ra'oh tir'eh — "you will see what I will do" (Berakhot 31b); ga'oh ga'ah — He is destined to be exalted (Mekhilta Beshallaḥ, Parashat ha-Shirah ch. 2); im bo yavo — a second coming (Sifra Metzora ch. 14); pasoh tifseh — to include the eighth, ninth and tenth [days] (ibid., Tazria ch. 4); amen amen — for the past and for the future (Sotah 18b). c) That the doubling indicates an action which, if it is done, will in the end bring about another like it, whether for reward or for punishment: im shamo'a tishma — if a man has heard, etc., he is made to hear, etc. (Mekhilta Vayassa ch. 1); and im ha'lem ya'limu — etc., in the end, etc. (Sifra Kedoshim ch. 10). d) To indicate an action that is to be done even several times until the purpose is attained: as hokhe'aḥ tokhi'aḥ — even a hundred times (Bava Metzia 31a); ve-rappo yerappe — even four and five times (Mekhilta Mishpatim ch. 6); abbed te'abbedun — even ten times (Avodah Zarah 45b); shalle'aḥ teshallaḥ — even four and five times (Ḥullin 141a); and im paro'aḥ tifraḥ — even if it returns, etc. (Sifra Tazria ch. 2). e) To indicate that the action is done twice: as im amor yomar — until he says it and repeats it (Kiddushin 22a); im anneh te'anneh — until he afflicts and repeats (Mekhilta Mishpatim ch. 18). f) Extension to all modes of doing, and of this kind are most of the doubled forms: as hakkeh takkeh — in any case (Sanhedrin 45b); ḥavol taḥbol, hashev tashiv, naton titten, patoaḥ tiftaḥ, ha'anek ta'anik, ha'avet ta'avitennu (see Bava Metzia 31b and in many places); im anneh te'anneh — both much affliction and little affliction (Mekhilta Mishpatim ch. 18); asham asham (Sifra Vayikra ch. 21); ma'en yema'en — what is the reason⚠13 (Ketubbot 44b). g) To indicate two modes of the action: as akhol ye'akhel — two eatings (Menaḥot 17a); darosh darash — two inquiries (Zevaḥim 101b); shakhav yishkav — two lyings (Sifra Metzora ch. 7). h) To include a thing that is not in the meaning of the verse: as ganov yigganev — to bring in the lost object (Mekhilta ibid. ch. 16); hamer yamir — to include the woman (Temurah 2a); ga'ol yig'alennah — to include the wife and the heir (Sifra Beḥukkotai ch. 4). Thus this rule widened in the course of time without end.

v1_06_19:1) In these two readings there is nothing but an uncertainty in what was heard, which arose among the hearers because of the likeness of the sounds of the words. For the sound of yofi lekha is quite like the sound of Yah ve-lakh, since the sound of the ḥolam is like the sound of the qamaṣ in pronunciation. And so I conjecture that this too was the reason for the uncertainty there (Sukkah 4:5) about the saying they said at the circling, which according to some was worded "Anna Adonai hoshi'ah na, etc.," and according to R. Judah they said "Ani va-ho hoshi'ah na." For if we consider well and exactly the sound of the words ani va-ho, we find that it is close to the sound of the words anna H' as written; but the small change in it they made because they did not want to pronounce the Name of four letters. But a slight proof emerges for us from this, that in reading the Explicit Name they did not read the yod with a sheva as written, as in our vocalization, but read the yod with a ḥiriq; for then ani va-ho would be close in its pronunciation to anna H' as written. I do not assert this conjecture as settled, but it is closer than the conjecture of the Talmud, which went into gematrias.
