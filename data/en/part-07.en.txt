Chapter Seven

The work of the Scribes with regard to the Hebrew language.

The cord that binds the individuals of every nation to be of one heart and one spirit has always been religion and language. For this reason we have seen at all times that if the bond of society and unity in one of the nations has weakened a little, and its best men, its heads, its sages and its men of understanding have roused themselves to strengthen the slackened bond, then they turned their eyes first to religion and strove to renew the youth of their religion, and second they turned their eyes to the language. Those who returned from the exile also understood this, and therefore their first act was to strengthen the religion and the Torah that were an inheritance to them from their fathers; and they also gave heed to their language, which is Hebrew, which had been the language of their fathers from of old. All the days they dwelt in their land, until they were emptied from vessel to vessel [Jeremiah 48:11], Hebrew was the language they spoke. The Aramaic language they did not know and did not understand (Jeremiah 5:15). Only individuals among them, and especially the men who were close to the government, alone understood the Aramaic language (Isaiah 36:11). But from the time that people of the nations of Assyria, whom the king of Assyria settled in the cities of Israel when he exiled Israel to Assyria, mingled with them, and afterwards, at the exile of Israel from its land, when a great number went into exile to Babylon, from then on they learned the language of the people among whom they were forced to live and in whose land they were forced to dwell. When they returned from the exile, Nehemiah complained of the upheaval that had taken place in the land of Israel with regard to the loss of the language, because he found that a new generation had arisen in the Holy Land that did not know the language of its fathers and could no longer speak the Jews' language (Nehemiah 13:24). But this complaint of Nehemiah is a testimony that the knowledge of the Hebrew language had not died out entirely from among the people, since his complaint was not about all the inhabitants of the land but about individuals in it, namely the children of the mixed marriages, born of Jewish fathers who had taken foreign wives. And it also appears from this that those who returned from the exile knew the Hebrew language as before; for otherwise he would surely have had to complain about them too, as about the inhabitants of the land. And how can it enter our mind to say that they forgot it entirely during the years of their exile, when we see the sublime prophecies that were prophesied in the exile, and the psalms and songs that were sung at that time, and the books that were composed narrating chronicles and other matters? Were not all these meant to instruct the people and to teach them knowledge, and they are all written in the Hebrew language. How then shall we not say and decide that they understood the Hebrew language well? But the truth in this matter is that they spoke both languages, Hebrew and Aramaic; and indeed, as we have seen, the new generation in the land of Israel was faithless to the Hebrew language, and perhaps the young people who were born and grew up in the exile were also faithless to it and did not learn the language and the writing, so that for them too the Aramaic language was more intelligible. For this reason too it became a statute in Israel in the days of Ezra to translate the Torah into the Aramaic language. And had not the understanding of Aramaic among the multitude of the people been stronger than the understanding of Hebrew, there would have been no place for this enactment. But of all their teachings that they taught in the Aramaic language nothing is left to us of which we can say with certainty that it is from that time, except one which, by way of conjecture, may be ascribed to the first Scribes in its own writing and its own language. It is in the Mishnah, which has transmitted to us a testimony given by one of the sages who lived at the beginning of the days of the Maccabees: "Concerning the ayil kamtsa locust, that it is clean; concerning the liquids of the slaughterhouse [of the Temple], that they are clean; and that whoever touches a corpse is unclean" (Eduyot 8:4). Now this sage testified to these things, and this shows that he speaks from an ancient tradition. And the sayings to which he testified are in the Aramaic language, the like of which is not found in the Mishnah. And although we have found a few more sayings in the Aramaic language in the Mishnah, said in the name of Hillel the Babylonian, such as: "A name made great is a name destroyed; he who does not increase, decreases; he who does not learn deserves death; and he who makes use of the crown passes away" (Avot 1), these are only moral proverbs that this Babylonian was used to have on his lips. But our discussion here is about matters of interpretation and halakhot, and in this respect this testimony that Yose ben Yo'ezer gave is truly unique of its kind in the whole Mishnah. Therefore we may rightly conjecture that this is a remnant of the interpretations of the Scribes, who taught the people in Aramaic translation. However, even if the strength of the Hebrew language weakened, it was not yet broken. And it is a clear matter that from the time they returned from the land of their exile they thought to strengthen this weakness. And as they strove to raise up the Torah and to strengthen it, it being the true means to strengthen and unite their national society, so they strove to raise the standing of the language, it too being a means to reach this goal. Indeed, from all the actions that the heads of those who returned from the exile did for the good of their nation, their desire to restore all that had been forgotten to its former strength is plain to our eyes. Therefore we shall not doubt that the language did not fall behind all the other forgotten things. And it is no exaggeration if we say that in one respect the language had a further advantage in this regard. For the language is the means to the unity of the nation, because a shared language gives birth to an inclination of hearts one to another among all who speak it. Besides this, it had an advantage in that it is a pure source of the wisdom of the Torah, from which are the issues of life [Proverbs 4:23] to revive the spirit of wisdom and knowledge and the fear of the Lord among the nation, since it is the language in which their holy writings were written. There is no doubt that their longing to raise up the language had already become human action immediately after they came up from the exile. For we see how, out of his great complaint and vexation [1 Samuel 1:16], Nehemiah contends with his people because their young people speak with a stammering lip and another tongue [Isaiah 28:11] and cannot speak the Jews' language. Therefore, from this complaint of his about the lack of knowledge of the language that had grown strong among them, we can surely judge rightly that he did not rest nor keep quiet until he had made an effort to make straight what was crooked, to improve the state of the language and to restore it to its former condition. A great proof of this endeavor of theirs is given us by the enactment of the prayers, the benedictions and the praises that were enacted during the time of the first generations of the Scribes. Indeed it will not enter our mind to decide that all the prayers and benedictions ascribed to the first generations are in truth the work of their hands and their enactments, for no one who considers the matter with faithful and upright criticism can agree to this in general; but it cannot be denied either that some of the prayers and benedictions are children of the first generations. Now these prayers and benedictions were all composed in the Hebrew language, and therefore we see two things from this. First: that this language was understood by the people. And further this: that they strove to strengthen it and to raise it up. For had it not been so, how could it have entered their mind to establish the prayers and benedictions in a language that they did not understand, and that they did not wish to strengthen and keep alive? Now it is clear that the Hebrew language was only weakened in their hands, and even this weakness was only among the multitude of the people; but among the sages it did not weaken, rather they always held fast to it and strove to raise its standing. And for this reason it is not surprising that all the words of the Oral Torah, which include many sayings of the Scribes, have all reached us in the Hebrew language. But the Aramaic language, which they came to know during the days of the exile, exerted a great force on the Hebrew language, until it bred and made grow various new characteristics on the soil of the Hebrew language. These characteristics are already recognizable in all the books of this period, and of the time before and after it, in those written in plain style (prose) as in those written in poetic style. Yet it is self-evident that in this respect plain style differs from poetic style: since the character of the language of poetry is to speak in pure and choice diction, the poet guards himself carefully against all the foreign elements that spoil the beauty, and against every alien characteristic that the multitude of the people attached to the language. Therefore the corruption of Hebrew through the force of Aramaic did not harm poetic style so much; and even though in psalms and songs that are children of this time there are, here one and there one, places where the marks of Aramaic are recognizable, nevertheless, since these are few and slight, the spirit of the old Hebrew still hovers over all these remnants, and the marks of their time that cling to them did not take away their beauty and the loftiness of their diction. Not so is it in plain style. Since in it the writer makes known and sets down his thoughts in the common language that he is used to speaking, he will therefore not escape the foreign elements that were born in the language in the course of time, and sometimes he will choose them on purpose, because in the common language he will explain and state his meaning more precisely to the multitude of the people, who are used to the common language. And for this reason the language is very hard in the books of this time written in plain style, such as Ecclesiastes, Chronicles, Daniel, Ezra, Nehemiah and the Scroll of Esther, on all of which the character of the Aramaic language weighs heavily. Now at any rate we see that in all the books of this time, both the books written in plain style and those written in poetic style, the character of the Aramaic language has grown strong over them all. And likewise in the benedictions and prayers whose foundation is in that time, it is also clearly seen that the language in which they were composed was no longer of the purity of the old Hebrew language. Still more do we find a change in the character of the language in the sayings on matters of Torah and statutes that they spoke to the people and to their disciples. Because of this great change the masters of the Talmud already gave a special name to the language in which things on matters of Torah were said or written, and called it: the language of the sages. And it is the language that we call: the New Hebrew language. We do not call it new because it uses words of which no mention is found in the Holy Scriptures; for the absence of their mention in the Holy Scriptures is no proof of their non-existence. But we call it new because marks are seen in it that are faithful signs that it changed its character, since the character of Aramaic grew strong over it. And at a later time it changed its character still more, because the characteristics of other languages too, especially Greek and Latin, came into it and they too changed its character. And indeed, if we consider the matter well, we find that they were forced to this. Nor is it a new thing under the sun [Ecclesiastes 1:9]; has not necessity at all times been the mother of every new invention? These generations too were pressed by necessity to widen the language, and this widening changed its character. For the times changed, and with them the kinds of knowledge too. New opinions and new concepts spread among them, and they were forced to invent words and terms to describe them in speech, for the old Hebrew did not suffice; and they used Aramaic and Syriac after the manner of Hebrew, until they were able to speak of every new matter and concept. This is not the place to inquire into the particular characteristics that the Hebrew language had at that time; we say only in general that they did not neglect to hold fast to their language, and even if its character changed in part, this is no proof that it went backward.{v1_04_10:1}

A great work concerning the language of Holy Scripture is ascribed to the first generations of the Scribes, namely: their correction of the script and of the language. As to the script, we are told that Ezra changed the script in which the Torah was originally written. For it was originally written in the Hebrew script, and Ezra came and wrote it in the Assyrian script (Sanhedrin 21b). The account of these matters is founded on an old tradition, held firm in all generations, and we have no compelling reason to cast doubt on this tradition. The sages in the Talmud wondered at the matter from various sides, but they did not cast doubt on it, for the event was verified in their eyes. They did, however, seek to find a support for the permission to change the script. On this one of the sages (R. Eliezer ben Jacob) adds that one of the prophets who came up with them from the exile testified to them that the Torah should be written in Assyrian (Zevaḥim 62b). This is like an explicit apology for Ezra's daring to change the script: he made the matter depend on the power of prophecy of the prophets who came up with them, on whose word Ezra did what he did. Other sages tried to devise another apology, by proving the permission — by their ways of midrash — from Scripture (Sanhedrin 22a). That these later sages turned away from the tradition of R. Eliezer ben Jacob, although they said of him that his Mishnah is “a kav, but clean,” and tried to make the matter depend on their midrash and to prove that the permission to change the script is already hinted at in Scripture, is no doubt because it did not suit them to make the matter depend on prophecy; for they found it hard to believe that the prophets permitted writing in Assyrian, since a prophet is not permitted to introduce anything new from now on. Now we know very well that these apologies are weak, but the core of the matter is clear: it is an old tradition that Ezra changed the script from Hebrew to Assyrian. From then on they wrote the Torah in the Assyrian script and left the Hebrew to the hedyotot [common people]. This came from their wish to give the holy writings an added holiness; therefore they ordained that it should have a script of its own, while for every common use they had the old Hebrew script. Indeed they left it also to the hedyotim, that is, the Kutim [Samaritans], whom they called by the nickname “hedyotim.” For the Torah of the Kutim is written in the Hebrew script to this day. Accordingly we find that the script on the coins struck by the Hasmoneans was not the Assyrian script, for they did not use it for any common thing; the script on those coins was Hebrew. And if we look at it carefully, we find that it is exactly like the script of the Kutim. Now the reason why Ezra and his company changed the script from Hebrew to Assyrian was twofold. First, the reason lay in the change of the times. For just as during the days of the exile they learned the language spoken by the peoples among whom they lived, so no doubt they also learned their script, as clearly follows from the words of the Talmud that the Assyrian script came up with them from Assyria (Sanhedrin 21b; Yerushalmi Megillah 1:11). But they had yet another reason for this, namely to widen the separation between the Jews and the Samaritans, that is, the Kutim, and to make a visible mark distinguishing the Torah of the Jews from the Torah of the Samaritans.{v1_04_11:1} For the Kutim themselves wrote their Torah in the Hebrew script, although Assyrian was the script of their land; for their copy of the Torah came from a time earlier than this, before Ezra changed the script, and therefore they held on to the script in which they had received the copy of the Torah. And the masters of the Talmud rightly conjectured that they left the Hebrew script to the Kutim; and no doubt their intent is that they left them this script in the copy of their Torah. By this Ezra also did a great thing for the preservation of the correct and true text. For it could easily happen that Jews would copy from a Samaritan Torah scroll, and who would stand surety for them that they would not falsify or corrupt the readings, as in fact in the course of time [the Samaritans] falsified the Torah in many places? Therefore Ezra did well to change the script; it became a dividing screen between the Jews and the Kutim and served them as a safeguard to keep them from every stumbling.

Yet not only with regard to the script but also with regard to the language did the Scribes make corrections, to set the readings of the Torah right. These corrections are called tikkunei soferim [corrections of the Scribes]. Of particular corrections — in which place, and what it was that they corrected — no clear word has reached us. The ancients already groped in the dark in this matter, and the eighteen places in Scripture that they pointed out, which in their opinion the Scribes corrected (Mekhilta and Tanḥuma, Be-shallaḥ; Genesis Rabbah ch. 49; Leviticus Rabbah ch. 11; and in many places), are nothing but a slight conjecture; and in most of the places where they thought another wording would have been fitting, we find that the correct one, by the plain sense of Scripture, is as it stands before us in Scripture, and it is not a correction made by the Scribes (Ibn Ezra on Numbers 11:15). But the core of the matter is clear: the Scribes made corrections in Scripture, and these were called tikkunei soferim. Moreover, in all the Torah scrolls in our hands there are hints of corrections by means of dots above some words of the Torah. These dots were ascribed to Ezra. The masters of aggadah said of them that some words in the Torah were doubtful to him, and he did not want to lay a hand on the readings before him; he left them as they were and put dots over them to give a hint of the doubt (Avot de-Rabbi Natan ch. 34; Numbers Rabbah ch. 3). It is true that these dots are very old. For we find that the great sages who lived in the first century after the destruction expounded their midrashim on these dots, and this is proof that they held them to be very ancient. From all this we see in any case that from of old the opinion spread among the people that the Scribes made corrections in the Torah; and further, that where a doubtful reading lay before them they made marks, namely the dots, to hint at the doubt. Hence of necessity they believed that in the time of the Scribes, when [the Scribes] strove to restore the Torah to its former state, they found readings of which some were corrupt and some doubtful. And it should not seem strange to us that the ancient sages thought the books had become corrupt in the course of time, although the Torah was set in order and kept in the Temple of the Lord. For we know the fate of the Torah scroll in the days of King Josiah son of Amon, and we have heard the complaint of the prophet Jeremiah against the wise men of his generation, when he said: “How do you say: We are wise, and the Torah of our God is with us? But behold, the false pen of the scribes has made it into a lie” [Jeremiah 8:8].⚠1 We are also told that even after the time of the Scribes, when the books were surely kept with excellent care, they nevertheless found in the Temple court scrolls with differing readings, so that they themselves were in doubt which was the right one and which the spoiled one; and they did not decide from tradition, but because they found the one reading in two scrolls they upheld it, and the other they found in only one scroll and set it aside (Soferim ch. 6). Also, many readings in our books do not have the agreement of the sages of the Talmud, since we see that these sages had readings on which they based their expositions, and these differ from the Masorah. Is there not an endless number of variants in the readings of Scripture between the books of Babylonia and the books of the Land of Israel? And although already in a very early time they occupied themselves with matters of the Masorah and with fixing the text of Scripture, it is nevertheless clear that at all times there were various readings in our holy books; and this very fact, that already in ancient days they weighed and searched with great effort to examine the readings, to sift them and to purify them, surely proves that their time needed it. How then can we wonder if the sages thought that in the time of the Scribes, before which there was a time of forgetting and neglect of the Torah, this neglect caused corruption of the books, so that the Scribes were compelled to make corrections and to restore the matter to its former state? But what these corrections of the Scribes were, of this no clear word has reached us. Only by way of conjecture can one penetrate the secret of this tradition. We should pay attention to two expressions brought in the Talmud, namely miqra soferim and ittur soferim. The masters of the Talmud explain the sense of these expressions in a strange way, and in a manner from which it appears that their sense was hidden from them and that they groped in the dark. The expression miqra soferim was understood by them as the correct reading of the words. This reading was handed down to us by the early ones, who were called Soferim (Nedarim 37b, in the Ran); they did not invent a new reading, but taught them the correct manner of reading as it had been in the first generations, back to Moses at Sinai (ibid.). They were compelled to this instruction because their time was a time of decline of the language, so that many did not know how to read a book, and even among those who knew, the reading of the words had surely become corrupt; therefore their first work was to clear these thorns from the language and to correct the reading — not as they invented it from their own heart, but the reading as it had been in the ancient generations. But a thing of such slight value as this is self-evident, and to mention it is superfluous. We cannot believe that they preserved for us in such an explicit expression a thing that is self-evident; even if it had not been preserved for us in tradition, no man of understanding would doubt it — while we see, on the other hand, that the most ancient ones hid from us many great and precious things and said nothing about them. Therefore the expression miqra soferim must of necessity be understood of another matter.{v1_04_13:1} Still more obscure is the expression ittur soferim. What is this ittur, and what is its sense and nature? Shall we understand by it that at times superfluous words were written to beautify the language, and it was called ittur [ornament] because it adorns the language by this? Or shall we say the opposite, that the sense of the word ittur is removal, and its meaning would be that at times letters that ought to have been put in the words were removed from them (the first explanation is that of the Ran and the second that of the Rosh)? But whether we say this or that, it is impossible that the word soferim refers to the ancient teachers [mevinim]. For if it referred to them, and they added words for the beauty of the language or omitted letters that ought to be there, it would not be right to say that they changed nothing but that such was the tradition — is not the ittur ascribed to the Scribes? Therefore of necessity ittur soferim does not refer to the particular Scribes, that is, the teachers in the days of Ezra and after him, but refers to the ornament for the beauty of the language that was customary among writers in general. But this too is very far from the truth, and we cannot believe it, for two reasons. First, from the form of the expression it is clear that it refers to the particular Scribes, and the expression soferim is not found with another sense. Second, for what purpose would one need to mention that all the turns of phrase that serve the beauty of the language, or what was removed for the sake of its grammar, all came from the pen of the writer of the Torah — and who ever doubted this? Some of the sages after the Talmud already sensed that the word soferim in the expression ittur soferim has no other sense than the particular Scribes. They said explicitly that in the time before the Scribes the common people corrupted some words by adding some letter in their reading, and the Scribes came and removed the letter and commanded to read without the letter; but they did not do so on their own judgment, but because they had a tradition to that effect (*Arukh*, s.v. ʿ-ṭ-r). But we are not told how they found the matter written in the Torah. If it was written without the letter that the corrupters added in their reading, and was written in the correct reading, in agreement with the reading of the Scribes, and the Scribes taught the people to read properly and as it is explicitly written — what particular work is this, that they specified it and ascribed it to the Scribes? Is it not also included in miqra soferim? In short, these two expressions are closed and sealed, and all that is said about them in the Talmud does not help us to remove the darkness from them.

Yet this we cannot deny in any way: the expressions tikkunei soferim, miqra soferim and ittur soferim are very old expressions, and they tell us of the different works that the Scribes performed. About the work of the corrections it is very hard to say anything clear, because before us the corruption is already corrected and we cannot know in which place the corruption was; and as for our finding that the masters of the Talmud in the midrashim count and number all the places that the Scribes corrected, we have already seen that their words have only the value of aggadah. Yet it is true that the Scribes made various corrections to purify the readings from corruptions that had come upon them in the course of time. And it is very likely that tikkunei soferim is a general term that includes all kinds of corrections in Holy Scripture, and its particular kinds are miqra soferim and ittur soferim. For miqra soferim is nothing other than a kind of the corrections they made by adding words for a better understanding; but they did not add them in writing, only in reading, and therefore they called such corrections miqra soferim [the reading of the Scribes]. Likewise ittur soferim is a kind of the corrections they made by removing written words; but they did not erase them from the writing, they removed them only in thought and commanded that they be read in a manner other than what is written before us. This conjecture finds its confirmation if we consider carefully that old saying which speaks of these expressions. When R. Isaac says: “Miqra soferim, ittur soferim, words read and not written, and words written and not read, are a halakhah [given] to Moses at Sinai,” will not every man of understanding see from the measure of these words that the expressions miqra soferim, ittur soferim, read and not written, written and not read, were not invented by the speaker, but were old expressions, known and famous, which are names for works of the Scribes, and that the speaker only added an explanation of them according to his opinion? But we have already seen that his explanation has no foundation or root. Yet the expressions need an explanation only if they are understood as four kinds of corrections that the Scribes made. But it is close to the truth that this is not so, and that we have here only two kinds of their corrections together with their explanations, namely miqra soferim and ittur soferim: the explanation of miqra soferim is “read and not written,” and the explanation of ittur soferim is “written and not read.” But they corrected only in the reading and not in the writing, because even according to what is written the sense of the matter is not lacking; only by their correction they brought it about that the saying has a clearer sense, and their correction is like an explanation of Scripture. And if the masters of the Talmud ascribe all these corrections to a halakhah [given] to Moses at Sinai, we do not know their intent in this; but this we know, that it is impossible to say that the writer of the Torah delivered the words as they are written before us and commanded that they not be read as they are written but in another manner, for this is a thing that sound reason cannot bear. How shall we say that Moses wrote a word in the Torah whose sense in the saying we do not know,⚠2 and commanded that it be read in the fitting manner — why did he not write it properly at once? And even if we could appease our mind by saying that he had a hidden intent that is concealed from us, what shall we say about the “written and not read” and the “read and not written” in the rest of the holy writings? Shall we say of these too that there is a secret and a hidden intent in them? And even if we wish to concede even this strange thing, and to say that the prophets who wrote their words in a book also had a concealed intent in changing the written text from the fitting reading, how can we find any opening for argument in those writings which, according to the opinion of the sages of the Talmud, were not written in a book by their authors, but others wrote them according to what they had received? Surely these of necessity received the text as it ought to be read, for so they had heard it; why then do we find in these books most of these changes? Therefore it seems clear that these corrections came from the hand of the Scribes; they made them and established them for the benefit of a clear understanding of the holy writings. A third kind of corrections the Scribes made: at times they corrected the written text and commanded that some word be read otherwise than we find it written, from their wish to speak in clean language.{v1_04_14:1} Of this kind also is the change in the reading of some word which, if read as written, could lead the reader into error in the belief in corporeality. This is found especially where Scripture speaks of man seeing God, as in “Three times in the year shall all thy males appear before the Lord God” [Exodus 34:23], and many like it, where no doubt it was originally written and read yir'eh [shall see], and not yera'eh [shall appear] as it is pointed in our books. For if it were so, it would not be fitting to say et penei Elohim, but el penei Elohim. Therefore it is very likely that the Scribes changed the reading in order to keep away the belief in corporeality.{v1_04_14:2} Yet in all these kinds of corrections the reason was not that the books had become corrupt, but that they too thought that the written reading was the reading that existed originally, only they changed its reading for one of the reasons we have stated. But no doubt they also found readings which in their opinion were corrupt, and nevertheless they did not change their writing but changed the reading. This was where the corrupt word could be explained without any correction; yet because in their opinion the word is explained by their correction in a clearer and more ample manner, they did not refrain from correcting it; but they did not correct it in the written text, they corrected only its reading. Of this kind are many of the “written and not read” that we find in Scripture.{v1_04_15:1} But not all the “written and not read” arose from the corruption of the readings before the time of the Scribes, for many of them arose in the time after the Scribes, down to a very late time, and only through the fault of the copyists. For this reason we find various variant readings that the masters of the Talmud and the midrashim had, against the Masorah. And in the Targums we find that at times they translated according to the ketiv, but mostly according to the qeri; and this is a sign that many of the variant readings are later than the ancient Targums. And who knows whether some of the corrections in the Masorah that were made by changing the reading did not have their root in the ancient Targums, that is, that the masters of the Masorah corrected the reading against the ketiv according to them? Now we see that most of the kinds of corrections that we have ascribed to the Scribes have the character of interpretation, and we can judge in part from their nature that precision in expressions and words was already the essence of their interpretations and explanations of the holy writings.

We have already said that yet another kind of correction in Holy Scripture was attributed to the Scribes. This is that they sometimes corrected some expression which, in their opinion, was not fitting to be said in this way with regard to God; therefore they corrected it by choosing another expression, in order to give honor to God; and they point to eighteen places which the Scribes corrected in this manner. But we also said that in most of these places there is no compelling reason to say that they are corrections; yet the root of this view is without doubt strong and received, and not only have we no reason to deny it, but we must admit its truth, since we see from some places in Holy Scripture that the hand of correctors ruled over them, and we have found no time more fit for such work than the time of the Scribes. Now for the purpose we have stated they sometimes exchanged one word for another,{v1_04_15:2} or changed the form of the word or the position of the parts of the sentence, whereby a different sense is given to the sentence;{v1_04_15:3} and so too they sometimes omitted whole passages for some reason and purpose.{v1_04_15:4} And now we shall see that a number of things in Holy Scripture as well, which appear from their content to be appendices and are marked by signs and indications showing that they are later than the time of the composition of the book in which they are found, are also to be counted among the corrections which the Scribes made. And they did a great thing in this, in that they preserved for us various precious writings which prophets and sages of the early generations composed, and which were scattered scroll by scroll; and they gathered them and attached them in the proper place, to teach us, to help us understand the words of the book to which they were attached, for a fuller understanding; and for this reason the name by which they were called befits them: ba'alei asufot [masters of collections]. Indeed, even though we have no clear reports on all these things that reach back to the time of the Scribes, but have relied on late reports, nevertheless it appears that this was really so, since the existence of all the corrections mentioned cannot be denied, and anyone who has eyes will see that they are really found in fact; and we have no reason to attribute them to others than the Scribes alone, for they were the men whose office was to refine and purify our Holy Scriptures, and to them the authority was given.
