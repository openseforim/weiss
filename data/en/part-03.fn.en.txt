v1_03_02:1) Maimonides, in Hilkhot Melakhim 3:10, wrote that the king has authority to kill without clear proof and without witnesses. But this has no basis in the Torah, and it was founded only on incidents that took place; that is, since we saw that the kings actually did so, from this they judged that it is permitted to do so. But for the inquiry into antiquity such a judgment, from the later to the earlier, is no proof that the thing is right. And we must still ask: if the general statute was not so, why did they depart from it? And if we say that they were permitted, where is the permission written?

v1_03_02:2) According to the scholar Shadal [S. D. Luzzatto], in his commentary on Isaiah 1:17, the sense of the verse "You shall not bear [ta'aneh]" etc. is: "You shall not answer [ta'aneh] your neighbor as a false witness"; and according to this interpretation there is no need to say that the root anah is a fixed term for the notion of testimony. But in the verse "to testify against him [la'anot bo] of wrongdoing [sarah]" one cannot add the word "as a witness" unless we explain that the word sarah describes the character of the witness's words, that is, that his words are sarah, and its sense is like "falsehood," as Luzzatto himself explained (ibid., verse 8).⚠17 But if we explain that the sense of sarah is the character of the deed that the sinner did, against whom he testified, and the intent of the verse is "to testify against him that he did wrong," then adding the word "as a witness" has no basis at all, and then the root anah necessarily means "testified." Indeed, if we consider the sequence of the verses in the Torah, we find that Luzzatto's interpretation is refuted. For these are the words of Scripture: "If a malicious witness rises up against a man to testify against him of wrongdoing, then both the men between whom the controversy is shall stand before the Lord ... and the judges shall inquire diligently, and behold, the witness is a false witness, he has testified falsely against his brother" (Deuteronomy 19:17–18). From this it appears that it means to say: if a witness testifies against a man that he committed a sin, and through the judges' inquiry it becomes known that what he testified is false. Hence sarah is necessarily a description of the sin and not of the character of the testimony, for the character of the testimony becomes known only through the judges' inquiry. Accordingly it is proved that the root anah in itself is a term for the notion of testimony. So also "and my righteousness will testify for me" [Genesis 30:33], and "our sins testify against us" [Isaiah 59:12].

v1_03_05:1) In 1 Kings 8:65-66: "And Solomon held the feast at that time ... seven days and seven days, even fourteen days. On the eighth day he sent the people away." And in 1 Chronicles⚠20 (7:8-10): "And Solomon held the feast at that time seven days ... And on the eighth day they made a solemn assembly, for they kept the dedication of the altar seven days, and the feast seven days. And on the three and twentieth day of the seventh month he sent the people away." How are we to understand this? For according to one version he sent the people away on the eighth day, and according to the other the eighth day was the solemn assembly. And according to the version in Kings the feast was exactly fourteen days, and how could this be? Necessarily, as we wrote in the text, the festival of consecration began with special intention on the ninth of the month; therefore the festival, that is, the first seven days, ended on the fifteenth of the month, and that was already the first day of Sukkot; and there remained for Sukkot together with Shemini Atzeret only seven days, and on the eighth day after these seven days he sent the people away, and that was the twenty-third of the seventh month. And only in this way can these two verses be reconciled, which as they appear to our eyes contradict each other.

v1_03_11:1) If we consider the last two sections of the book of Joel, we find that they have no proper connection at all with the preceding ones. For the preceding ones speak of the evil that came upon the land from the locust and the palmerworm, which made everything a desolation, and the prophet calls to repentance and promises that when they return the Lord will have pity on His people, and they will know through this that the Lord is God. But the last ones do not speak of this event at all; they are only prophecies about future time. And the prophet speaks of the return of the captivity of Judah and Jerusalem, and of their scattering among the nations and the division of the land, which already existed in fact. He speaks also of their precious things that were brought into the temples (Ezra 1:7), and he speaks of those sold to the sons of the Greeks. And all these are matters that were certainly and clearly not prophesied before the destruction of the Temple. See further on this in *Moreh Nevukhei ha-Zeman*, gate 11.

v1_03_12:1) Indeed it seems that the ending of the book of Malachi is not part of the book; rather the last verses, "Remember the Torah of Moses My servant ... Behold, I will send you Elijah the prophet" [Malachi 3:22–23], are the conclusion of the collection of the Torah and the Prophets. And the epithet "Elijah the prophet" does not refer to the ancient Elijah; rather it calls the prophet in general by the name Elijah, just as Ezekiel (37:24) called the king who will arise by the name "My servant David." And it is likely that this conclusion is from a time when they complained most about the cessation of prophecy. See in Psalms chapter 74, the whole of which psalm shows that it was composed in the time of the Hasmoneans, and there it complains of this: "We see not our signs; there is no more any prophet; neither is there among us any that knows how long" [Psalms 74:9]. And in several places in the book of the Hasmoneans the expression is found, "until a prophet arises in Israel," or "until a true prophet arises in Israel." And according to this we may suppose that this conclusion was made at that time, that is, at the time when Judah Maccabee, after he had prevailed, gathered the holy writings that had been scattered in the days of the war and the persecution, as is explained in the Second Book of the Hasmoneans; then they made this conclusion to seal his collection.
