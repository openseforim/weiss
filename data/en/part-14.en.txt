Chapter Fourteen

Yoḥanan the High Priest; the Kutim [Samaritans]; Joshua ben Peraḥiah; Nittai the Arbelite.

Even if the heart of the people was divided in religious views, in one thing they were of one heart, namely: in throwing off the yoke of oppression from upon them to attain lasting prosperity. From the day that the Hasmoneans arose and stirred up zeal against their enemy, their love for the religion of their fathers stood by them, so that they prevailed and did valiantly. More than his other brothers had done, Simeon the Hasmonean did to achieve the freedom of Israel. But the freedom was not completed until the days of his son John Hyrcanus, who is known by the name Yoḥanan the High Priest. He did greater things than all his brothers: he enlarged the borders of the Land of Israel and subdued peoples under his feet. In all the wars that he fought he succeeded, and through the whole breadth of his land happiness flourished for glory and splendor. Then the honor of the religion of Israel rose to the highest, and many of the peoples of the land became Jews. For when the Lord gave him rest from the Greeks, he went up against the Edomites, who were thorns in his side, and made war with them, prevailed over them, subdued them under his rule, and forced them to accept the religion of the Jews. This coercion was not only for a political reason but also for a religious one, for he strove with all his might to remove the snares to religion that were around him, in order to strengthen the religion of the Torah and give it a firm foundation. True, according to our views today this coercion is an impure stain on the life of Yoḥanan: was it not for freedom of faith and religion that all the wars his fathers fought were fought, and for it tens of thousands of Israel shed their blood like water? How then could he himself now force others to make them abandon their religion? But how can we know today the great reasons that moved him at that time to do a thing, even if he could not justify it? For many were the obstacles that lay in wait for them on every side, and their existence and the existence of their religion depended on their efforts to remove the obstacles by whatever means. But from another side the matter is very astonishing. How did he force the Edomites to accept the religion? Is it not so that according to the Oral Torah such a conversion, which is not voluntary, is no conversion? For even one who converts for the sake of a woman, for the sake of fear or for the sake of love is not a proselyte. So they said of those who became Jews in the days of Mordecai, that they are not proselytes, because they converted only since the fear of Mordecai fell upon them (tractate Gerim ch. 1); and likewise they said of one who converted for the sake of the table of kings or for the sake of authority (Yevamot 24b). How then shall we say that the Edomites were full proselytes, since they converted only because of the fear of Yoḥanan upon them and from their wish to remain in their land, and not willingly and not for the sake of Heaven? But it is clear that all the halakhot that teach the nullity of a forced conversion, or of one made for some other motive, did not yet exist at that time, but were introduced at a later time. They are not earlier than the time when the hand of the Romans was strong over Israel, when many proselytes joined the house of Jacob, and many of them converted for the sake of marriage or in order to find favor in the eyes of the leaders of the Jews (Graetz vol. 3, p. 331); and especially in the days of the emperor Tiberius and after him, in the last half-century before the destruction of the Temple, when the matter was displeasing in the eyes of the government. Therefore they made the conditions of conversion heavier and decided that only one who becomes a Jew for a moral reason is to be accepted, and then these halakhot came to light.

The conquests that Yoḥanan made in the land of Syria were the cause of new enactments. He conquered several places in the land of Syria, drove out their inhabitants and settled Jews in them; and it is self-evident that from that time he treated these places like places in the Land of Israel with regard to the tithe, and therefore without doubt their status as liable to tithes began in his time. Of two places that are mentioned by name and that Yoḥanan conquered, namely Beit She'an (Megillat Ta'anit ch. 3) and Kefar Tzemaḥ (Josephus), we know specifically that the commandment of tithes was observed in them until more than a hundred years after the destruction, when Rabbi Judah the Prince came and released Beit She'an and Kefar Tzemaḥ from the tithes (Yerushalmi Demai ch. 3; Ḥullin 8b⚠1). Likewise one should ascribe to the time of Yoḥanan the introduction of several other halakhot stated regarding the land of Syria: since he conquered a large part of it, they were compelled to speak about it, what its law should be with regard to the statutes that apply in the Land. Included in this is the halakhah "one who buys a field in Syria is like one who buys in the outskirts of Jerusalem" (Tosefta Kelim ch. 1; Gittin 8a). For we have seen that Yoḥanan strove to enlarge the borders of the Land of Israel and to bring Syria under the authority of the Jews, as he did in the other provinces near his border, to the point that he forced the Edomites to become Jews; and therefore it was made a statute and a commandment to strive for the settlement of Syria by Jews, and they said that one who buys a field there is like one who buys in Jerusalem, and likewise they introduced there the commandments of tithes and of the Sabbatical year (this too ibid.). And it seems that most of the halakhot stated regarding the land of Syria were founded in the time of Yoḥanan.{v1_07_10:1} Yet on the other hand it was difficult to treat the land of Syria as the Land of Israel in all its laws and statutes; for if they treated it in all respects as the Land of Israel, then with regard to impurity too it would be considered as the Land of Israel. Therefore in this matter too they made a great enactment. Namely, they did not extend the decree of impurity that had long before been decreed upon the land of the gentiles to the land of Syria as well, except in a place that one cannot enter in purity, where the road leading to it is a road through impure land; for only then is the mind inclined to place a decree of impurity on it: since the places on the border of the Land of Israel that are inhabited by gentiles are impure, reason will not bear that cities beyond them should have pure soil because Jews dwell in them; and therefore they decreed that in this way the soil of the land of Syria conveys impurity like the land outside the Land (Tosefta Kelim ch. 1). But in the cities of Syria on the border that are inhabited by Jews, with no impure land between them and the Land of Israel, so that one can enter them in purity, that portion of land is pure. Therefore, one who buys a field in Syria adjoining the Land of Israel: if he can enter it in purity, it is pure, and if he cannot enter it in purity, it is impure (Ohalot 18:7).{v1_07_11:1} All these enactments were stated anonymously and were not ascribed to Yoḥanan the High Priest, but it is very likely that their foundation lies in his time. But there are decrees and enactments stated explicitly in the name of Yoḥanan, which he decreed and enacted to strengthen religion. These enactments have been preserved in the books of the Talmud: Yoḥanan the High Priest abolished the confession of the tithe, and in his days no one needed to inquire about demai (Ma'aser Ani ch. 5⚠2), for he appointed pairs (Yerushalmi ibid.). The brevity of these words has caused them to stand before us like a riddle. The masters of the Talmud try to shed light on the nature of the enactments, but with all their words a cloud still rests on the event itself. One of the masters of the Talmud relates that Yoḥanan sent through all the cities of Israel and found that they set aside only the great terumah and did not set aside the tithes; therefore he decreed concerning the demai of the common people and enacted that one who buys from one of the common people is obliged to tithe [the produce], and all produce of the common people was called demai; and for this reason one need not inquire about demai, since he enacted that all who buy from the common people are obliged to tithe (see Sotah 48a). And for this reason Yoḥanan enacted that they should not make the confession over the tithe, because they did not set it aside; and even the ḥaverim who did set it aside did not set it aside as the Torah prescribes, but gave the tithe to the priests, so that in no way could they make the confession and say "and also I have given it to the Levite" [Deuteronomy 26:13], for they had either not given it at all or not given it to the Levite; and how could they give thanks before God over something that is not true? But others thought that he abolished the confession of the tithe because there were men who took the tithe by force, and in this way one could not say "and also I have given it." But who does not see in all this that they have nothing on the matter but conjectures? Only one thing can we hold with certainty, namely: that he sent through all the cities of Israel and found that they did not tithe. But if we consider attentively the short report that has reached us, the matter will become properly clear to us. Now if we have heard that in his days no one needed to inquire about demai, we learn from this that Yoḥanan made some enactment regarding the tithe that had not been so before. And what is the enactment? This is explained in two words: that he appointed pairs. But the meaning of these two words is found in another story told in the Talmud: that Eleazar ben Paḥora and Judah ben Paḥora used to take by force. True, the masters of the Talmud blamed Yoḥanan for this, that he did not protest against them doing such a deed, because they understood that these men were Levites who came by force upon the owners of the produce to collect the tithes for their own use; and according to their understanding they rightly blamed Yoḥanan, who had the power and the ability to protest against the evildoers, and who in all other matters also had the will to oppose evildoers: how then did he allow such an evil thing, that men should stand and oppress the people by force, and he did not protest against them? But in truth they had no clear knowledge of this affair, and they blamed Yoḥanan unjustly. For in truth they did nothing against the will of Yoḥanan, and the deeds they did were themselves the result of the enactment that Yoḥanan made. For we have seen that he found that they set aside only the great terumah and not the tithes. And perhaps Yoḥanan saw that the good order of the state in his time required of him not to compel the tithes. For at the time when the Land of Israel was divided among the tribes, and the Levites had no portion and inheritance with them, the tithe was a matter of strict justice; but in the time of Yoḥanan the matter was reversed, and the tithe became a heavy yoke from two sides: first, because days and years had passed in which the land was ruined by the horror of the wars; and second, that they were compelled to give taxes from the produce of their sowing to the government. And so without doubt the common people became negligent in giving the tithe to the Levites; and now Yoḥanan saw that it was not just to compel them in this, yet to abolish the commandment of the tithe was impossible; therefore he made the matter depend on the will of each individual. The sellers he did not compel, for they did not sell their produce as presumed to be tithed; and the buyers too were not compelled, for who could bring proof that it was not tithed? And even if it was not tithed, eating it was not reckoned a sin in his view. But it is not so with produce from which the terumah of the tithe had not been set aside, for this is a prohibition stated explicitly in the Torah; to this Yoḥanan's enactment refers. Namely, he appointed officers in every city who would compel the setting aside of the terumah of the tithe, and these officers were called "pairs" because they were two; and one of these pairs was Eleazar ben Paḥora and Judah ben Paḥora; and what these took by force was the terumah of the tithe, with the consent of Yoḥanan and at his command. And now we understand the connection of the words: that no one needed to inquire about demai — and why? — because Yoḥanan appointed pairs who were in charge of this (see *Korban ha-Edah* on the Yerushalmi). But the commandment of the tithe in general was kept in those days only by a remnant among the people, and therefore they were compelled to abolish the confession of the tithe. And it is no wonder that they were not careful with this commandment at that time as is proper and as commanded, for this negligence was a result of the events of the time, which forced them to it. And perhaps this too: that formerly they used to go beyond the measure in multiplying the tithes, until these became a heavy yoke upon them, and the ancient proverb came true: "If you grasp much, you have not grasped." For we find great hints that among our ancients the commandment of the tithe was understood in another way than among the later ones. The author of the book of Tobit (1:7, 8) relates that Tobit gave his tithe to the Levites, the redemption of the second he gave in food and drink in Jerusalem, and the third he gave to those for whom it was fitting, that is, to the poor. And it does not say that he did so year by year, because it speaks of the third year, in which he gave the three tithes. And likewise Josephus explains the commandment of the tithe (*Antiquities* book 4, ch. 8): to the two tithes that they were commanded to give every year, one to the Levites and the second for eating in Jerusalem, they are to add in the third year one for the widow and the orphan. And so it is hinted in the Septuagint and in the Aramaic translation. Therefore it appears from this that the opinion of the ancients was that they gave three kinds of tithe from the produce of their sowing, two every year, and in addition a third tithe for the poor in the third year (Geiger, *Urschrift* p. 176 ff.).{v1_07_12:1} And thus the yoke of the tithe was too heavy to bear, and it is no wonder that in a time of calamity and poverty they became negligent of it altogether. And perhaps what is related (Ma'aser Sheni ch. 5 in the Yerushalmi) refers to this time: at first the tithe was made into three parts, a third for the priests and Levites known [to the owner], a third for the treasury, and a third for the poor and the ḥaverim in Jerusalem; and they did this with the consent of the sages, to lighten the burden. Moreover, in the later time they devised various stratagems to be exempt from the tithe, as the sons of Ḥanan did, who removed their produce from the scope of the tithe, for they expounded "you shall surely tithe ... and you shall eat" [Deuteronomy 14:22–23]: and not one who sells nor one who buys (Sifrei Re'eh, pisqa 105; Yerushalmi Pe'ah ch. 1; Bava Metzi'a ch. 8). And several stratagems were made on the advice of the sages themselves (Berakhot 31a⚠3, and in many places).

Another enactment Yoḥanan made: until his days there was striking with the hammer in Jerusalem, and he abolished the matter. This enactment too, according to the view of the ancients, was made for the repair of religion. That is: until his days they were accustomed to permit work on the intermediate days of the festival, and Yoḥanan enacted that no work may be done on them. Yet from their mentioning "striking with the hammer" it appears that they thought Yoḥanan forbade only public work, such as striking with a hammer, whose sounds all the people hear; and therefore until his days they were accustomed to permit all kinds of work, and the intermediate days of the festival stood on the level of working days. But who knows whether they said this from an ancient tradition or from conjecture? Perhaps this was nothing but an enactment for the benefit of the inhabitants of the city, that no tumultuous noise be heard in the royal city. And what is the connection between this enactment and the time of Yoḥanan the High Priest? If the reason for the enactment is the benefit of the inhabitants of Jerusalem, so that the sound of the hammer blows coming out of the workshops of the craftsmen who work in iron should not be heard in the royal city, did the hour not already require this before the time of Yoḥanan? But it seems that in truth it was already the custom of old that the smith and the metalworker had their dwelling outside the city, in the outskirts of Jerusalem; but in time of war, when the enemy was outside, the smith and the metalworker who made weapons of war fled into the city. For such was the custom in ancient days, that the conquering enemy removed every smith from the conquered land, lest they make sword or spear (1 Samuel 13:19); and so the king of Babylon exiled, together with the mighty men of valor, "the craftsmen and the smiths" [2 Kings 24:14]. Therefore in the days of the Hasmonean war they settled the ironsmiths inside the city, and there was striking with the hammer in Jerusalem, until Yoḥanan came, in whose days there was peace and security in the land, and he restored the matter to its former state. In regard to the divine service in the Temple, too, he made enactments. The first is that he abolished the "awakeners." For until his days the Levites said in song: "Awake, why do You sleep, O Lord" (Psalms 44). That psalm was composed in the days of the first wars that the Hasmoneans fought against Antiochus Epiphanes, who reviled and blasphemed the armies of God (verse 16⚠4); the pious were forced to go into the caves of the rocks [cf. Isaiah 2:19] and into the place of jackals (ibid. 20); for the sake of God's Torah they were killed all the day (23), and they were scattered among the nations and sold as slaves (12, 13). At that time many prayers were composed, and they have been preserved for us in the collection of the book of Psalms. And this is the psalm in which they prayed to God: "Awake, why do You sleep, O Lord!" This psalm was sung in the Temple all the days of the wars, until the days of Yoḥanan. But we already know that Yoḥanan, out of love of truth, refused to say anything before God that did not agree with the truth, and he abolished the declaration of the tithe because it was a thanksgiving not fitting for his time; so too he abolished the saying of "Awake, why do You sleep, O Lord," because in his days the sun of salvation shone upon them and they defeated their enemies, and he saw that this psalm no longer fitted his time (*Tosafot Ḥadashim* on Ma'aser Sheni ch. 5; *Moreh Nevukhei ha-Zeman*, gate 11). Another enactment he made in the Temple service: formerly the custom was to cut the sacrificial animal between its horns, so that the blood would fall into its eyes and it would be easy to bind; and Yoḥanan abolished this custom, for he said that this was nothing but like inflicting a blemish on the holy offerings of the altar, since through the cutting one makes the offering terefah. But this reason too was not accepted among them, for some said that he abolished this custom because it is the custom of the nations in their sacrifices (Babylonian Talmud Sotah, and the Tosefta there, ch. 13). Perhaps he had no other reason than to spare the animal needless suffering, since it could be done in another way in which its suffering is not so great. And so he did in truth: in place of the cutting he enacted that they make rings in which to insert the animal's neck.{v1_07_14:1} — These are the enactments ascribed specifically to Yoḥanan the High Priest; but without doubt other enactments too were made in his time, to fix the nails of religion firmly as of old. And it seems very likely that a number of new statutes and laws that have come down to us concerning the Kutim [Samaritans] should be ascribed to his time. For like most of the nations around them, so the Kutim in their midst were a grief of spirit and thorns in the eyes of all the Jews. Therefore Yoḥanan stirred up his wrath to chastise them. He went up against Shechem, the dwelling place of the Kutim, captured it, and destroyed their temple on the top of Mount Gerizim after it had stood two hundred years; and this temple of theirs never rose again (*Antiquities* 3, 16⚠5). But the Kutim were not chastened by all this. The inhabitants of Samaria now did Israel even more harm, for their hatred had grown greater than it had been in earlier days. And Yoḥanan went up against Samaria and besieged it a full year. But he did not lead the war himself, for he was old and advanced in days, and at the head of the army he placed his two sons, Aristobulus and Antigonus. About the outcome of this war a legend spread in the generations after him: that Yoḥanan heard a heavenly voice coming out of the Holy of Holies, saying: "The young men who went to wage war have been victorious!" And they wrote down that hour, and it was found to match (Sotah 33a; Josephus, ibid.). Now whatever the value of this legend may be, at any rate we see how great Yoḥanan's honor was in the eyes of his people, so that they ascribed to him the rank of prophecy as well. But the war and the victory cannot but have been the cause of a number of enactments regarding the Kutim.

The Kutim, or Samaritans, were a mixed people, made up of Israelites who remained in Samaria and in other cities after the exile of the land by Sennacherib, and of the nations whom the king of Assyria settled in the land of Israel. Of their deeds until Judah's return from Babylon we have no established information, but we have some traces of them. The righteous kings who were in Judah endeavored to bring them truly into the covenant of the Lord. When Hezekiah made the Passover, he sent into their land and asked that they come to make the Passover. Many responded to his request, but many laughed at them and mocked them (2 Chronicles 30:10); and these mockers were without doubt of the Israelites who had made common cause with the nations that had settled in their midst. King Josiah too endeavored to do them good (ibid. 34). In the days of Jeremiah men came from Shechem, from Shiloh and from Samaria to bring an offering and frankincense to the house of the Lord (Jeremiah 41:5), and they were of the good ones. To Zechariah too came men from Bethel to ask about the fasts [Zechariah 7:2–3], and from the names of the men one may judge that they were not Israelites (Jost, p. 46). After the Jews returned from the exile, the Samaritans wished to join the Jews, to be one nation and to build with them a sanctuary to the Lord; but the Jews pushed them away with both hands and said to them, "You have nothing to do with us in building a house to the Lord" (Ezra 4:1–4). This rejection widened the separation, and from that time on they harassed Israel, informed against them before the government, and caused a great delay in the building of the Temple. The Jews did all in their power to keep them away and laid down harsh statutes against joining with the nations who had been settled in the land, although these assured them that they too, like them, would seek the God of Israel; they set it as a statute that shall not pass away: "Do not give your daughters to their sons, and do not take their daughters for your sons, and do not seek their peace or their good forever" (ibid. 9:12). But against this the Samaritans too exerted all the force of their strength and devised schemes to entice many of Israel to join them, and their desire succeeded in their hand; and not many days passed before they built themselves a temple on the top of Mount Gerizim and became a separate sect. Already at the time they built their sanctuary on Mount Gerizim they had a copy of the book of the Torah of Moses, and perhaps already in the first copy they made falsifications that are still found in their copy today; for on the basis of one falsification that they introduced into their Torah they endeavored to show that Mount Gerizim is the place that the Lord chose. Apart from the falsifications they differed from the Jews with regard to the Holy Scriptures also in this, that they had no Holy Scriptures at all except the Torah of Moses and the book of Joshua. But their corrupt opinion in these matters caused the Jews too to make fences and hedges on their side, as we have already seen above (chs. 7 and 10). In the principles of faith, too, they differed from the Jews, for they did not believe in the resurrection of the dead, and according to the report of the Talmud this was so already among the first Kutim (Berakhot ch. 8, and Rashi's commentary there). And without doubt they differed from the Jews in other matters touching religion as well; for if they were so strict about keeping them away that they forbade intermarriage with them, this is a sign that they regarded them as the peoples of the land. And all that is known of their affairs from the beginning of the coming of the Greeks in the wake of Alexander bears witness that as their status was then, in the days of Ezra, so was their status afterward, in the days of Alexander; no compromise was made between them and the Jews, but the separation grew wider and wider. From the words that Ben Sira said about them (50:25–26) it also appears that they were regarded as the peoples of the land. But we have no reliable evidence about particular enactments that should be ascribed to that time. From the days of the Maccabees, however, there was great reason to lay down statutes concerning their affairs. For until that time they were at any rate, to outward appearance, a branch of the believers in the Torah of Moses; but in the days of Antiochus Epiphanes they removed the veil from their faces and revealed what was in their heart. Then they were in great fear lest the cup of staggering pass over them too, as over the Jews; so they came before Antiochus and declared that they were not Jews at heart, that they did not keep the Sabbath and the festivals with a willing soul, and that they wished to worship the gods of the Greeks and to dedicate their temple to them (*Antiquities* 12, 5). After such a confession, who would regard them as faithful to the religion of Moses? Of this the ancient proverb says: "Sorry is the dough when its own baker testifies that it is bad." Therefore, from the state of religion too, Yoḥanan had no reason to spare them.{v1_07_15:1} Indeed, if we consider the affairs of Yoḥanan and his enactments, we find that great was the effort he made for the repair of religion; therefore there is no doubt that after he had subdued the Kutim he also set his heart to make religious enactments regarding them. As the sanctuary on Mount Gerizim had been thorns in their eyes while it stood, so they rejoiced at the news of its destruction, and therefore they made that day, the seventeenth of the month of Kislev (another reading: 540⚠6), a day of rejoicing and festival (Megillat Ta'anit ch. 8). But even after its destruction the Jews did not try to win the Samaritans over with words of goodwill, so that they would now bring their offerings to the Temple in Jerusalem. Rather the opposite: it was then that they made it a statute that obligatory offerings are not to be accepted from the Kutim (Sheqalim 1:8⚠7). One decree that the later [sources] ascribed to Ezra, that a man may not eat the bread of the Kutim (Pirkei de-Rabbi Eliezer ch. 38), and that "whoever eats the bread of the Kutim is like one who eats pork" (Shevi'it 8:10), was perhaps also a decree of Yoḥanan the High Priest and the sages of his time; for only in his time were the Kutim regarded by them as worse than full gentiles, and therefore they decreed even against their bread, although the bread of gentiles had not yet been forbidden in those days (Shabbat ch. 1). Later, however, they regarded them as true converts, as is found in various places; in the days of R. Meir they sought to decree against them, and [the others] would not agree to the decree, until in the days of the Amoraim they made them like full gentiles.{v1_07_15:2}

The chief reason why the temple of the Kutim was a grief of spirit to the Jews was that they did not give the Temple in Jerusalem the honor due to it, and said that the place the Lord chose is not in Jerusalem, but Mount Gerizim is the chosen place, and their temple is the house that the Lord desired to make His dwelling there. This aroused the hatred of the Jews against them from the beginning of the founding of their temple until its destruction. Even if these Kutim had not harassed them with their plots at all times and in every way, this matter alone — that they raised the rank of their temple above the rank of the Temple in Jerusalem — was enough for their hatred to stay fixed, never to be wiped out. Therefore it was this matter alone that made their separation a complete separation, not their deviation from the accepted understanding of some statutes and from the manner of their observance among the Jews. Now we will understand why the temple in Egypt was not likewise a cause of separation between the Jews in the land of Israel and the Jews of Alexandria. For in Egypt too the Jews who lived there built a temple to God. This temple was called the House of Onias. It was founded in the days when King Ptolemy Philometor reigned over Egypt. Then Onias, of the priestly seed and of the descendants of Simeon the Just, fled to Egypt, because his soul loathed the oppressor, Antiochus Epiphanes. There he found favor in the eyes of King Ptolemy and his wife Cleopatra — and according to some (Ewald vol. 4, p. 409; Graetz vol. 3, p. 35) he became commander over the king's armies — and he received permission from the king to build a temple in Egypt. At the beginning of this temple's days there was great turmoil in the land of Israel, and the hour was not free to pay attention to events in a distant land; therefore without doubt they took no notice of the matter. Besides, there was no need to take notice, for the building of this house was not evil in the eyes of the leaders in the land of Israel. For the Alexandrians, though they honored with reverence the temple they had built, did not on that account cease to turn their eyes and their heart to holy Jerusalem; they thought and believed that the Temple in Jerusalem is the place that the Lord chose to make His dwelling there, and to it they lifted up their soul. There they sent their freewill gifts, and from time to time offerings too. That being so, they themselves did not regard their temple as equal in value to the Temple in Jerusalem, but only as a great high place. Therefore in no way was this house a cause of separation between the Jews in the land of Israel and the Jews of Alexandria. And since the Jews in the land of Israel regarded the house in Alexandria at any rate as a minor sanctuary, it is self-evident that it was necessary to define the law of this house, its rules and its sanctity: with regard to dedications made without specification, does the one who dedicates fulfill the vow he vowed when he brings the dedicated offering to the House of Onias? And so they were compelled to define other matters like this. It seems that the most fitting time for defining statutes in this matter was the time of Yoḥanan. For then they had relief on every side, and not only was the hour free for it, but the hour demanded it. For his time was a time of new foundation for the Torah, and religion renewed its youth like the eagle [Psalms 103:5], to be strengthened by firmly planted nails [Ecclesiastes 12:11]. And especially, after we have seen that Yoḥanan endeavored to destroy the Kutim from the land, that it was decreed that they be worse than the gentiles, and that they destroyed their temple, they were therefore compelled to speak also of the house in Alexandria: to distinguish it from the house that the Kutim had, and to make known that even if its value is not like the value of the house in Jerusalem, it is at any rate regarded as a high place consecrated to the Lord. But of their enactments concerning this house we have only a few remnants. The first halakhah on this point is: "One who says, 'I take upon myself a burnt offering,' shall offer it in the Temple; and if he offered it in the House of Onias he has not fulfilled his obligation" (Menaḥot 109b). By this they wished to make known that those living in Egypt still have obligations that bind them to the Temple in Jerusalem: that we should not think that the temple in Egypt would be for the inhabitants of the land of Egypt what the Temple in Jerusalem is for the inhabitants of the land of Israel; rather, the essence of holiness depends on Jerusalem for all Israel, wherever one's place of dwelling may be. Therefore they made it a statute that for the inhabitants of Egypt too the Temple in Jerusalem is the main thing, and for this reason whatever a man of Israel dedicates to the altar without specification, in whatever place it may be, he is obliged to bring to Jerusalem. And another halakhah they taught: "One who says, 'I take upon myself a burnt offering that I will offer in the House of Onias,' shall offer it in the Temple; and if he offered it in the House of Onias he has fulfilled his obligation." Thus, although he said explicitly that he would offer it in the House of Onias, he should nevertheless offer it in the Temple, since by right all vows and freewill offerings should be brought to the Temple in Jerusalem, which is the main thing. But if he did as he specified in his vow and offered it in the House of Onias, he has also fulfilled his vow. A similar halakhah was taught regarding Nazirite vows. The idea underlying these halakhot is this: although they endeavored to hold fast the cord binding the members of the nation in all the lands of their dispersion, so that their eyes and heart would always be turned only to the one point, namely the Temple in Jerusalem, and surely in their heart they were not pleased with the House of Onias, nevertheless, since they had built this house there to the name of the Lord, the sages in the land of Israel did not wish to turn their good intention into evil and to count their deeds as rebellion and trespass against the Lord. Therefore they permitted offering in this house, although the law of the Torah forbade all offering outside the Temple and warned with the penalty of karet every man who offers up a burnt offering or sacrifice outside the Lord's sanctuary [Leviticus 17:8–9]; and they acted against the statute because they saw that the hour required it. And how right are the words of one of the later [sages] (Rava), when he says: "This one intended a gift; he said: if the House of Onias suffices for me, I take the trouble to bring it, but more than this I do not wish to do, for I cannot trouble myself to go on a long journey." Now just as this justification sufficed for the private individual who dedicated his offering, so it is a sufficient justification for the early sages for not questioning the very founding of the house in Egypt and its establishment: since they intended the honor of the Lord and established it because the way was far from them, so that if this house sufficed for them, good, and if it did not suffice them, they would wholly neglect the service of offerings; and on this account the sages made no complaint against them. And it is very surprising that they spoke only of vowed and freewill offerings, and there is no speech nor words [Psalms 19:4] about obligatory offerings of the community or of the individual; this is a sign that offering in their temple was not regarded as forbidden even by the sages in the land of Israel. It is plain that their aim was only to lay down a statute regarding vows and freewill offerings, so that their table should not be full and the table of the house of God in Jerusalem empty.{v1_07_17:1} Yet although, for the sake of wisdom and peace, they were compelled to agree to what they had no power to change, there is no doubt that they held a grudge in their heart. For this reason they used to penalize the priests who had served in the House of Onias, that they should not serve in the Temple. But this penalty too was made with fairness: they penalized them only in that they did not let them approach the altar of the Temple to offer, but they did not curtail their livelihood; rather, they shared and ate the holy things with their brothers the priests (Menaḥot 109a). Whether all these enactments that came to light in the days of Yoḥanan, some of which are mentioned specifically by his name, all came out with the agreement of all the sages of the Torah among the Pharisees, we are not told explicitly; but it seems that it was so. For one enactment that he made was against the will of the sages, and it is mentioned specifically that it was against their will, and Yoḥanan too retracted it on this account; this is a sign that they did not dispute his other enactments. This enactment is told as follows: The Greek kingdom issued a decree that the name of Heaven not be mentioned on their lips; and when the Hasmonean kingdom prevailed and defeated them, they enacted that the name of Heaven be mentioned even in documents, and they would write: "In year such-and-such of Yoḥanan, high priest to God Most High." There is no doubt that this enactment was made in the days of Yoḥanan, for before him, in the days of Simeon, it was not so. In the days of Simeon they wrote "In year such-and-such of Simeon being high priest," and did not write "to God Most High" (Graetz vol. 3, p. 470). And before Simeon they did not count by the years of the high priest; only then, when they appointed Simeon as nasi, did they establish and take upon themselves that all letters and notes be written in his name and that they count by the year of his rule (1 Maccabees ch. 14, and *Antiquities* 12, ch. 6⚠8). But before that, when the hand of the Greeks was strong over them, they wrote and counted by the Greek kings and wrote the name of the ruler in documents (Yadayim 4:8). Therefore the enactment to write "high priest to God Most High" came out in the days of Yoḥanan; but it was not to the liking of the sages, for when the sages heard of the matter they said: "Tomorrow this one pays his debt, and the document is found thrown on a dunghill," and they abolished the matter, and they made that day a festival (Rosh Hashanah 18b). This day was the third of Tishri, and it is mentioned among the days on which one may not eulogize or fast (Megillat Ta'anit ch. 6). Now without doubt the Pharisee sages agreed to all his other enactments. This agreement was not disturbed until the end of his life; but until that time he cleaved to the Pharisees and was a strong support for establishing religion on its original foundation according to the opinions of the Pharisees. Therefore on the whole the days of his reign were good for Israel also with regard to restoring religion to its former state, and the time was fit for the study of the Torah and its development; and then too the eshkolot [the pairs of sages], which had ceased at the death of Yose ben Yo'ezer, returned.

These eshkolot ["clusters," that is, the pairs] were Joshua ben Peraḥiah and Nittai the Arbelite; Joshua was Nasi and Nittai Av Beit Din (Ḥagigah 2:2). No halakhot are found in their name except one in the name of Joshua ben Peraḥiah: "Wheat that comes from Alexandria is unclean because of their antlia" (Tosefta Makhshirin ch. 3; R. Samson [of Sens] on the Mishnah there 6:2). For by means of the antlia, which is the bucket with which the Egyptians watered the fields from the waters of the Nile, and because the water was poured with human intent, the wheat became susceptible to impurity (*Erekh Millin*, p. 101). Therefore Joshua ben Peraḥiah was concerned about the wheat that came from there, lest it had become moistened by the water after it was already detached and so had become susceptible to impurity. But the Sages did not agree with him and were not concerned about this; rather they said: "If so, let it be unclean for Joshua ben Peraḥiah (since he is concerned about this, he may be stringent with himself) and clean for all Israel," for we are not concerned lest it became moistened.{v1_07_18:1} And indeed it is very possible that Joshua had a further, hidden intention in this. For although most of the traces that point to the house of Onias and to the opinion of the sages of the Land of Israel about it tell us that they raised no great objection against it and were accommodating toward it, willingly or unwillingly, still we find that this house had some opponents who went so far in their resentment of it that they did not grant it the sanctity of a house of the God of Israel (Menaḥot 109a). Now perhaps Joshua too was in his heart opposed to this house, and devised a remote concern in order to show that all the produce of the land of Egypt is of doubtful impurity, and therefore all their meal offerings that they would offer in the house of Onias are unclean. Apart from this halakhah nothing of his has survived in matters of halakhah, but there is from him a very precious moral saying (Avot 1): "Make for yourself a master and acquire for yourself a companion." In these words there is a warning needed for that time, in which the division of opinions in matters of religion and Torah was growing ever greater; and at such a time it was surely very necessary to rouse the hearts to expound the Torah and to cleave to it according to the views of the Pharisees. This is attained through study with a worthy master and through attachment to companions. These two principles that Joshua set up bear on their face the stamp of his love for the teaching of the Pharisees and of his effort to root it among his people. And he also set up a third principle, whose foundation lies in feelings of love for human beings; he used to say: "Judge every person on the scale of merit." We have no measure of kindness and love toward human beings greater than this. From Nittai the Arbelite too we have received a precious saying (also there), but this sage speaks with a very bitter soul. He used to say: "Keep far from an evil neighbor, do not associate with the wicked, and do not despair of retribution." And there was no time in which the warning against associating with the wicked was more fitting than the time of John Hyrcanus. For John himself, although his hand was with the Pharisees, nevertheless did not keep far from the evil neighbors, who were, in the view of the Pharisees, the Sadducean Jews; he associated with them and dealt with them in brotherhood. It is very likely that when Nittai warned to keep far from the evil neighbor and not to associate with the wicked, the conduct of John was before his eyes. For from a terrible event that happened at the end of John's days it appears that Nittai the Arbelite spoke well and foresaw what was to come.⚠9

King Yannai (that is, John the high priest, not Alexander Yannai) went to Koḥalit in the wilderness and conquered sixty towns there, and on his return he rejoiced greatly and called all the sages of Israel. He said to them: "Our fathers ate salted herbs when they were occupied with building the Temple; let us too eat salted herbs in memory of our fathers." And they brought up salted herbs on tables of gold. There was a man there, a scoffer, evil-hearted and worthless, and Eleazar ben Po'irah was his name (according to Josephus, Jonathan the Sadducee), and Eleazar said to Yannai: "King Yannai, the hearts of the Pharisees are against you." "And what shall I do?" "Stand up before them with the frontlet between your eyes." There was an elder there, and Judah ben Gedidim was his name (Josephus calls him Eleazar), and Judah ben Gedidim said to Yannai: "King Yannai, the crown of kingship is enough for you; leave the crown of priesthood to the seed of Aaron," for people said that his mother had been taken captive in Modi'im. The matter was investigated and not found [true], and the sages of Israel departed in anger (their anger was kindled at the words of the elder, who suspected Yannai of being the son of a captive woman without cause, and for this reason they passed sentence of lashes on Judah). And Eleazar ben Po'ira said to King Yannai: "King Yannai, a commoner in Israel, such is his judgment (for if he spreads an evil name about his fellow, his sentence is lashes), and you are king and high priest, is such your judgment? (Does not the strict measure of law dictate that the punishment of one who insults a person grows with the greatness of that person's rank?)" "And what shall I do?" "If you take my advice, trample them." "And the Torah, what will become of it?" "Behold, it is rolled up and lying in a corner; whoever wishes to learn may come and learn." And they killed all the sages of Israel, and the world was desolate until Simeon ben Shetaḥ came and restored the Torah to its former state (Kiddushin 66a).{v1_07_19:1} However, what is told here, that all the sages of Israel were killed, was not so according to the account of Josephus; he only tells that he separated from the Pharisees, abandoned their teaching and joined the Sadducees. This association was the cause that all the laws and statutes deriving from the Oral Torah were abolished. And not only did he abolish them, but he set a punishment on anyone who would follow the Pharisees. Therefore, from the end of John's days until the time of Simeon ben Shetaḥ the hand of the Pharisees was underneath, and the ways of their teaching were in mourning. Without doubt at that time he also removed Joshua ben Peraḥiah from his high office. And perhaps the words of Joshua (Menaḥot 109b) refer to that time: "At first, whoever told me, 'Go up to it' (to office), I would bind him and put him before the lion; now, whoever tells me to go down from it, I would pour a kettle of hot water on him." And it seems that at that time Joshua fled from the wrath of the government and went to Alexandria, where he stayed until the days of wrath were over, when Simeon ben Shetaḥ brought him back, as will be told in the next chapter. Thus this separation, by which John separated from the Pharisees at the end of his days, brought great harm to the exposition of the Torah. He removed the Pharisees even from the Sanhedrin and called Sadducees to their place, and all religious matters were decided by them; and this lasted a long time, for even after John's death the matter did not change. Aristobulus his son, who reigned after him, did not depart from his father's last ways of hating the Pharisees and siding with the Sadducees. This Aristobulus did not reign more than one year. His brother Alexander Yannai came to the throne after him, and he married the widow of Aristobulus, who was his yevamah [his brother's widow]. And although he was high priest, who may not marry a widow even when she is his yevamah (Yevamot 20a), he married her before he was appointed high priest. For when Aristobulus died, Yannai was still in the prison where his brother had shut him up with the rest of his brothers. But at his death the kingdom came into the hands of his wife Shelamzion, and she brought all her husband's brothers out of prison and bound herself to Yannai in the bond of marriage (Josephus, *Antiquities* 13, 11). Now without doubt she made this bond at once, before he was appointed high priest, and in that way a high priest is permitted to marry a widow (Yevamot, there). And perhaps this event was the reason for the sages to establish this halakhah and on its basis to permit Yannai to marry his brother's widow. We do not find that Yannai did harm to the Pharisees at the beginning of his reign. But neither was the Sanhedrin of Sadducees removed at the beginning of his reign, and every dispute and every plague still came before them, as it had from the end of John's days until then.
