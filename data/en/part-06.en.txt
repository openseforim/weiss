Book Two

The state of Torah study at the beginning of their return from the exile; the work of the Scribes in regard to the Hebrew language;

the sayings of the Scribes and their ordinances; the antiquity of the halakhah and its expansion; Simeon the Just, of the remnant of the Men of the Great Assembly.

Chapter Six

The state of Torah study at the beginning of their return from the exile.

About fifty years had passed since the day of the exile from the land (A.M. 3402)⚠1, and the signs that the prophets had foretold long before [Isaiah 41:23] about Babylon and its kingdom had come; not one of them had failed. Babylon, the glory of kingdoms [Isaiah 13:19], fell at the feet of the Persian conqueror; her pride was brought down to Sheol [Isaiah 14:11], and her king was slain among the slain. In this downfall Israel saw the end of its exile and hope for its redemption. Seeing that the Lord had fulfilled the word of His servants the prophets concerning Babylon and its kingdom, the hope grew strong in them that the vision which the prophets had seen for good concerning Judah and Jerusalem would also be fulfilled, and that the time of their deliverance was near. Indeed, the days did not grow long before Darius the Mede, under whose reign Babylon had been conquered, died, and Cyrus, who had conquered it, rose to the kingship in his place, and the kingdom of Babylon came under his sceptre. He was a kindly king to Israel. In the first year of his reign he gave them permission to go up to the land of their birth and to build a house to the Lord in Jerusalem. Then many of Israel made the effort to go up, and they went up. This ascent was the first step that "these, the people of the Lord" [Ezekiel 36:20] took to fulfil the great and holy destiny for which this people had been designated from of old: to be a banner to many nations. Deep, deep within them was sunk the memory of the hardships and sorrows that had passed over them for a long time for their walking in the abominable statutes of the nations and in ordinances by which one does not live [Ezekiel 20:25]. The memory of all this was a lesson to them, to make their latter end good. The idea of their mission filled the heart of the best of the nation, and it is engraved with an iron pen [Job 19:24] in all the holy words that remain to us as a blessing from the men of wonder who lived and worked in the exile. All the prophecies and words of comfort from that time depict this idea in the robes of sublime eloquence. Their desire and aim was not to establish a kingdom like the kingdoms of the earth, but to establish their holy religion and to give it standing and survival until the end of days. At the head of the first who went up stood Zerubbabel son of Shealtiel, of the seed of the house of David, and Joshua son of Jehozadak, the high priest. With them went up forty-two thousand three hundred and sixty men, besides the women and the children, and besides their menservants and maidservants (A.M. 3452). When they reached their land, all the people gathered to Jerusalem, and the heads rose up and built an altar to offer burnt offerings on it; but the house was not yet founded until the second year of their going up. Then days and years passed and they could not complete the building, for they had many adversaries among the people of the land, who brought an evil report of them to the kings of Persia. Above all the Kutim [Samaritans], whom the king of Assyria had settled in the cities of Samaria, harassed and denounced them. Therefore the building was held back until the second year of Darius the Persian (A.M. 3467). Then the prophets Haggai and Zechariah son of Iddo stirred their hearts to found the house anew. It seems that in the days of Darius their adversaries were not hostile to them, or achieved nothing with Darius, for he was a kindly king. Yet the prophets kept silent in the first year of his reign, because, as it appears, that year was a year of drought, and therefore it was not the right time for building the house (Haggai 1:6; 2:17). From the government too they obtained permission to complete the building, and with doubled strength they now worked at the holy work, and in the sixth year of the reign of Darius the building was completed. The completion was on the third of the month of Adar, and they at once made the dedication of the house with joy; without doubt they made it at the beginning of the month of Nisan, like the dedication of the altar in the Tabernacle. But in the sacrifices that they offered for the dedication of the house they did not do as is written, and they departed from Scripture both in the kinds of the sacrifices and in their number (Ezra 6:17). And indeed, even though the service returned to Zion and the sanctuary rose from the heaps of dust, the religious spirit that the prophets had sought had not yet returned, and many days still passed for Israel without Torah [2 Chronicles 15:3]; and no traces of Torah study at all are found in all the first days and years, from the day they went up in the first ascent led by Zerubbabel son of Shealtiel, governor of Judah, until the days of the reign of King Artaxerxes (A.M. 3523)⚠2, under whose reign the second ascent took place.

This work of reviving the spirit of religion among his people was reserved for one of the men of wonder, who was called to plant the plantings of the Torah in the vineyard of the Lord of hosts [Isaiah 5:7]. This exalted man was Ezra son of Seraiah, of the family of the priests, the fourteenth generation from Phinehas son of Eleazar son of Aaron the priest (Ezra 7:1–4). A multitude of honorable virtues were a crown of glory on his head; but one virtue surpassed them all [Proverbs 31:29]: his strength and power in the Torah of Moses, to teach the people statute and ordinance (ibid. 7:10). In the seventh year of the reign of Artaxerxes, Ezra obtained permission from the king to go up with a large number of his people to the land of Judah, to the inheritance of their fathers. When he spoke before the king to ask of him permission for his people to return to their land, he based his request only on the cornerstone of religion; his whole desire was only to bring his people back to the religion of their fathers and to the Torah. In the copy of the letter that the king gave into Ezra's hand, the purpose of the ascent is also set out: to appoint judges and officers who would judge between case and case in Jerusalem, for all who know the law of God, and to teach whoever does not know it (ibid. 7:25). For this reason too the later sages compared Ezra's ascent to Moses' ascent of Mount Sinai, for the purpose of both was to teach the people Torah (Sanhedrin 21b; Tosefta ibid., ch. 4). And they said of him that he founded the Torah a second time after it had been forgotten in his days (Sifre Ekev, piska 48; Sukkah 20a), and they raised his rank above that of Aaron the priest, until they said of him: had Aaron been alive, Ezra would have been greater than he in his time (Midrash Kohelet). Yet although Ezra was of the family of Phinehas son of Eleazar son of Aaron the priest, and a shoot from the stock of Zadok, of whose family none went astray when Israel went astray [Ezekiel 48:11], and he stood at the head of those who went up, with all the best of the house of Israel in his hand, nevertheless we do not find that the desire seized him to take for himself the high priesthood, which he could easily have filled, since every man of Israel followed him (see Song of Songs Rabbah on the verse "I arose"). But Ezra did a great thing in refraining from serving in the high priesthood. This is in keeping with what we have seen: that in all the generations the forgetting of the Torah came mostly through the fault of the priests. Therefore it was necessary to remove the chief causes that brought harm to the Torah and its study, and this could be achieved by working for its spread and for its study. And this effort could be strengthened only if the custody of the Torah were not reserved to the priests, as it had been in former days. The attainment of the aim of raising the horn of the Torah and strengthening religion was the goal of Ezra's life. In the keeping of the statutes of the Torah he saw the foundation for the survival of the nation, and therefore Ezra did not wish to reach his goal by virtue of being a priest, but by virtue of being a sage who had set his heart to seek the Torah of the Lord [Ezra 7:10]. Ezra the priest was called a scribe (sofer) because of his skill in the art of writing, and he used to write Torah scrolls to distribute them in Jacob [Genesis 49:7]. This virtue, of being a master of the art of writing, is a small thing in our eyes today, but in those days it was precious. There is no hint that before that time there were copies of the book of the Torah of Moses in the hands of the people. King Jehoshaphat sent men through the cities of Judah to teach the people Torah, and the envoys, the teachers, took with them the book of the Torah of the Lord (2 Chronicles 17:7–9). From this we can judge that no book was found in the hands of the people, for otherwise they would not have needed to carry a Torah scroll in their bosom from city to city in all the cities of Israel. And after this, in the days of King Josiah son of Amon, there was no copy even in the king's hand (2 Kings 22). But Ezra was a wonderful master of the art of writing and a ready scribe in the Torah [Ezra 7:6], and he did his work with speed and wonderful skill. And since this skill was found only among the sages, the sages too were therefore called by the name Scribes (Soferim), until the name ḥakham ("sage") and the name sofer ("scribe") were equal in their meaning. This name suits the teachers in another respect too: that they taught from the book.{v1_04_02:1} The masters of the Talmud gave the name Soferim yet another sense, and say that the sages of that generation were called Soferim because they counted (sofrim) the letters of the Torah, or because the Soferim made the Torah into counted sections (sefurot sefurot).{v1_04_03:1} But whether the Scribes of this time, with Ezra at their head, were the authors of such works still needs proof. It seems more likely that such explanations have only the value of aggadah, and that in this too they went their usual way of explaining matters or names according to their need on the basis of new concepts. In truth the name sofer means only one who writes or copies books, or one who learns from the book. The name sofer is a synonym of mevin ("one who understands") (1 Chronicles 26:32)⚠3; and mevin is a common epithet describing the teacher, and it is mentioned in contrast to talmid ("pupil") (ibid. 25:8). From this name we recognize the character of their teaching: that they taught with reflection, adding to the words of the book an explanation to make its matter understood. But when Ezra came to Jerusalem at the head of the second group that went up, the time was not yet fit to make the spread of Torah study the chief of his work, for the religious state that he found in the land of Judah was still bad; and the beginning of his work was to separate the people from the mixture with the peoples of the land, for many of Israel, even of the priests and the Levites, had mingled with them (Ezra 9). But many days still passed, and his desire did not prosper in his hand as he had hoped, until in the twentieth year of King Artaxerxes great help reached him. Nehemiah son of Hacaliah, who was cupbearer to the king, obtained permission to build the walls of Jerusalem and the ruined city. The king commanded him to be governor in the land of Judah. This man, who loved his people and feared God, stood at Ezra's right hand to support him in his holy desire. At that time (A.M. 3541), on the first of the seventh month, which is the Day of Remembrance, all the people assembled, and Ezra read before them in the book of the Torah of the Lord; and on the second day too the heads of the fathers' houses of all the people, the priests and the Levites, gathered to Ezra, and he instructed them in the words of the Torah (Nehemiah 8). The manner and way of their teaching was this: they read in the book of the Torah of God distinctly, as it is written and in its own language, and gave the sense with understanding and with fitting reflection, to explain it to the hearers, and caused them to understand the reading (ibid. 8:8). And indeed, included in the explanation and the understanding was also a translation of the book into the language to which part of the people was accustomed (Megillah 3a; Nedarim 37a). For many of the Jews who had grown up in Babylonia, and also the Jews who lived in the land of Judah, were accustomed to speak in the language of the peoples among whom they lived (ibid. 13:24). Already from ancient times it was the custom to read in public words of moral instruction and reproof from the prophets, and the reading of portions from the books of the prophets, which we call the haftarah, is very ancient.{v1_04_03:2} It is possible that at that time they began to read in the Torah on the days of assembly, on the Sabbaths and on the festivals of the year, and one would stand and translate. In any case it is clear that the great aim on which Ezra set his eye was to teach the people knowledge of the statutes of the Torah, so that they would know with right understanding the way in which they should walk. The way that Ezra and the Mevinim chose for themselves when they wished to interpret the books of the ancients we can recognize if we pay attention to some of our holy writings that were composed on the basis of written sources by authors who preceded them. For there are writings among the Holy Scriptures whose authors drew from old books, and they did not use these sources as written and in their own words, in the way of copyists, without investigation and criticism; rather, they reflected on the sources before them and expounded them according to their own opinions and according to the concepts of their time, or also according to the received meaning. A faithful witness to this will be the book of Chronicles that we have. The author of this book himself testifies in scattered places that he drew his narratives from old sources.{v1_04_04:1} These sources were written. Many were lost, and we do not know what became of them. Most of the Holy Scriptures that we have today were also among his sources, and in all the places where his sources are open before us as they were before him, we see that he departed from them in various ways and chose a way of his own in the sense of the deeds and events. Even where the Torah was the source of his narratives, he appears as one who interprets the words of the Torah. In several places he adds words to the words of the source, sometimes changes the wording, and expands the phrases, so that it appears that his intention is to interpret the sources from which he drew his words. And if we pay attention to the character of his additions, expansions and changes, by which he wished to give added explanation to the original source that lay before him, we easily find that he turned his attention to three kinds of interpretation. The first: the effort to reconcile apparent contradictions and to shed light on obscure passages.{v1_04_04:2} The second: that he describes the words of the ancient book according to his own opinion and understanding and according to the concepts of his time.{v1_04_04:3} The third: that he tries to explain the words of the ancient book by seeking out their reason and their cause.{v1_04_04:4} From this we can come to know the way in which they went at that time in their interpretations, and in what manner they used ancient books. For the author of the book of Chronicles lived and wrote his book in the third generation after Ezra.{v1_04_04:5} Therefore, when we see that this author was accustomed to go deep and reflect on the sources that lay before him, tried to reconcile the obscurities and the contradictions, expounded them according to his opinions and the concepts of his time, and gave a reason and a cause for a matter that the ancient source reported plainly and without a reason, so too we may judge rightly that the way of the sages of the first generations, who are the generations of the Mevinim and the Scribes, was of this kind: when they gave understanding of Scripture, their understanding of it was not only the explanation of the words, or a translation according to the plain sense of the words as written, in the way of the masters of Scripture; rather, they went deep into it, straightened and reconciled what was obscure and difficult, interpreted according to their own opinion and according to the concepts of their time wherever they had no received interpretation or where it did not suffice for their need, and tried to teach their hearers, and make them understand, the reason of the matters and their grounds.

The first traces of interpretations that have these characteristics are found in the Holy Scriptures that were written in the days of Ezra. A clear trace of this matter shines out from the things that Ezra himself relates.{v1_04_05:1} After he has told, in the book called by his name, the events⚠4 of his journey with those who went up, he relates further that the princes approached him, saying: "The people of Israel, and the priests and the Levites, have not separated themselves from the peoples of the lands, according to their abominations, of the Canaanites, the Hittites, the Perizzites, the Jebusites, the Ammonites, the Moabites, the Egyptians and the Amorites; for they have taken of their daughters for themselves and for their sons" (Ezra 9:1–2). In these words we see explicitly that what the Torah warned concerning intermarriage with the nations does not mean particular nations, namely the seven nations written in the Torah, but all the nations in general that act according to the abominations of the seven nations. So, without doubt, was the statute of the Torah that warns against intermarriage understood, and so was it interpreted at that time; for this is why they stated precisely that they had trespassed by not separating themselves from the peoples of the lands that act according to the abominations of the Canaanite, the Hittite and all the other nations against marriage with whom the Torah warned explicitly. It is an error on the part of those who say that Ezra made a new decree forbidding intermarriage with the other nations, and that it was not forbidden by the Torah.{v1_04_05:2} For if there had been no prohibition beforehand, for what reason did the princes complain against the people who had not separated themselves from the peoples of the lands? Had they not done nothing against the law? But the truth is that from of old the statute of the Torah was interpreted in this way: all the nations that act according to the abominations of the Canaanites are included in the prohibition, and the Torah spoke only of what was common. One document that was taken up into the book of Nehemiah, and whose author was without doubt Nehemiah himself, is full of religious and legal matters, and it gives us clear hints of the character of the interpretation of the Torah at that time. This document is the document of the making of the covenant, in which they made a sure covenant and wrote it, and to the sealing were the princes, the Levites, the priests and the rest of the people, who took upon themselves with a curse and with an oath to walk in the Torah of the Lord (Nehemiah 10:1–40). The later generations already held this document to be a book by itself, and in the course of time various wondrous aggadot were attached to it, which is a sign of how greatly it was prized in the nation, and also of its age (Ruth Rabbah 4). Now in this document come details of commandments and statutes, and from their description we recognize how they were understood by them and how they interpreted the words of the Torah with regard to these statutes and commandments.{v1_04_05:3} One of the statutes that they took upon themselves with a curse and with an oath is: to sanctify the Sabbath day. And it is said in particular that they took upon themselves not to take from the peoples of the land who bring wares and any grain on the Sabbath day to sell. And if we ask: was this act forbidden to them from the days of old, or did they decree it at that assembly? We find the answer in the words of Nehemiah himself, who said to those who bought wares on the Sabbath day: "What evil thing is this that you do, and profane the Sabbath day! Did not your fathers do thus, and were punished!" (Nehemiah 13:16–18). If this prohibition had not existed since long ago, how could he contend with them over it, and call this act "an evil thing," and say that their fathers too were punished for doing this evil thing? Therefore it is clear that this was not a new decree but a fixed statute from the days of old, whose traces are seen already in the words of the former prophets (Amos 8:8)⚠5 and of the latter ones (Isaiah 55:13)⚠6; and as in former times, so now too they understood the statute of the Torah thus: that to sanctify the Sabbath day the Israelite should restrain himself from doing his weekday business, even if it is not servile work.{v1_04_06:1} They further took upon themselves to bring the firstborn of their sons and of their cattle, as it is written in the Torah, and the firstborn of their herds and their flocks. Here we have before us three kinds of firstborn: the firstborn of man, the firstborn of cattle, and the firstborn of their flocks and herds. Yet he does not speak of the firstling of an ass, and instead he repeats the firstborn of cattle, which is very strange. This can only be because it is clear that in saying "the firstborn of cattle" he includes in this all kinds of cattle, even the unclean, or the unclean in particular; and in saying "as it is written in the Torah" he meant its saying (Numbers 18:15): "but the firstborn of man you shall surely redeem, and the firstling of unclean beasts." And he understood that "unclean beast" here includes every unclean beast, and that where it says elsewhere (Exodus 13:13), "and every firstling of an ass you shall redeem with a lamb," Scripture spoke only of what was common, since that was the kind of beast they had in their homes for their use, while other unclean beasts they did not raise in their homes because they were forbidden. And since Ezra and Nehemiah understood the statute of the Torah in this way, he rightly said in general terms "the firstborn of our cattle," for in truth, according to their understanding of the Torah, all beasts are subject to the law of the firstborn. Indeed, at a later time they departed from this sense (Bekhorot 5b) for a reason unknown to us; but it is clear that in Ezra's generation the statute was understood in the way we have said. The commandment of the tithe of the tithe too has a special character there. For in speaking of this commandment there is not even one word hinting that the tithe of the tithe shall be for the priest himself and for his needs. Rather he says (Nehemiah 10:38): "and the tithes of our ground to the Levites; and the Levites shall take the tithes in all the cities of our tillage. And the priest the son of Aaron shall be with the Levites when the Levites take tithes, and the Levites shall bring up the tithe of the tithes to the house of our God, to the chambers, into the treasure house." From all this it appears that the tithe of the ground is to be for the Levites, as it is written in the Torah (Numbers 18:11)⚠7, and from the tithe that is given to the Levites the Levites shall tithe their tithe; but this tithing is to be under the supervision of the priest, the son of Aaron. And after they have done this with exactness and precision and have set apart the tithe of the tithe, then the Levites shall bring up this tithe of the tithe, set apart under the priest's supervision, to the Temple, to the treasure house, and it is designated for the needs of the Temple. In their time this was very necessary, as is said there explicitly: "and we will not forsake the house of our God." And another prophet had already remarked on this (Malachi 3:10), saying: "Bring the whole tithe into the treasure house, that there may be food in my house." Now even if it be, as some of the sages of the Talmud hold, that they took this commandment upon themselves of their own accord, at any rate there is no reason to think that they took it upon themselves in another manner than that in which their fathers had been commanded concerning it by the Torah. And now, as we see that they enacted and fixed that the tithe of the tithe shall go to the treasure house and be designated for the needs of the Temple, without doubt this commandment of the Torah was understood by them in this way too. And when the Torah commanded the Levites, saying: "Thus you also shall set apart an offering to the Lord of all your tithes ... and you shall give of it the Lord's offering to Aaron the priest" [Numbers 18:28], the intention was not, in their view, that they should give the tithe of the tithe to the priest for himself and for his needs, but that they should give it for the needs of the Temple. And where it says that they shall give from the tithe the Lord's offering to Aaron the priest, it means that a tithe of the tithe shall be given to the priest and through him reach the treasury of the Temple, as they fixed in the days of Nehemiah: that the Levites shall set apart their tithe before the eyes of the priest, who shall watch over the precision and exactness of the setting apart, but the tithe thus set apart shall be brought to the treasury of the Temple.{v1_04_06:2} These few proofs give their testimony in part on the nature of the interpretations of the first Scribes and on their character. They show us that their way of understanding the Torah was a reasoned way, and that they did not go far from the plain sense of the verses. To occupy themselves with the interpretation of the Torah in this way was the holy charge they took upon themselves, for the Torah and the religion were for them the sure foundation on which to establish the happiness of the nation. To reach this aim, men of distinction, wise and God-fearing men of the generation, arose and joined together into a society that is called by the name of the Great Assembly. Over its beginning and the length of its existence a cloud still rests. The sages of both Talmuds set its beginning in the days of Ezra; only they differ in their opinions as to the number of its members: the Babylonians put their number at one hundred and twenty (Berakhot), and the Palestinians put it at eighty-five (Yerushalmi Megillah 1:5). But all agree in one opinion concerning the existence of this assembly, and that it began in the days of those who returned from the exile, and they know how to give a number for its members. And their opinion has a basis and root in the book of Ezra. For it is related there: when Ezra came up from the exile and found in the land of Judah great confusion in religious matters, and especially when he saw that they had mingled with the peoples of the lands and had married foreign women, then there were separated with Ezra men, heads of fathers' houses after their fathers' houses, and they sat down to examine the matter (Ezra 10:16). This was the first root of the Great Assembly. A few years later the assembly was completed and became a great assembly, ordered and arranged, and it enacted statutes and ordinances for its generation. This was about thirteen years after Ezra came up (A.M. 3544)⚠8, when Nehemiah came up with the king's permission, repaired the breaches of the city and rebuilt its ruins and its walls. And when they had finished the building, they met in an assembly and made a sure covenant and wrote it, and to the sealing were the princes, the priests and the Levites. All of them are designated by name, and their number, eighty-five, agrees with the number the Palestinians gave for the Great Assembly (*Moreh Nevukhei ha-Zeman*, gate 11). The opinion that this assembly began in the days of Ezra was so firm among the later sages that when they mentioned things found in the books of Ezra and Nehemiah they ascribed them to the Men of the Great Assembly (Berakhot 33b; Yerushalmi there 1:9; Genesis Rabbah 79; Ruth Rabbah 4), since in their view Ezra was the first and the head of this assembly. Since then we have become used to thinking that the Men of the Great Assembly and the Scribes are only one body under changing names. And in truth they are alike in their endeavor and in their chief charge, but for all that they were distinct in their activities. That is: the Scribes, who were called "those who give understanding," were occupied with the interpretation of the written statutes and founded laws and judgments on what followed for them from the received interpretations; it was also their task to give the people understanding and to teach it knowledge, and to make a safeguard for the Torah and to build a fence as they saw fit. The Men of the Great Assembly, however, were the members of the society that established these laws, which the Scribes derived from their interpretations, to be fixed and standing statutes in Israel in practice. Accordingly, the relation of the Scribes to the Men of the Great Assembly is like the relation of men of learning who found a religion, or who explain an old religion by the ways of learning and experience, whose activity and right is nothing but scholarly inquiry (theory), to the men who have the right of enactment and ratification, and to whom alone is given the authority to ratify and to establish the statutes that those founders and explainers taught, and by whose word alone, through their ratification and establishment, these become fixed statutes in practice. Now this matter throws light on the character of the charge that belonged to the Scribes and of that which belonged to the Men of the Great Assembly, and on how far these differed from those in their activities and their rights. For this reason it is never found that one says "an ordinance [takkanah] of the Scribes," for the right of enactment was not theirs; for the concept of takkanah denotes the fixing of a statute for a person's action, and this was not part of the Scribes' charge. Conversely, it is not found that the action of the Men of the Great Assembly is spoken of in any other term than "they enacted" [tiknu], for only they had the right of establishment and enactment. And since this is so, there is no doubt at all that the Men of the Great Assembly, being the heads of the nation, were surely the source of the rule that the Scribes were to keep in their work, and that they commanded them what the aim was that should stand before their eyes in their inquiry. This rule has been preserved for us in the Mishnah (Avot 1:2)⚠9: "Be deliberate in judgment, raise up many disciples, and make a fence for the Torah." From the character of these sayings we recognize that they are not meant for the multitude of the people, for judgment and law do not belong to the people, nor are they called upon to raise up disciples, nor is it for them to make a fence for the Torah. Rather, the Men of the Great Assembly prepared and directed these words to "those who give understanding," that is, the Scribes, to be for them a line and a measure by which to keep their charge. And they are in truth the sum of all the chief foundations for reaching the aim that the Scribes set before themselves.

We have heard various complaints that many make against the work of Ezra: that he did not lift his soul to the sublime thoughts of the earlier prophets regarding the great and rational foundations of the Israelite religion, and that he made his first aim the founding and establishing of practical statutes. But if we consider the state of the people at that time and the level of its education, we find that these complaints are simply false. The multitude of the people, who stood at the lowest level of education, had not yet prepared their hearts to rise to the lofty thought that is the goal of the Torah and the religion. But Ezra recognized in his wisdom that the first cause of their bad religious state, and of their inability to rise from their low standing, was their lack of knowledge of the Torah. And he saw that his first duty was to make up this lack. He had no other desire but to spread Torah in Israel, so that they would know the judgment of its statutes and attain its truths. And his desire prospered in his hand, though not all at once but little by little; and from the day he began to teach statute and judgment in Israel, another spirit too was in the people, and a new light shone upon them. This light went on growing brighter for many generations. Then, as the times changed, the studies also changed and went in ways that were not imagined in the days of Ezra, as will be revealed and shown to our eyes in the coming chapters.
