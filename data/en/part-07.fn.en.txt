v1_04_13:1) See Rashi on Nedarim at the place mentioned, who explains nothing about the word soferim; it seems that he explains the word soferim as the reading of teachers in general, that is, the customary and usual reading. Likewise he does not explain, about ittur soferim, what is meant by the word soferim; and perhaps he too understood it in the way we wrote in the text, and not of the particular Scribes. The Rosh also explains nothing at all about the word soferim, and the Tosafot do not explain it either. But I have already shown in the text that there is no doubt about this, that the intent is clearly the ancient Scribes. So too the Ran understood it in his explanation of miqra soferim, and so the explanation of the *Arukh* on ittur soferim. Whoever considers the words of R. Isaac there in the Gemara will easily find that he was not the inventor of these expressions, but they were very old and he explains them. And because they refrained from deciding that the books had become corrupt at any time, they tried to explain these expressions in various directions by way of aggadic midrash; and so he explained that all of it is a halakhah [given] to Moses at Sinai. But for the purpose before us such explanations have no power to bring us out of the confusion, because of the contradictions in them, and also because this is no foundation on which we can build in our inquiry into antiquities.

v1_04_14:1) From this: tishgalenah, which they read tishkavenah; sheneihem [their urine], and we read mei ragleihem; ḥoreihem, read tzo'atam; maḥara'ot⚠8, read motza'ot (Megillah 25b), and in Tractate Soferim ch. 9; and all this is in order to speak in clean language.

v1_04_14:2) Wherever ra'ah is found in the Nif'al, it is only with the relational preposition, in combination with el, al or le-. Therefore, wherever the root ra'ah is used with et ha-Shem or et Elohim, the text had the Qal, and they corrected the reading to the Nif'al out of reverence. And we found "When shall I come and appear before God [penei Elohim]" (Psalms 42), and the Nif'al here too is a correction of the Scribes. And we find "appears unto God [el Elohim]" (ibid. 84), and it appears from this that this psalm is late, since the author himself was already careful and wrote yera'eh in the Nif'al and in combination with the relational preposition.

v1_04_15:1) The commentators on Scripture inquired into the root of the qeri and ketiv. R. David Kimḥi and Don Isaac Abravanel wrote in agreement with what we have written, and the author of the introduction to the *Mikra'ot Gedolot*, Venice printing, cried out like a crane against them; but in all his words we see no proof and no just judgment, only noise. And see also the *Efodi*, ch. 6⚠9, Vienna printing.

v1_04_15:2) The intention is to some of the places which according to the Mekhilta, the Tanḥuma and Midrash Rabbah are corrections of the Scribes. For in some of them the law is with them. One of them is 2 Samuel⚠10 3:13, "for the iniquity which he knew, because his sons cursed themselves [lahem]." On this the authors of the midrash said that it is a correction of the Scribes; and in the opinion of the commentators lo [him] was written in place of lahem; but this is not the intention of the midrash; rather Elohim was written, and they omitted the alef and yod out of reverence. And perhaps in other places, where Scripture tells of a curse with which one of the persons cursed God, Scripture turns its language from a curse to a blessing, as "Naboth blessed God" (1 Kings 21:13), "bless God and die" (Job 1:9⚠11), and others besides; perhaps these too are corrections of the Scribes. "And curse by his king and by his god" (Isaiah 8:21) they did not correct, because it refers to the idols, not to the God of Israel, as in the Targum Jonathan and all the Jewish commentators; and the commentators who take it the other way are mistaken.

v1_04_15:3) I mean the verse "and Abraham stood yet before the Lord" (Genesis 18:22), where too the words of the midrash are right that this is a correction of the Scribes, the Men of the Great Assembly. For according to the sequence of the passage it is clearly proved that it should be "and the Lord stood yet before Abraham," but they corrected it out of reverence toward on High.

v1_04_15:4) I already hinted above, in ch. 3, in a note, that in a number of the psalms they made additions for the needs of their time, when they used them in the Temple. And it seems that they also corrected and took away. The psalm Tehillah le-David [Psalm 145] was in my opinion one of the psalms they used in their prayer in very early times, and therefore the sages of the Talmud gave it great value for every early prayer⚠12. The psalm is built on the alphabet, and the letter nun is missing from it, and in the Talmud they gave the reason that it contains the downfall of Israel. But this reason is only an aggadic homily; the main point is that the verse with the letter nun was not suited to their purpose, namely for prayer, so they omitted it. And in the psalm "I will bless the Lord at all times" [Psalm 34] it appears clearly that the connection is very difficult between the verse "The face of the Lord is against them that do evil" etc. and the verse after it, "They cried, and the Lord heard" etc.: to whom does it refer in saying "they cried"? And who are those who cry? But it appears that the last verse, "The Lord redeems the soul of His servants" etc., was the letter pe in the psalm, and was written in place of "The face of the Lord is against them that do evil," so that "they cried, and the Lord heard" refers, that is, to His servants who trust in Him. But so as not to end with an evil thing, "Evil shall slay the wicked" etc., they ended with the verse "The Lord redeems," and added the verse "The face of the Lord," although it has no proper connection with what follows. And I heard that R. Solomon Judah Rapoport also remarked on this. Other examples like these are found, and it is not marvelous nor far-fetched if I say that this was the work of the Scribes, since they were the enactors of the earliest prayers, and they also made other corrections in Holy Scripture. R. Abraham Geiger has already dealt at length with this point in his book *Urschrift*, but he exceeded the measure; whoever wishes to understand the matter and enter into its depth should look there, and for us, for the sake of the work before us, what we have written is enough.
