Chapter Thirteen

The Pharisees, the Sadducees, and the Essenes.

Behold, we have seen so far that the time after the victory of the Hasmoneans was fit for widening the matters of religion, and the events compelled it. For this reason the Oral Torah grew and multiplied like a plant. Many ordinances were instituted and many decrees were decreed to make a fence for the Torah, and also many laws and statutes were newly made and learned from the midrash of the verses. And from the account of the events it has become known to us that already in the days of the early Elders the midrash of the Torah began to sprout in such a way that a slight departure from the sense of the words as written could already be seen in it. And all this came from their wish to give a source and a foundation to their traditions and also to their halakhot. The results of this matter, and the results of the events that befell them, were various divisions, by which the members of the nation were divided in their opinions and in several details of belief, until they split into three sects. We see in every religious community that at times some incline away from others in their opinions, for various reasons. There are people who place the glory of every spiritual and scientific statement in the words of the statutes of that religion, and utterly reject reflection and contemplation on the book of statutes of that religion, until they go to the last extreme, not to step even one step beyond the sense of the words as written in the book. And the reason for this is mostly that they see that some light-minded people went astray according to their understanding of the written statutes of the religion, and contemplation became a stumbling block for them, and the departure from the plain sense became a departure from the religion. Over against this we find in every religious community people who go in keeping the religion beyond the bounds of the judgments of their co-religionists, and forbid to themselves what the religion permits, and add stringency upon stringency, until by this they separate themselves from the company of their co-religionists and from their judgments and their customs, and are solitary in their thoughts and in their deeds, and think that by this they will attain nearness to God. And the reason for this always lies in an inclination to superstition and false belief; but what brings about this belief and the inclination to it is mostly the misfortunes of the time. For the hardships and troubles that pass over the heads of men always prepare their hearts to incline after vain beliefs. Such traits, which are in the human soul in general, grew very strong within the Israelite nation. On the one side, the trouble that passed over the head of the holy people like a scourge, "as destruction from the Almighty shall it come" [Isaiah 13:6], worked so that many went to excess in the quality of piety, and despised the pleasures of temporal life, and separated themselves from the community in their deeds, in their customs, and in a piety that passed the bounds. And likewise, on the other side, there were many among the people whose soul did not accept interpretations of the Torah that do not accord with their plain meaning and with the sense of the words as written. Because they saw that many of those who were drawn after the opinions of the Greeks, and interpreted the Torah in agreement with those opinions, went astray in understanding the Torah in its plain sense, they therefore rejected every interpretation that departs from the sense of the words as written, and cast behind their back all interpretations of this kind, and with them also the interpretations of the sages in general, since these too depart from the sense of the words as written. With these opponents sided most of the leading men among the people and the men of wealth. For it is a known thing that the leading men and the men of wealth were never among those who love to widen the borders of enlightenment; and besides this, through the rejection of the interpretations of the sages and of the Oral Torah in general, a benefit grew for them, for they could break the bands of the stringencies, loosen the knots of the decrees, and cast away the cords of the statutes of the Oral Torah; therefore they joined with a willing soul the sages who rose up against the tradition. And these two tendencies quickly became sects, until the people divided into three sects, namely Pharisees, Sadducees, Essenes.

Pharisees was the name of all the adherents of the Jewish religion who in the foundations of their faith followed the Oral Torah. Those designated by this name were the majority of the adherents of the Jewish religion. Already at the very beginning, after they came up from the exile, they found among the inhabitants of the land two kinds of Jews who differed in their religious practice. The one kind were the Jews who had not separated themselves from the uncleanness of the peoples of the land (Ezra 9:1). The other kind were those who feared and trembled at the word of the Lord, and they separated themselves from the peoples of the lands to the Torah of God (Nehemiah 10:29). Perhaps already at that time the name nivdalim ["those who separated themselves"] was a special designation for all who had the Lord in their heart, and afterwards this name was turned into its Aramaic translation, namely perushim, for in their sense nivdalim and perushim are one (Geiger, *Urschrift*, p. 103). Therefore the reason why they were called by this name was that they conducted themselves in separation [perishut], and this name became a fixed designation for all who followed the Pharisees in custom and in faith. The core of their separation was guarding against uncleanness. In their opinion the reason for the prohibition of forbidden foods and of all the things that the Torah forbade is that they defile; and therefore they used to guard their ordinary food [ḥullin] from uncleanness and ate it in purity (Ḥagigah 18b). The more scrupulous kept yet another separation and kept away from the ammei ha-aretz, not eating with them or of their food, because at that time these were suspected about the gifts due to the priests and the Levites and held their produce untithed. Because of this the Pharisees all resembled a single association, and each and every member of the association was called a ḥaver. The association was not closed and shut; it was open to every person of Israel, only on condition that he take upon himself the obligations of membership before three. In respect to this acceptance a scholar and an am ha-aretz were equal. The kinds of membership were two: for "wings" and for "purities." One is accepted for "wings" and afterwards for "purities" (Tosefta Demai ch. 3; Bekhorot 30b). "For wings" means care against the uncleanness of the hands, for "wings" [kenafayim] is a designation for the hands; and [the uncleanness of the] hands is a decree of the sages for holy things and for terumah (Shabbat 14b), and afterwards they decreed also for ordinary food, that it requires washing of the hands (Ḥullin 106a). "For purities" means care in all one's other foods, to prepare them in purity, so that we may hold them to be in complete purity and eat of them without apprehension. R. Judah further tells of the marks of the ḥaverim and of the things by which they were to be distinguished: that one should not raise small cattle, should not be unrestrained in vows and in levity, should not defile himself for the dead, and should serve in the house of study (Demai 2:3). Now the ḥaverim were all Pharisees, but they conducted themselves in their separation with the measure of piety [ḥasidut]. But not all the Pharisees were ḥaverim. The qualities of the true Pharisees were honorable. They served the Lord out of love and with all their soul (Yerushalmi Berakhot 9:7). They believed that the eyes of the Lord range over everything and that He will bring the deeds of men into judgment, for man does his deeds by free choice and therefore must give an account of them before his Creator; but on the other side they believed also in a divine decree (Josephus, *Antiquities* 13), and this concerning the nation as a whole (Graetz, vol. 3, p. 89) or mankind as a whole, as in what they said about the power of rain (Ta'anit 9a). And they taught that even if reward and punishment do not come on the heels of the deed, so that there are doers of good whom no good reward reaches for their toil and there are doers of evil to whom it is not done according to the deserving of their hands, nevertheless one is not to despair of them, for there is judgment and account after death (Kiddushin 39b); and He keeps His faith with those who sleep in the dust, to revive the dead at the end of days, "some to everlasting life, and some to shame and everlasting contempt" [Daniel 12:2]. They were very, very deliberate in judgment and judged every sinner in the scale of merit (Josephus, ibid.), as is clearly evident from all their laws on matters of bodily punishment. Their practice was to behave with humility toward every person, to receive people with a cheerful countenance, and to do kindness as far as their hands could reach and beyond their ability; therefore they were beloved by the people, and the heart of all Israel was after them. There is no need to list here all the precious qualities that have been attributed to them; from their teachings, spread through all the books of the Oral Torah, we can judge their own qualities and the character of their lives. However, it happened to them as it happens to all the upright in all times: the purity of their thought is not recognized in the eyes of the hypocrites, and these say of them that they do their deeds deceitfully. For this reason too we find that the slanderers of the Pharisees were many. Yet this too cannot be denied: that there were in truth among them people who followed them in deed but whose intention was not pure. And who does not know that this is found in all sects of men: even if all the members of the sect are alike in their deeds, they are not alike in the intention that moves the deed. The Pharisees themselves did not hide it under their tongue that there are many among them who are called by the name Pharisees whose intention is not acceptable. They counted several kinds of Pharisees who were Pharisees only for the sight of the eyes, for the sake of gain, or out of love of honor, or for some other motive, but their inside was not as their outside, and they did their deeds deceitfully (Yerushalmi Berakhot 9:7; Bavli Sotah). Such frauds, who make a trade of the fear of Heaven, existed in every generation, and in the days of Alexander Yannai they already had a special name and were called "painted ones" [tzevu'im] (Sotah, ibid.).

The opponents of the Pharisees were the Sadducees. We have already seen above the time and the cause of their separation from the Pharisees. We saw also that the separation was not, first and foremost, for the sake of a political aim in particular; rather, the core of the separation was a difference of opinions on the keeping of the religion. But since the opinions and the ways of the Sadducees were better suited to ease the actions of the men charged with the government of the state, so that they would not stumble in their course over obstacles on the side of religion, since they did not hold to the decrees and to many of the statutes and laws that the Pharisees taught and instructed, it is self-evident why the notables and the men of wealth followed the Sadducees. For the notables of the people were close to the government and were its leaders, and the teaching of the Pharisees was more of a burden to them than to the rest of the people. And when the support of the Sadducees grew strong through the notables of the people who followed them, then too the separation widened more and more, for the will of the notables became powerful over them, and this will turned their opinions wherever it pleased. The core of the Sadducees' opposition was that they denied the Oral Torah. They said that only what is written explicitly in the Torah has binding force, not what the elders taught (Josephus, ibid., 13:10). Even though from all that we know of the opposition of the Sadducees at the time when their dispute with the Pharisees began one cannot judge with certainty that the core of their opposition was the denial of tradition, nevertheless it is so; for we have no reason to make Josephus, who testifies to this, a deliberate deceiver. Although in many places he exaggerates in his narratives, adding or subtracting for some reason, it is impossible that he would invent from his own heart a thing that never was and never happened, at a time when every one of his contemporaries could contradict him. Now if the Sadducees of his generation said that only what is explicit in the Torah has binding force, and not what the elders taught, why should we not say that such was the opinion of the Sadducees from the start and from their beginning? Especially when we see that in all the matters in which the Sadducees opposed the Pharisees, we find that the Sadducees hold to things as they are written. And from the Talmud it follows explicitly that there was agreement between the Sadducees and the Pharisees only in matters of which we can say, "Go, read it in the schoolhouse" [zil kerei bei rav]. What lies in the words as they are written, only that was a thing the Sadducees admitted; and what follows from the midrash of the sages is a thing the Sadducees do not admit (Sanhedrin 33b; Horayot 4a). And because of their denial of tradition they also denied the resurrection of the dead, since it has no mention in the Torah of Moses. From the disputes that the Pharisees had with them at all times we see that they did not believe in the resurrection of the dead. Once it is put in their mouth that they asked, as if in wonder: "From where [do we know] that the Holy One, blessed be He, revives the dead?" (Sanhedrin 90a), and once we find that they said to the Pharisees: "You say that those who lie down will live," or: "You say that the dead will live." Such disputes are certainly faithful witnesses that their denial of the resurrection of the dead was a well-known thing. Likewise we find that the Sadducees mocked the Pharisees for their belief in the world to come (Sukkah 48b) and for their saying that the souls of the righteous are stored away under the Throne of Glory (Shabbat 152b), from which it appears that they denied even the survival of the soul after death. True, all this is told of the Sadducees after the destruction; at all events it is proven from this that the Sadducees denied the resurrection of the dead, and there is no reason to decide that in this matter the later Sadducees differed from their predecessors. The aggadah in the midrash (Avot de-Rabbi Natan ch. 5) also tells that they denied another world and the resurrection of the dead, and mocked the Pharisees, saying: "They afflict themselves in this world, and in the world to come they have nothing." And even if it is true that this story is an aggadic figure of speech, it cannot in any way be denied that its root is true and firm, namely: that the Sadducees denied the resurrection of the dead. Especially when we see that Josephus too states this with certainty. And likewise we find it written in the writings of the Gospels (Matt. 23:22⚠1, and in many places). Therefore, after all these reports, there is no longer any doubt about what we have received from our predecessors, that the Sadducees did not believe in another world and in the resurrection of the dead.

Of the matters of their opposition in the interpretation of the Torah twelve items remain. They are these: (a) The Sadducees interpreted "the morrow after the Sabbath" [Leviticus 23:11], said of the offering of the omer, as referring to the Sabbath of Creation, so that Shavuot always falls after the Sabbath (Menaḥot 65a⚠2; *Megillat Ta'anit* ch. 1). (b) They said that an individual may volunteer and bring the daily offering (Menaḥot and *Megillat Ta'anit*, ibid.), for they took the words of the Torah as written, as it is said, "The one lamb shalt thou offer in the morning" [Numbers 28:4], in the singular. (c) They said that the incense burned on the Day of Atonement should be prepared outside: he puts the incense on the fire and afterwards brings it in (Yoma 53a; Sifra Aḥarei ch. 3), for they expounded "for I will appear in the cloud upon the ark-cover" [Leviticus 16:2] to mean that he should enter only in the cloud of the smoke of the incense, which already raises a cloud before he enters within. Others add that they also brought a proof from an a fortiori argument: if one does so for flesh and blood, all the more so before the Omnipresent (Yerushalmi Yoma 1:6). (d) Of the preparation of the red heifer the Sadducees said that it was done by those on whom the sun had set (Parah 3:6), because they explained that what the Torah said, "a man that is clean" [Numbers 19:9], is said only of one on whom the sun has set and whose purification is complete, for then he is a clean man. (e) The Sadducees ate the meal offering of cattle (*Megillat Ta'anit* ch. 8), that is, the meal offerings that come with the animal sacrifice (Menaḥot 6:2). (f) They interpreted the verse "eye for eye" [Exodus 21:24] as an actual eye (*Megillat Ta'anit* ch. 4), and with this interpretation R. Eliezer ben Hyrcanus also agrees (Bava Kamma 84a). (g) They said that a plotting witness is not put to death until the one against whom he testified has been put to death, as it is said, "life for life" [Deuteronomy 19:21] (Makkot 5a); and necessarily they explained the word zamam ["he plotted"] as referring only to the wicked deed [itself], in the sense of zimmah, as the Septuagint translated it. (h) They said that the daughter inherits together with the son's daughter, and their proof was from an a fortiori argument (Bava Batra 115b; *Megillat Ta'anit* ch. 8). (i) They held [the master] liable for damages done by a male or female slave, by an a fortiori argument (Yadayim ch. 4). (j) The Sadducees declared the unbroken stream [nitzok] unclean: that is, if one pours from a clean vessel into an unclean vessel, the water in the clean one is made unclean by the water poured into the unclean vessel, which is unclean because of the unclean vessel (ibid. 4:7). But it is not explained on what they relied.{v1_07_04:1} (k) "And spit in his face" [Deuteronomy 25:9], said of the levirate widow, is in their opinion actually in his face.{v1_07_04:2} (l) They said that "and they shall spread the garment" [Deuteronomy 22:17] (said of one who brings an evil name [upon his bride]) is meant as written, an actual garment (*Megillat Ta'anit* ch. 4); with this interpretation R. Eliezer ben Jacob agreed (Ketubbot 46a). These are the items specified in which the Sadducees departed from the interpretations of the Torah given by the Pharisees. We should not think that they departed only in these and not in others; for in truth they departed from everything that the Pharisees interpreted and expounded by the ways of their midrash where the matter is not explicit in the Torah (Sanhedrin 33b; Horayot 4b). But sometimes the difference of opinions was in interpretations received of old, and sometimes it was in doubtful interpretations.{v1_07_05:1} We find also that the Sadducees agreed with the opinion of the Pharisees in the interpretation of Scripture even where the plain sense of Scripture may be interpreted otherwise. So in the commandment of tefillin, where the verse bears two interpretations: either we say that "and it shall be for a sign upon thy hand, and for frontlets between thine eyes" [Exodus 13:16] is meant figuratively, as many expressions like it are found in Scripture that are interpreted only figuratively, and this is the plain sense of Scripture, for such is the character of all Eastern languages, to speak in figures and images; or we say that it refers to an actual sign to be placed on the hand and between the eyes, as in the midrash of the Pharisees. In the commandment of tefillin the Sadducees agreed with the opinion of the Pharisees, and of this commandment it was said that it is one in which the Sadducees conceded to the midrash of the Pharisees (Shabbat 108a). Perhaps the reason is that it seemed better to them to agree with the Pharisees in their midrash than to interpret the verse figuratively, which was the way of interpretation of the Alexandrians and of all who followed Greek wisdom. We have already seen that in several interpretations attributed to them they always refrained from interpreting figuratively, as in their interpretations of "the morrow after the Sabbath," "and spit in his face," "and they shall spread the garment," "eye for eye," all of which they interpreted literally as written. And there are very many interpretations alluded to in the midrashim of our sages which the sages decisively refuted and rejected, and these are midrashim of the Sadducees, even if their name is not mentioned with them. Whoever considers the ways of midrash and the rule of its inquiry will find that it was not enough for the masters of midrash to bring the interpretation that is the main one in their view; rather they first debate and bring the possible interpretations and refute them, and at the end decide on their interpretation, which is in their view the true one. When they do so, their way is to use special expressions, saying: "One might think [yakhol] that it is proper to expound thus and thus," and they bring a proof that this exposition does not hit the truth, and expound in another way. Sometimes they bring the exposition that is true in their view and debate the matter, saying that perhaps it is not so but the meaning of the verse is thus and thus, and they use the expression "or is it not [o eino]," and they bring their decision to light by a proof that only their exposition is the true one. Now in some of the interpretations of the Sadducees that the Pharisees brought in their midrashim and refuted, they also use these expressions, "one might think, or is it not," as we see in their midrash on "the morrow after the Sabbath" and "eye for eye," of which they say these expressions, namely: "One might think that we interpret the words as written," as the Sadducees interpreted. Now is it not possible that in other places too, where the Pharisees used these expressions, saying "one might think that we interpret thus and thus," or saying "or is it not but thus and thus," is it not possible that, if these possible interpretations are interpretations of the words as written, they are the very interpretations that the Sadducees gave?

The matters of opposition to tradition that do not concern the interpretation of the Torah are six in number. (a) The water libation on the Festival: the Sadducees did not concede it (Sukkah 48b). (b) They did not concede that the beating of the willow overrides the Sabbath (Sukkah 43b; Tosefta ibid. ch. 3); and without doubt they did not concede at all the commandment of the willow, to circle the altar, since it has no proof from the Torah, just as they did not concede the water libation (Graetz, vol. 3, p. 515); and a proof of the matter is that they let it override the Sabbath in order to make known that it is from the Torah (Sukkah, ibid.). (c) They objected, [holding] that the Holy Scriptures do not render the hands unclean, whereas the Pharisees say that they do (Yadayim 4:6). (d) They objected to the ordinance of the Pharisees, who ordained that one write in a bill of divorce "according to the law of Moses and Israel"; they said that this is not proper, since in a bill of divorce one writes the date by the year of such-and-such a king, and it is not in keeping with the honor of Moses that his name be written together with the name of the ruler in a bill of divorce (Yadayim, ibid. 4:8). (e) They did not concede the eruv, and they denied the prohibition of carrying on the Sabbath (Eruvin 6:1). (f) They were very, very lenient in the prohibition of the menstruant, and already with regard to the early Sadducees the Pharisees were concerned about the uncleanness of the menstruant (Niddah 33b). Besides these there is a record of one more opposition, namely that the Sadducees were not pleased with the practice of the Pharisees with regard to the sanctification of the new month. It is not told on what side they objected, or which of the Pharisees' practices was evil in their eyes; it is only told that once the Boethusians acted corruptly (Rosh Hashanah 2:1), wanted to mislead the sages, and hired false witnesses (Bavli ibid. 22b, and Yerushalmi ibid., and Tosefta ch. 1). From this it appears clearly that the practice of the sages in the sanctification of the month was evil in their eyes, but the particular practice that did not please them has already been forgotten in the length of time. But for all their opposition they did not have the strength to stand by their opinion and follow it in their deeds at a time when the hand of the Pharisees was strong over them. Once the wife of a Sadducee said to the high priest: "Although we are wives of Sadducees, we fear the Pharisees and show [our] blood to the sages" (Niddah 33b, and Tosefta ch. 5); and likewise a certain Sadducee said to his son: "Although we are Sadducees, we fear the Pharisees" (Yoma 19b; Tosefta ibid. ch. 1). Now from the few matters of the Sadducees' opposition that remain to us in detail, we see that a number of laws and statutes arose in the course of time because of their opposition; and this was because the sages of the Pharisees wanted to make an impression, in order to make known that one should not act according to the opinion of the Sadducees. For as the Sadducees increased their opposition to the teachings of the Pharisees, to their midrashim and their fences, so they too added yet more safeguards upon safeguards to subdue the Sadducees; and so in the course of time they heaped up the measure too much, especially in matters of uncleanness and purity, until once they immersed the candelabrum and the Sadducees mocked them and said: "See the Pharisees immersing the orb of the sun" (Yerushalmi Ḥagigah 3:8). With the priest who burns the red heifer too they made a public display, rendering him unclean deliberately, to show [their stand] against the opinion of the Sadducees. Likewise they made it a statute that the commandment of the willow overrides the Sabbath. And several statutes were introduced concerning the reaping of the omer and its offering, all because of the complaint of the Sadducees; and for this reason they expounded and inquired to fix the day of the festival of Shavuot on the sixth of the third month, and sought many calculations until they found that on the very day when the forty-nine days, the seven weeks between Passover and Shavuot, are completed, there was also the day of the giving of the Torah; and they deliberately ordained that one say in the prayer "the season of the giving of our Torah," in order to make a public stand against the Sadducees, who say that Shavuot falls after the Sabbath. For this reason too we find that the sages gave greater stringency to the words of the Scribes than to the words of the Torah (Sanhedrin 9:5⚠3), and from this came the rule: the sages made their words stronger than those of the Torah (Ketubbot 84a). Now there is no doubt that a number of laws in the conditions of the commandments were founded and introduced for this reason and for the need of their time, to subdue the Sadducees with them; and it is clear to us from this that it was never the intention of the sages of the Pharisees to load a burden on the people, but it was the Sadducees, they and no others, who forced them to it.{v1_07_06:1}

The third sect is the sect of the Essenes. Their root is the true piety [ḥasidut] that took root in the nation from the time of their return from the exile and reached its perfection at the beginning of the wars against Antiochus Epiphanes, from the time when they groaned under the government because of the upheaval.⚠4 Then with doubled strength they held fast to every religious matter, to sanctify the Lord God of Israel. However, just as piety founded on uprightness of mind and heart is the fitting and proper degree to bring a man to the perfection of his soul, so also piety bound up with confusion and muddle of thought has from of old turned many of the pious away from the goal of perfection and caused entanglement of the soul and its folly. Now if we consider the character of the Essenes as the chroniclers who knew their deeds depict them, we find that all their deeds and opinions do not fit the purposes of human society and are not grounded in the intent of the Torah; rather they oppose the final purpose on the face of the earth and the world, which is the repair of the world and the joining of all human beings together in one moral outlook, so that we may say: if all who come into the world were to act as they do and incline their hearts to their opinion and their way, the world would not endure even one hour. True, their root was the true piety whose foundation is the keeping of the Torah and its study; but this root quickly rotted, for they left the middle way. For piety is a precious quality while it keeps its measure and its bound. But these Essenes overstepped the bound, and their piety flooded with force, and as it flooded it carried along with it in its course foolishness and the bearing of vain burdens and delusions, until they were regarded in the eyes of those who saw them as people driven by madness. The most fitting designation for them is what the sages of the Mishnah called them, namely: a foolish pietist [ḥasid shoteh]. And they said of them that this pietist is among those who destroy the world (Sotah 20a). There is also no doubt that those whom the sages of the Mishnah call by the name "dawn bathers" [tovelei shaḥarit], of whom it is said that they disputed with the Pharisees, refers only to the Essenes. Here a polemical conversation between the dawn bathers and the Pharisees is told. The dawn bathers say: "We complain against you, Pharisees, that you mention the Name in the morning without immersion." The Pharisees say: "We complain against you, dawn bathers, that you mention the Name from a body in which there is uncleanness" (Tosefta Yadayim ch. 2). From this we see that the dawn bathers were a special association whose custom was to immerse every morning; and it is also true that this custom was found especially among the Essenes; therefore this conversation was certainly between the Pharisees and the Essenes, and it follows for us from this that the way of these pietists did not please the Pharisees. Already at the beginning of the war of the Hasmoneans the first pietists [ḥasidim ha-rishonim] risked their lives to fight for the freedom of Israel and the freedom of the religion. But although the degree of their piety was great and lofty, they nevertheless did not go beyond the bound with it, and the Torah was always before their eyes. Therefore they did not refrain, because of their piety, from fighting on the Sabbath (1 Maccabees 44⚠5); and if it is found that they sometimes exceeded the middle measure of piety in religious matters, in this too they stood on the ground of the Torah, to fulfil the commandment of the Men of the Great Assembly, who said: "Make a fence for the Torah." It is not fitting to say of them that they were a separate sect. Rather, these pietists were the choice of the nation and the mighty on whom the existence of the religion of the Torah rested; and they were the ḥasidim and they were the Pharisees. Therefore the name "sect" fits neither the one nor the other; rather, first and foremost, "ḥasidim" or "perushim" was a designation for all the good and worthy men who were scrupulous in the commandments. And since most of the members of the nation followed them, this designation was given to all the Jews whose hand was with the Pharisees, and they were the majority of the nation. But the sect called Essenes was nothing but a corrupt offshoot of the Pharisees or the first pietists, and the Pharisees were not pleased with them and their deeds. True, all their characteristics are somewhat like the character of the pietists from whom they were hewn; but in each of the characteristics by which they were distinguished they overstepped law and bound, until they lost the essence of the characteristic and its moral nature as it was found among the pietists. Thus in keeping purity they overstepped the law so far that they spent much time in washings and immersions. Also, out of their fear of uncleanness they did not marry women. The mark of the ḥaver or the Pharisee was, as we saw above, not to raise small cattle; and they withdrew their hands from raising cattle altogether and engaged only in tilling the soil. Likewise the mark of the ḥaver was that he not be unrestrained in vows, because what the Torah forbade is enough for us; but among the Essenes this fence was widened until they refrained from swearing even in a time of need and necessity. And rightly such a perverse way did not please the sages. Their immersion in the morning too was an excess without a reasonable cause. True, the pietists, their predecessors, and the Pharisees also practiced morning immersion, but only on account of a seminal emission, and that was according to the law; and this continued among the Pharisees until the last of the Tannaim (Berakhot 22b), and still in the time of R. Ḥanina, one of the first Amoraim, dawn bathers are mentioned (see Yerushalmi Berakhot end of ch. 3). But the immersion of the Essenes was constant, without a special cause, and it is nothing but an excess of folly. Moreover, all the separation, the immersions, the mortifications, and the keeping away from what was permitted to them, to which they accustomed themselves, and especially their isolation from the society of people who were not members of their association, were the cause that their soul became ensnared in contemplation of the wondrous and in speculations of fancy, until they believed in their souls that by their way of life and their rules they would attain the spirit of prophecy, to know the future. They made use of the holy names, and perhaps also of the Ineffable Name. Among the secrets of their association was the knowledge of the names of the angels. They also had writings special to their association, and they also healed the sick by means of various roots and stones and according to old writings. This without doubt refers to the *Book of Remedies* spoken of in the Talmud and attributed to King Solomon (Pesaḥim 56a). And it is told in particular that they performed their works by the virtue of the roots and the stones combined with the virtue of the writings (Josephus; and see Graetz, vol. 3, p. 526). They strictly warned the members of their association to guard the writings of the association with great care; from this it appears that besides the *Book of Remedies* mentioned, they had still other writings in which they recorded their teachings and their expositions. Every day they rose early in the morning, and before sunrise the first work of their day was to pray in supplication to God to renew the light of the day. The text of the prayer they used was handed down from generation to generation, and without doubt this refers to the blessing before the reading of the Shema, whose text is: "Who gives light to the earth" etc. (R. Solomon Judah Rapoport, *Toledot Kalir*, note 20); and in this they held to the custom of the vatikin, who would finish the Shema with sunrise (Berakhot 9b); but vatikin is not a designation for the Essenes. In food and drink they spurned luxuries, and they did not eat meat or drink wine, and in particular they refused all human pleasures. In resting on the Sabbath they were scrupulous to a hair's breadth and overstepped the bound, so that they did not move any object, small or great, on the Sabbath. Likewise on the Sabbath they lived a life of privation and stinted themselves in taking food. These are the outlines of their ways and their deeds.

For our purpose this little suffices to recognize from it their relation to the Pharisaic sages, and to know what effect the doings of this sect had on the Pharisees, and how it was the cause of several enactments and statutes. There is no doubt that their practices, in that they lived a life of privation on the Sabbath and abstained from eating meat and drinking wine, were the reason for the sages to insist with extraordinary strictness on delighting the Sabbath with food and drink (Shabbat 118a), to institute the Grace after Meals over the cup (see Berakhot 51a), that the Sabbath be sanctified over wine (Pesaḥim 106a), and that people gladden their household on the festival with meat and wine (ibid. 109a). Likewise, the fact that the Essenes refrained from handling any vessel on the Sabbath also caused the sages to stand against them and make a new enactment. For the practice of the Essenes in this is without doubt one of the excesses by which they went beyond the measure of the early Ḥasidim. For already at first they used to say: three vessels may be handled on the Sabbath, a cutter for fig-cakes, a ladle for a pot, and a small knife on the table (Shabbat 123b); and the Essenes came and were strict in their manner and refrained from handling any vessel, and the matter was displeasing in the eyes of the sages, and they voted and decided to permit everything (ibid. 122b). And similar to this was the custom of the early Ḥasidim, who went to great lengths to make a fence for the separation of a man from his wife in the days of her menstruation, and said that it is good that a woman make herself unattractive to her husband in the days of her menstrual impurity, and not paint her eyes nor rouge herself; and we have seen how far the Essenes extended this separation, until they went to the furthest extreme and refrained from marrying women at all. And R. Akiva came and saw how many harms came about through the excess of the Ḥasidim, and said explicitly: if she does not paint her eyes and adorn herself, in the end she will become unattractive to her husband; and he permitted everything (Shabbat 64b; Sifra, end of Metzora). And let us pay attention to the mighty warnings with which the sages warned one who refrains from marrying a woman. They likened him to one who sheds blood and to one who diminishes the [divine] image, and said of him that he causes the Shekhinah to depart from Israel (Yevamot 63b–64a). Were it not that abstention from marrying a woman had taken root among many members of the nation for a particular reason, surely the sages would have had no reason to go so far in warnings without need; for in the ordinary, usual course they all married women, and against a few individuals who are exceptions to the rule the sages would not have been so inflamed as to go to endless lengths in warnings. It can only be that their warnings were directed against a particular sect that made it a rule for itself not to marry women, and we know of no other sect than the society of the Essenes; therefore without doubt all those warnings are directed at them. And perhaps also their saying, "One who whispers [an incantation] over a wound has no share in the World to Come" (Sanhedrin 10:1), is directed against the Essenes, who healed the sick by means of incantations (Graetz vol. 3, 526); and likewise what they said, "one who pronounces the Name by its letters has no share in the World to Come": perhaps this too is directed at the Essenes and their incantations. In any case we see from all this that the doings of this society were displeasing in the eyes of the Pharisaic sages. But it is true that they paid them no attention and had no dispute or dealings with them, for their tendency was not dangerous to religion: besides that its members were few in number, they were also weak in strength, dwelt alone and apart, and never quarreled with the Pharisees. Whether the Scroll of the Ḥasidim mentioned in the Talmud (end of Yerushalmi Berakhot), from which the masters of the Talmud copied a precious saying, "If you forsake her one day, she will forsake you two days," is among the remnants of the literature of the Essenes, there is no proof at all. It is true that we have many sayings (tractate Avot) from which the traits of the Essenes look out (Jellinek, *Orient* 1849, pp. 537, 553). But this is no proof that those who said them were definitely Essenes; rather, those sayings comprise the qualities of the true Ḥasidim, and we already know that the views of the Essenes flowed first and foremost from the views of the early Ḥasidim. The most compelling proof that the Pharisees did not approve of the way of the Essenes is that they did not leave their name and memory for good in any of the books of the Oral Torah. For all the conjectures that many students of antiquities have put forward, seeking to find a trace of their name in the Talmud, are all conjectures hanging by a hair, with nothing to rely on.{v1_07_09:1} Moreover, besides all this they had other customs that were not to the sages' liking, such as that they refrained from offering sacrifices in the Temple (Josephus and Philo). And without doubt the Pharisaic sages were displeased with their custom of living in solitude, having no dealings with anyone, and withdrawing from the wars, which were wars of the Lord. But we have already said that because they were men of peace the Pharisees paid them no attention, did not persecute them and did not quarrel with them. Not so the Sadducees: they were the adversaries of the Pharisees, and at various times they grew strong and prevailed over them; against them the Pharisees roused themselves with all their strength, and these quarrels became for the Pharisees the cause of new enactments, of various interpretations and halakhot, as we shall see in the following chapters.
