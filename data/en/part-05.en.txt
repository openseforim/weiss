Chapter Five

The period of the exile until their return from the exile.

Jerusalem still stood on its mound and the Temple of the Lord in its place; but the strength of the nation was broken, and the marks of its fall were already seen in the land. In the days when King Jehoiachin reigned (A.M. 3391) Jerusalem came under siege at the hand of Nebuchadnezzar king of Babylon, and was taken but not destroyed. He only exiled from Jerusalem all the princes and the nobles and the mighty men of valor, about ten thousand, and all the craftsmen and smiths (2 Kings 24:10–15). There were also some of the sages and of the prophets among the exiles (Ezekiel 1:1). Among the first exiles, in the days of King Jehoiakim, there were also some of the royal seed and of the nobles, youths of good understanding and skilled in all wisdom; they were taken into the king's palace and were taught the learning and the tongue of the Chaldeans. The choicest of them were four: Daniel, Hananiah, Mishael and Azariah (Daniel 1). After this first exile about twelve more years went by, and all those days were days of oppression, evil and horror. Yet with all that came upon them no one laid it to heart that their fall was near, and all the words of the prophet Jeremiah, who told them "the things that are to come hereafter" [Isaiah 41:23], bore no fruit above, among the great men and the princes, and struck no root below, among the mass of the people, until all the words of his prophecies were fulfilled and not one of them failed. Nebuchadnezzar made Zedekiah king, and he too did evil like the kings before him. In the ninth year of his reign he rebelled against the king of Babylon; then Nebuchadnezzar came up with all his army to Jerusalem and besieged it, and in the eleventh year the city was taken and became a burning of fire; the Temple of the Lord too was burned, and the rest of the people that had remained after the first exile was now exiled to Babylon. And all the prophecies were fulfilled that Jeremiah had prophesied concerning the land of Judah, concerning Zion and Jerusalem, concerning the kingdom and the people of the land, and that Ezekiel had seen in his visions in the exile. Now the house of Israel awoke from vain dreams and recognized that both prophet and priest, who had said to them all day long that God would give them "assured peace" [Jeremiah 14:13] and who "healed the breach of the people lightly" [Jeremiah 6:14], had all together dealt in "vain burdens and causes of banishment" [Lamentations 2:14]⚠1 and had prophesied falsehood to them; and on the contrary they recognized that the prophets who had testified against them that they would perish for their deeds if they did not return to the Lord had spoken the truth. And indeed, although this recognition could not undo and mend the evil that had already been done, it was of great benefit to their lot in the days that came toward them, and it worked to improve their religious condition. For when they saw how faithful were the words of the prophets who had prophesied their evil end, this was to them a sure sign that their words were faithful also in this, that they had given their forsaking of the Lord as the cause of their evil end, and that the terrible distress that had found them was caused by their evil deeds. These considerations, by which they examined their bitter lot, brought them to turn from their way and to confess their iniquity and the iniquity of their fathers; and when they saw that the words of the Lord and His statutes,{v1_03_19:1} which He commanded His servants the prophets, had overtaken them, then they returned and said: "Like as the Lord purposed to do unto us, according to our ways, and according to our doings, so hath He dealt with us" (Zechariah 1). And the most precious benefit they had from this is: that when the recognition of the truth of the prophets' words came into their hearts, as to their evil end, as to the causes that brought it about, and as to the deeds, beliefs and opinions that are fit to remove the evil that had struck them, then they also set their hearts on the good and comforting things that the prophets had announced to them, and good hopes for the future took root in them. And now all these recognitions did much to strengthen in them from then on the belief in one God and to bring them back to the religion of Moses in its purity, clean of dross and of any admixture of idolatry. Therefore we can justly say that their religious condition was now improved to the utmost, compared with the condition of religion before the exile. The change in their moral condition also worked much for good on their intellectual condition. They did very great wonders in the field of wisdom and of song. And the spirit of love of the good that was now awakened in them prevailed over all the distresses that found them in the land of their enemies. A broad literature in wisdom, in poetry and in prophecy is the offspring of that time. Love for their nation bred in them the desire to preserve for the last generation the events of its history and the remnant of the wisdom of its sages that was left to them out of the overthrow. Many songs and praises that were taken up into the book of Psalms that we have were written then by poets whose names are indeed hidden from us.{v1_03_20:1} Many prophecies that we have in writing among the Holy Scriptures are children of the days of the exile.{v1_03_20:2} And several narratives of history were composed together at that time on the basis of old sources.{v1_03_20:3} Now, in the measure that the spirit of the Torah of the Lord increased through the work of the sages and was set down in the writings, and still more in the writings of the prophets, so the traces of the Torah and of the statutes in those writings now increase for us, and signs of the interpretation of the Torah come to light. But a great difference is already seen between now and the ancient time; for whereas all the early prophets did not turn their gaze so much on the practical statutes and spoke of them only very little, we see that in the exile people began to be exact in the practical statutes, and the sages of the generation too made them the aim of their inquiry. And this lies in the nature of the thing. Every people that has been thrust out of its political condition, whose freedom is lost,⚠2 and that lives a life of distress on foreign soil, finds comfort and hope in its customs and its statutes that are holy to it; these it cherishes and in these it delights, until it is exact in them to a hair's breadth and sets "precept upon precept, line upon line" [Isaiah 28:10] in order to keep its charge. And this phenomenon has shown itself especially in the people of Israel at all times; it is therefore no wonder when we see this phenomenon before our eyes also among the Jews in the exile. Zeal and exactness in keeping the practical statutes began little by little to take root in the people; for without doubt Daniel and Hananiah, Mishael and Azariah were not alone in refraining from defiling themselves with foods forbidden by religion and from drinking the wine of gentiles (Daniel 1). And this is truly a great exactness, going beyond the measure of the statute in the Torah. That they guarded themselves from drinking the wine of gentiles was without doubt a custom established and standing among the good in the people; and it cannot be said that they invented this prohibition for themselves and decreed on themselves to keep it, since they were only boys, young in years; surely they forbade themselves only a thing that people had long been accustomed to treat as forbidden (see Avodah Zarah 36a). Also when Daniel and his companions, who walked in the ways of the religious statutes, grew up and succeeded in being honored in the eyes of the king and his princes, "their taste remained in them" [Jeremiah 48:11] and they did not exchange their fear of God. And Daniel in particular was a model among his people for his righteousness and his keeping of the religion and the statutes (Ezekiel 14:14). And now, just as the conduct of the great in the nation has at all times acted on the mass of the people, so that if the great corrupted their way they too corrupted theirs, so it was now the other way round: when they saw Daniel and his companions keeping the religious statutes, the multitude of the people did so as well.{v1_03_21:1} And for this reason the interpretation of the statutes necessarily increased and began to come to a new stage, whose traces are already seen in the words of Ezekiel.

Ezekiel son of Buzi the priest prophesied "in an unclean land" [Amos 7:17], by the river Chebar. His book of prophecies differs from the books of the other prophets in that several matters of practical statutes appear in it. Those statutes, besides bearing witness to the existence of old traditions in customs and statutes that lived in the people at that time and were not written in the Torah, also show that, although he saw that keeping the practical statutes works on the people to improve their moral way, and he was very particular about their keeping, nevertheless we do not find that he was particular with great exactness about the outward form, whether it be thus or thus. In the eyes of the sages of the later generations this difference was like a contradiction of the Torah. And already in ancient days this was a matter that occupied the sages, and they almost lifted their hand against the book of Ezekiel to store it away and remove it from the world (Shabbat 13b; Ḥagigah 13b);⚠3 but they did not do so. They upheld it and set their minds and spirits at rest by saying that some of its sections Elijah⚠4 will expound in the future (Menaḥot 45b).⚠5 But this is a feeble setting at rest of the mind, for by it the contradictions were not reconciled. The truth, however, is that the prophet did not mean to contradict the words of the Torah deliberately and willingly; rather it is as we said, that at times the outward form of a statute was to him only a secondary matter. And yet at times this or that of his words will also seem to us a contradiction of the Torah, because, according to the way we are accustomed to understand some statute in the Torah, it is indeed an explicit contradiction; but we have not considered that perhaps the prophet understood this statute in another way, and therefore wrote what he wrote. Some places in his book will justify this judgment of ours. Behold, one of the great principles that had taken root in the belief of the people and is written in the Torah is that the Lord visits the iniquity of the fathers upon the children (Exodus 20:5). This principle was almost a pillar on which the whole building of religion leaned, and it made its way through all its history. Were not all the promises that had been promised from the beginning given not for their own righteousness but for the righteousness of their fathers? And so also when the wheel turned against them for evil, it was likewise said to them that the iniquity of their fathers too was visited upon them. But this thing greatly distressed the men of Ezekiel's generation. For if the children perish for the iniquities of their fathers for several generations, then their hope of the good that is to come upon them is lost; and this hope was their comfort in their grief. And indeed Ezekiel spoke against this principle, to strengthen the hope of his people and to rouse their hearts⚠6 too to return to the good, and to assure them that they would not pine away in the iniquity of their fathers if they mended their ways; and he said: "The soul that sinneth, it shall die" [Ezekiel 18:20] (Herzfeld, vol. 1). And as if the prophet himself felt that he was speaking against the opinion that ruled in the people and was founded on the Torah, he seeks to reconcile the contradiction by explaining his words with understanding and knowledge, and he said: that if the wicked father begets a son, who sees all his father's sins which he has done, and does not do likewise, he shall not die for the iniquity of the father, he shall surely live (ibid. 18:14–17). And now, besides teaching them thus to justify the justice of God and to revive the heart of the crushed so that they should not despair of their hope, he further teaches them how the words of the Torah are to be understood when it says that God visits the iniquity of the fathers upon the children, and how they are to be reconciled with the justice of God (see Sanhedrin 27b). One of the contradictions between the words of the prophet and the words of the Torah to which the sages alluded is this: the prophet says, "The priests shall not eat of anything that dieth of itself, or is torn" (ibid. 44:31), whereas in the Torah this warning was said to all Israel. For of the torn animal it is said explicitly: "Ye shall not eat any flesh that is torn of beasts in the field" (Exodus 22:30); and of the carcass too they were warned: "Ye shall not eat of anything that dieth of itself; thou mayest give it unto the stranger that is within thy gates, that he may eat it; or thou mayest sell it unto a foreigner" (Deuteronomy 15).⚠7 How then will the prophet justify his words over against these statutes of the Torah? But we have already seen that the prophet was not particular about every single letter, to be exact to a hair's breadth. And it is very possible that the prophet thought that many things of which it was said not to do them are to be understood not as a warning but as a request and a wish. And indeed there are many statutes that make known only the wish and will of the divine Lawgiver, and they are like good counsel to the members of the religion; and of this kind are most of the statutes concerning the sacrifices. And there is no doubt that all the prophets understood these statutes so, and only for this reason Jeremiah said: "For I spoke not unto their fathers, nor commanded them ... concerning burnt-offerings or sacrifices" [Jeremiah 7:22] (Radak). And many times the Torah said, do not do thus and thus, and did not mean to warn against doing it, but meant that there is no need to do it. And in many statutes the intention of the Torah is plain, that they are said as a commandment and as a statute "which shall not pass" [Psalm 148:6]; and among these there are many degrees. There are some against which the warning is more emphatic than against others, and the warning is made stronger by punishments; and at times it gives it force by adding emphatic words such as "beware," "take heed to thyself," and the like, or by saying explicitly that whoever does thus and thus shall bear his iniquity. And mostly one recognizes from the context of the warning what is by way of command and what by way of wish and will; as in all the duties between a man and his fellow it is understood of itself that they are by way of command. Now if we consider the statutes that speak of eating the carcass, we do not find, except in the two places we have mentioned, an absolute warning. Rather it is said: "And he that eateth of the carcass of it shall be unclean until the even" (Leviticus 11:40); and likewise: "And every soul that eateth that which dieth of itself, or that which is torn of beasts ... shall wash his clothes, and bathe himself in water, and be unclean until the even" (ibid. 17:15); and when it says: "And the fat of that which dieth of itself, and the fat of that which is torn of beasts ... ye shall in no wise eat of it" (ibid. 7:24), there is in all of these no absolute warning not to eat them. It is almost the opposite: the Torah left open the possibility that they would eat them, and said that if they eat carcass and torn meat it will cause them uncleanness. Therefore, seeing this, we can easily judge that when it says, "Ye shall not eat any flesh that is torn of beasts in the field," and likewise what is said in Deuteronomy, "Ye shall not eat of anything that dieth of itself," it said so only by way of will and wish: that it is fitting for every man of Israel to sanctify himself and to keep himself far from eating the carcass, out of the quality of holiness; for since they are "a kingdom of priests and a holy nation" [Exodus 19:6], it is fitting that they conduct themselves after the custom of the priests; and the priests were warned absolutely, and with an emphatic warning, against eating the carcass, as it is said: "Whatsoever man of the seed of Aaron ... that which dieth of itself, or is torn of beasts, he shall not eat to defile himself therewith" [Leviticus 22:4, 8]. And indeed, to the two statutes of carcass and torn meat words were added that can easily bring us to this interpretation and sense. For before the warning "Ye shall not eat any flesh that is torn of beasts in the field" it puts the words "And ye shall be holy men unto Me" [Exodus 22:30]; and the warning "Ye shall not eat of anything that dieth of itself" is sealed with the saying "for thou art a holy people unto the Lord thy God" [Deuteronomy 14:21]. We see that here it explains with a special intention the will and the wish that they conduct themselves in this matter in holiness and after the custom of the priests. Therefore it is possible that the prophet Ezekiel understood the statute in this way and thought that the absolute warning applies only to the priests, and therefore he said: "The priests shall not eat of anything that dieth of itself, or is torn." But he did not mean by this that the Israelite is permitted carcass and torn meat. The law of the priests and their holiness with regard to marriage, too, the prophet describes with a great divergence from what is said in the Torah. According to the words of Ezekiel, the priests who minister at the altar shall not take a widow or a divorced woman for their wives, but virgins of the seed of the house of Israel, and a widow that is the widow of a priest they may take [Ezekiel 44:22]. And this is not in agreement with the statute in the Torah. For the Torah distinguished between the high priest and the ordinary priest, and forbade the widow only to the high priest. Yet in some way we can find a reconciliation, namely: if we consider the statute of the Torah, we find the marriage law for the ordinary priest only in a negative form; that is, it is not said of him whom he shall take as a wife and from which family or tribe, but it is said whom he shall not take. And it is not said absolutely that he is permitted to take the widow; we only understand so from the fact that it is not mentioned of him that he shall not take a widow, as it is mentioned of the high priest; from this we judge that an ordinary priest is permitted to take a widow. And since the Torah did not permit the widow to him explicitly, but only kept silent about it, this was not in his eyes an uprooting of a statute of the Torah. And if we ask why the Torah forbade the widow to the high priest explicitly, to this we answer: that for the high priest the widow is forbidden in every case, for it is said of him: "but a virgin of his own people shall he take to wife" [Leviticus 21:14], and this teaches that he shall not take a widow even if she is of his own people (that is, of his family, the widow of a priest), and he shall not take a virgin who is not of his own people. And now we can judge from what the Torah said, "And he (that is, the high priest) shall take a wife in her virginity" [Leviticus 21:13], that this wife shall be only a virgin of his own people, and not a virgin who is not of his own people, and not a widow of his own people; these the high priest shall not marry. And it is proved that it is not so with the ordinary priest, but he may take a virgin even if she is not of his own people, or a widow if she is of his own people. Therefore it is no wonder that the Torah kept silent about the widow in the case of the ordinary priest; for with the ordinary priest the widow is not forbidden in every case, since there is a widow whom he is permitted to take as a wife. And now, are not the words of Ezekiel explained with complete exactness in this way? He rightly said, "neither shall they take for their wives a widow, nor her that is put away," but not in every way as with the high priest; rather he may take a virgin even if she is not of his own people, and this is "virgins of the seed of the house of Israel," and he may take a widow who is of his own people, and this is "a widow that is the widow of a priest." So in this way we can find a reconciliation between the words of Ezekiel and the words of the Torah. Yet even with this reconciliation it cannot be denied that in all the statutes concerning the priesthood recorded in the book of Ezekiel we see a great divergence from the statute of the Torah; and in general, wherever this book makes mention of religious statutes, we find this divergence in them.

Of the office of the high priesthood the prophet said nothing; and it appears from his words that in the ministry of the altar he wants all the priests who keep the services of the altar to be equal, but the supreme authority in the sanctuary is to be in the hand of the prince (44:3; 46:10). But he laid it down as a statute that not all the seed of Aaron shall be set apart to keep the charge of the altar, but only part of it, namely the priests the Levites, the sons of Zadok (44:15); and he removed the priests who are not of the family of Zadok, so that they shall be only ministers of the house. And from within the words of this statute a special character looks out that he had in the interpretation of the statutes: namely, that he went down to the end of the intention of the Torah and sought out the reason of its statutes. For if he removed some of the seed of Aaron, this is only because he judged in his mind that the priesthood is not a personal and bodily right, but only together with the moral excellence that the members of Aaron's family have; and if this excellence is lacking in them, then their right is lost. And since the sons of Zadok were after the Lord at all times, the right of the priesthood remained to them also for the generations to come; and since the rest of the priests, who are not of the sons of Zadok, went far from the Lord when Israel went astray after other gods, they shall not come near to the Lord to minister to Him as priests, but shall only be keepers of the house for all its service. And we see that at first, when he mentions the matter of the sanctuary, he gives to these as to those the name "priests," only he distinguished between them by the difference of their office: those who went astray when Israel went astray he calls "priests, the keepers of the charge of the house," and the sons of Zadok he calls "the keepers of the charge of the altar" (ibid. 40:45, 46). And at the last he calls the priests who went astray and were removed (according to the prophet's will) from the charge of the altar only by the name of Levites (44:10; 48:11–13). We see that it was the prophet's will to remove part of the seed of Aaron from the holiness of the priesthood altogether. A great divergence from the statutes of the Torah we find in him when he describes the commandment of the consecration offerings (ibid. 45:18, and Menaḥot 45a), and likewise with regard to the sacrifices on the festivals and the feasts of the year, where he sometimes added and sometimes took away (ibid. 45:21–25). And some of the festivals he did not mention at all, such as the Feast of Weeks, the Day of Remembrance and the Day of Atonement. On the other hand we find new things in him, such as the signs of mourning that a man performs for his near kin; and this is nothing but an ancient custom, for the prophet speaks of them as of a thing long customary (24:17). And we learn from his words{v1_03_24:1} that the signs of mourning were four: not to bind on the headtire, not to put on sandals, to cover the upper lip, and to eat the food of others (Mo'ed Katan 15a). And whoever considers his words will find other matters that are new and cannot be learned from the book of the Torah of Moses (Yoma 71b and in many places). And now this is to us a sure sign that they had in their hands various traditions in religious matters that are not written in the Torah; and further we have seen in what way the statutes were understood by the prophet, and that for the most part they were not understood by him in the way they were understood by the sages of the later generations,{v1_03_24:2} and that he did not mind departing at times from the statutes of the Torah as to their form. We cannot know today the causes that moved the prophet to depart in this respect from the Torah, since a span of time of several thousand years lies between us and him; but the fact in itself cannot be denied, and without doubt he was compelled to it by the need of his time and hour. At times the words of Ezekiel decide the sense of some obscure statute. Behold, when he reproves the people and mentions the iniquity that they did, he speaks to them, saying: "In thee have they uncovered their fathers' nakedness; in thee have they humbled her that was unclean in her impurity. And one hath committed abomination with his neighbour's wife; and another hath lewdly defiled his daughter-in-law; and another in thee hath humbled his sister, his father's daughter" (Ezekiel 22); and in all these the prophet means to reprove them for the corruption of their way within the family. But the nakedness of the mother he did not mention; and this is a proof that he understood the statute of the Torah that says, "The nakedness of thy father, and the nakedness of thy mother, shalt thou not uncover" (Leviticus 18:7), only of the nakedness of the mother, which is the nakedness of the father; and it did not occur to the ancients to interpret "the nakedness of thy father" as the nakedness of the father himself, as some of the sages of the Talmud thought (Sanhedrin 54a). And likewise, when the prophet speaks of "other garments" that the priests shall put on when they come near to the people (Ezekiel 42:14; 44:19), we deduce from his words that by the expression "other garments" we are to understand ordinary garments and not other holy garments, as the sages of the Talmud interpreted (Yoma 23a; Sifra, Tsav, ch. 2).

Also in the other prophetic books from the time of the exile precious traces are found that point to various traditions which lived among the people from ancient times, and through which the statute of the Torah is broadened (Isaiah 58:13, and see above, ch. 5). For our purpose there is no need to bring all the details; but it is fitting to remark on one more matter, for two reasons. The first: because it interprets a statute in the Torah, and that interpretation was upheld and accepted by all the prophets down to the end of the days of the exile. The second reason is that it is one of the interpretations that in the course of time was abolished entirely. As regards the standing of the day of the new moon, or Rosh Ḥodesh, nothing clear is found in the Torah, and we do not know whether it has holiness like the other festivals of the year or not. It is only said that on the new moons special sacrifices are offered, as on the Sabbath and on the festivals (Numbers 28:11), and likewise that the trumpets are blown over the sacrifices as on a festival (ibid. 10:10). But from the words of the prophets we learn that they used to celebrate the day of the new moon. Already in the days of the prophet Elisha the good among the people used to go to the prophet to inquire of the Lord on the day of the new moon as on the Sabbath day (2 Kings 5:23)⚠8, and this is a sign that it was a day of rest. Likewise Isaiah calls it a holy convocation, like the Sabbath day (Isaiah 1:13), and Ezekiel too makes it a holy convocation on which all the people of the land shall come to bow down before the Lord (Ezekiel 46:1). And not only was the day of the new moon a festival with a special divine service on it, but they also refrained from work on it. For they called the day before the new moon "the working day" in contrast to the day of the new moon, which is the day of rest (1 Samuel 20:19, and see Targum Jonathan and the Septuagint, and the Babylonian Talmud Megillah 22b with Rashi's commentary), and on it they refrained from buying and selling (Amos 8:5). From all this it is clearly seen that they rested on Rosh Ḥodesh as on a festival. Without doubt they interpreted the statute in the Torah in this way: since the day is distinguished by special sacrifices, it therefore has the holiness of a festival. But this interpretation did not endure in the course of time; perhaps already after they returned from the exile they no longer kept it, but made the day of the new moon like the intermediate days of a festival, and refrained from doing work on it only when the work was not urgent (see Megillah, ibid.); and in the course of time the strap was loosened altogether, and the custom remained only among the women (Ḥagigah 18a and the Tosafot there, and Yerushalmi Ta'anit ch. 1). Now if we cast our eye over all the traces of the Torah that we have mentioned so far, we find that it is no empty thing that our ancestors handed down to us: that besides the statutes and ordinances written in the book of the Torah there were still among the people ancient traditions, statutes and customs that were not written in the book. On such traditions several of the written statutes were founded. It is likewise true and established that the teachings and statutes that have reached us in writing were interpreted in all ages, from the days of old, wherever they needed interpretation. We have also seen that several interpretations changed in the course of the times and the generations. It is likewise clear that many times there is a contradiction between the sense of the statutes in an early time and their sense among the later generations; but sometimes agreement between them is also found. On this basis we can determine with clarity that many of the ancient interpretations endured and have reached us, and that they agree with several interpretations that we have today. And indeed it has also become known to us that the study of the Torah went step by step. Among the prophets of the first generations, until the end of the days of the Temple, it had a different character from what it had from then on. In the days of the exile they began to be more exact in the practice of the statutes and in their examination, and this was the time of transition. From the beginning of the time that came after it, the study of the Torah reached a new stage: exactness and examination grew from day to day and in each and every generation. And this new stage began from the time of their return from the exile to the rest and to the inheritance [Deuteronomy 12:9].
