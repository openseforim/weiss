Chapter Eight

The sayings of the Scribes and their ordinances.

From the day when religion renewed its youth like the eagle [Psalms 103:5] through the Scribes, with Ezra at their head, they began to build a fence and enacted ordinances for the benefit of the nation and the religion. But in the course of time the ordinances and decrees were fruitful and multiplied in every generation without end. And since they did not become the work of [one] man until most of the sages of the generation agreed upon them, who were counted and concluded to establish and found them, no particular founder's name was called upon them, for the founders were many; rather they were always called by the name divrei soferim [words of the Scribes]. But we must be very, very cautious in understanding this expression, for sometimes the sages of the Talmud used it loosely and said divrei soferim without meaning the early Scribes.{v1_04_16:1} But there are various things which were said in the name of the Scribes and are clearly from them. Of sayings stated specifically in the name of the sages of this generation, only one has reached us. It is the line and measure which the Men of the Great Assembly gave for how the Scribes should conduct themselves in their office, saying: "Be deliberate in judgment, raise up many disciples, and make a fence for the Torah" (Avot 1:2⚠1). The sages of the Mishnah and the Talmud attributed many ordinances to them. The order of the Eighteen prayers, which are called by the general name Tefillah (Megillah 17b), is in their opinion an ordinance of the Men of the Great Assembly. And this tradition is true in some respect. For how can we imagine to ourselves the founding of the worship of God in the Temple unless we say that they made ordinances concerning prayer? But we cannot say with certainty that the very wording of the Eighteen-benediction prayer is the very one that was handed down to us from the Men of the Great Assembly as we have it today.{v1_04_16:2} But the root of the Talmud's tradition, that the Men of the Great Assembly enacted prayers, is true. The one part of the prayers and thanksgivings consisted of various psalms which they selected from the psalms in the book of Psalms. Already in very early times it was the custom to sing songs of praise and thanksgiving to God, but without doubt they selected for themselves psalms suited to that time, and made corrections in them, additions or omissions, as they needed (above, chs. 3 and 6). They also composed new praises and songs, to sing them in the choirs in the Temple. The Hallel which it is our custom to recite on the festivals is one of the children of that time. Yet not all of it; rather it is composed of two parts. The one part begins with Halleluyah and ends with Halleluyah, and the second part begins with Hodu and ends with Hodu. The first part was not composed before the exile, and perhaps after it. So it appears from the images in which it depicts the idols (Psalms 115:4–7), which are close to the images of the prophet in the appendices to Isaiah; and from the rebukes against the idolatrous nations, since this practice too is not found in any of the prophets who preceded that prophet (De Wette in his commentary on Psalms); and it speaks of the troubles that befell them in the past and of the deliverance in the present (115). And this Hallel the Men of the Great Assembly ordained to be recited on the festivals (*Moreh Nevukhei ha-Zeman*, gate 11). The second part was added later, in the days of the Maccabees, as appears from the content of the whole psalm (118) from beginning to end; and there is not in all of it even one saying that does not suit the time of the Maccabees. Therefore we have seen, at any rate, that the first part was composed in the time we are dealing with, or a little before it, and its recitation was ordained by the Men of the Great Assembly or the Scribes.{v1_04_17:1} The second part of the prayers, of which we can say with certainty that it is the work of the Scribes, is: the first three and the last three benedictions of the Eighteen. The custom, still practiced today, of saying the first and last benedictions on Sabbaths and festivals as well is also a sign that they are very ancient, and the ancient custom has remained in our hands (Zunz, p. 36). So too it appears from the prayer Havinenu: although the sages permitted this short prayer to be said at times when the hour requires it, nevertheless they gave no permission to shorten the first and last benedictions, and permitted doing so only with the middle ones (Berakhot 28b⚠2 and Yerushalmi Ta'anit 2:2). This is because from of old these benedictions were more precious in value than the middle ones.{v1_04_17:2} Besides these prayers, further prayers and blessings, and kiddushot and havdalot, were attributed to the Men of the Great Assembly, in order (Berakhot 33). They also attributed to them the reading of the Megillah and its conditions (Megillah 2a), and the writing of the books of Ezekiel, the Twelve, Daniel and the Scroll of Esther (Bava Batra 4a⚠3). To Ezra in particular they attributed ten ordinances (Bava Kamma 82a), and so too the ordinance that the curses in Leviticus be read before Shavuot and the curses in Deuteronomy before Rosh Hashanah (Megillah 31a⚠4). But who knows whether the sages of the Talmud who attributed all these to them relied on a clear tradition? And who can deny that they sometimes attributed things and ordinances to them, or to other ancients, not because the matter was clear to them, but because the beginning of their origin was already hidden from them, and so they hung them on ancient enactors? If we consider the ten ordinances which they attributed to Ezra, we find that among them there are ordinances whose foundation lies in a late time.{v1_04_18:1} And the limit for attributing things to the ancients was not the time of Ezra; that is to say, the later sages attributed new things, lately come, not only to Ezra and the sages of his time, but went many more steps back along the path of time. How many ordinances and customs they attributed to the prophets! They said that the water libation and the encircling of the altar with the willow on the festival of Sukkot is a foundation of the prophets or a custom of the prophets (Yerushalmi Sukkah 4:1, and the Bavli there, 44a). It is very far-fetched to conclude that those who said so relied on a tradition handed down from generation to generation without anyone disputing it, for we find a difference in their opinions: these said so, and another says the water libation is a word of the Torah and derived it from a slight hint in the verse, and there is one who says that they had it by tradition back to Moses at Sinai. Of necessity all these sages spoke only by conjecture and not by relying on a clear tradition; and the truth is that the source of the custom was already hidden from them, and each one pointed to its source according to his opinion.{v1_04_18:2} So too they attributed to the prophets the two festival days of Rosh Hashanah (Eruvin 3:9 in the Yerushalmi). This too appears not to have been said by the sages from tradition, and in the Babylonian Talmud there is no mention of it; surely this is nothing but the conjecture of individual sages, who thought so because, since from of old they sanctified the month by sighting, it is self-evident that they were mostly compelled to make Rosh Hashanah two days, and from the time when the sanctification of the month by sighting began they made Rosh Hashanah two days. But since they did not know to what time this correction should be ascribed, they hung it on the early prophets.{v1_04_18:3} And without doubt in this conjecture they relied on their midrash which they expounded on the words of Ezekiel, who says of the prophets who see vanity that "they shall not be in the council of My people" (Ezekiel 13), where in their view this council [sod] is the secret [sod] of intercalation, and the prophets who see vanity shall not be in the secret of intercalation (Ketubbot 112b; Yerushalmi Sanhedrin 1:2). From this we see that they understood Ezekiel's words as ascribing the secret of intercalation to the faithful prophets, and this was the root of their conjecture when they attributed the ordinance of the two days of Rosh Hashanah to the prophets as well. To Haggai the prophet too they attributed three testimonies. From their content it appears that those who said so had no clarified tradition about it, but relied on an aggadah which the Talmud cast doubt upon, not believing that it ever came out of the mouth of Haggai the prophet (Yevamot 16a, and Tosafot s.v. Rabbi; and see the Yerushalmi there). Likewise they attributed the part of the Grace after Meals that speaks of the remembrance of Jerusalem to King David, and attributed to his son Solomon the ordinance that one say in the Grace after Meals "for the great and holy House" (Berakhot 48b); and certainly they said this only by way of conjecture. This is because David truly established that Jerusalem should be the royal city and supreme over all the cities, and because Solomon built the Temple; therefore they moved the time of the ordinance back to the earliest time to which it was possible to advance it, and they did not place it later, because they did not know on whom else to hang it. But the truth is that all the benedictions of the Grace after Meals as we have them originate from a very late time, and there is not even one saying in them in which the signs of youth are not recognizable. (And see Naḥmanides in his glosses on the *Sefer ha-Mitzvot* and the novellae of R. Solomon ibn Adret.) And everything besides these that they attributed to the earliest of the early generations and to their heads, its character testifies of it that it is the product of a late time. But they searched and sought hints in the words of those ancients only so as to have some small support to lean on in attributing to them the ordinance which, for some reason (sometimes known and sometimes not known to us), they wished to give a place in ancient years. So it appears clearly in all that they attributed to David and Solomon (Eruvin 21b; Yevamot 21a) and in what they attributed to Jehoshaphat king of Judah (Pesaḥim 92a): it all rests on slight hints according to the ways of aggadic midrash. And so too it is with many ordinances that were attributed to Ezra or to other sages in these early generations: they did not attribute them to them because they had clear knowledge that it was so, but since the time of their birth was hidden from them, they searched and sought to find some hint in the words of the heads of the ancient generations so that they might hang on them the founding of some ordinance; and even if they had no hint they would hang it on them, because the generation of Ezra was the first in which they began to enact ordinances and build fences.{v1_04_19:1}

Not a small part of the explanations of the commandments and statutes that have become known and established among us date from the time of the Scribes. We have indeed seen that from the days of Ezra began the effort of the leaders of the generations to teach the people statutes and laws, so that they would keep and perform the commandments that had lapsed since ancient years. Now how many are the commandments in the Torah whose manner of performance is obscure and not explained in the Torah, and cannot be understood without the explanation that shows in what manner a given commandment is to be done! Therefore, if we say that in the days of the Scribes they practiced them, we must necessarily also say that the Scribes made known to them in what manner they were to be done, since we cannot imagine the performance of the act without the explanation. Now it cannot be denied that some of the explanations that teach us the manner of performing the obscure commandments were received traditions among them; this holds for the commandments that had not ceased since they were given: since they performed the act of the commandment, they necessarily also knew the necessary explanation. But for the commandments whose performance was not kept up among the people and was entirely forgotten, we must conclude that their explanation, which shows the manner of performance, was also forgotten, and they were compelled to explain them according to their own understanding in order to find the intent of the Torah. It is not for us to decide what was forgotten and what was kept, nor is it in our power to decide, because all the historical narratives whose source lies in these ancient days are silent and tell us nothing about the performance of the commandments. But in any case one thing is clear: the explanations of the kind we have mentioned are an inheritance to us from the Scribes, and it is all one to us whether they weighed and searched them out or whether they were received traditions in their hands. Explanations of this kind are: “And you shall take for yourselves on the first day” [Leviticus 23:40] — that the taking be by hand. “And the whole assembly of the congregation of Israel shall slaughter it at dusk” [Exodus 12:6] — that one slaughters for many. “Seven days you shall eat matzot” [Exodus 12:15] — that matzot are only from the kinds of grain. “The fruit of a goodly tree” [Leviticus 23:40] — this is the etrog. All these appear to be among the most ancient explanations. Close to this kind, among the remnants left to us from the generations of the Scribes, there are found in the books of the Oral Torah sayings whose character testifies that they are the work of the earliest sages, that is, the Scribes. The content of these sayings comprises necessary specifications for ordinances and laws that we have recognized as products of the Scribes; and since the specifications were necessary to them, it is self-evident that when the ordinances and laws were enacted, their specifications were made as well. The antiquity of these sayings is further recognizable by their brevity and simplicity. We have already said that the commandment of the recitation of the Shema is an ordinance of the Scribes; likewise the blessings before and after it are also very ancient in their root, and if we separate from them the many additions that were added to them, we recognize in them the work of the ancients, and they have rightly been ascribed to the generations of the Scribes (Zunz, p. 366). It is therefore self-evident that the Scribes already laid down the very rule that one must bless before and after it, and perhaps the very saying in which this rule is described in the Mishnah (Berakhot 1:4) is also theirs. So too is the matter with what is said concerning pe'ah, the first fruits and the appearance-offering [re'ayon] — that they have no fixed measure (Pe'ah 1:1); for this rule was already necessary to them from the time they took upon themselves to give the gifts of the priesthood and the Levites as written in the Torah. The sign of this saying's antiquity is its use of the word re'ayon, which we do not find again in the Mishnah; at a later time they used the word re'iyah in its place. Likewise the order of bringing the first fruits as described in the Mishnah (Bikkurim 3:2) contains very old sayings, without doubt from the time of the Scribes; and there is found the word paḥot [governors], which clearly points to the time when Israel was still under the rule of the Persians. Also the sayings of the Mishnah: “There are four principal categories of damages”; “Whatever I am obliged to guard, I have made ready its damage”; “property that belongs to members of the covenant” (Bava Kamma ch. 1) — from their language and their brevity it appears that they are very ancient and from the time of the generations of the Scribes (*Moreh Nevukhei ha-Zeman*, gate 13). It seems that the term bnei berit [members of the covenant] in this place does not refer to the members of the Israelite nation in that they bear the holy covenant in their flesh, but refers to them in that they entered together into the covenant in the days of Ezra and made a firm agreement to keep the Torah. There are further sayings in the Mishnah which we may suppose are from the time of the Scribes because their form resembles an explanation of Scripture: they include the words of the verse with the explanation attached to them, and this was precisely the way of the Scribes (Nega'im 12:5), as when it describes how the priest is to proceed in inspecting the afflicted house in order to declare it unclean. For all the sayings there, if we strip from them the additions (which were added at a later time, have a style like the style of the teaching⚠5 of the early Scribes (*Darkhei ha-Mishnah*, p. 5). Whoever investigates the matter will find further sayings of these kinds in the Mishnah and baraita that are to be ascribed to the Scribes. But we must be very cautious in our judgments, for it is not the brevity of a saying, nor even the style of its language, that alone testifies to its antiquity and to its coming from the time of the Scribes, unless we look at its content and subject. We must know that the most ancient explanations are also the simplest. For in the early time they did not yet go beyond the bounds of the plain sense of Scripture, they did not engage in the fine points of letters, and they did not yet know remote and strange midrashim. Also the fences and ordinances of the early time did not exceed measure as in a later time. Therefore the difference in character between the ancients and the later ones, in the midrash of the Torah, in fences and in ordinances, will be for us the line and plummet of judgment [Isaiah 28:17] when we judge the sayings set before us in the books of the Oral Torah, to know which of them is ancient and may be ascribed to the Scribes and which is a child of the later generations. For like a spring-fed stream that at its source is only like the palm of a man's hand [1 Kings 18:44], and little by little grows and swells until it becomes a rushing torrent, so it was with the exposition of the Torah. Its beginning was small [Job 8:7]. The plain sense of Scripture was the character of the midrash of the Torah. But in the course of time the studies were fruitful and multiplied, the explanations grew and the branches spread, because they derived one thing from another and the derivatives [toladot] multiplied.{v1_04_21:1} These derivatives had already ceased to have the character of explanations, for they did not in essence come out of the words of Scripture but came by way of logical reasoning, by likening one thing to another; and from their careful consideration of the laws of the Torah they arrived at specifications in the commandments and statutes. On the other hand, they had traditions in laws, statutes and judgments; in addition to this they weighed and searched out and enacted decrees, fences, ordinances and good customs. Now all these derivatives, specifications, traditions, fences and ordinances were a business for students and teachers to be occupied with [Ecclesiastes 1:13], and this was a great cause of change and difference in the way of study and in its spirit. For at first the men of understanding taught the people and the students the text of Scripture and explained to them its matter and intent; and to help the memory of the explanations, so that they would not be forgotten by the hearers — the people and the students — they needed no aid from outside, for whatever came out of Scripture directly, the text itself acted to remind the students of the explanation given to it. It was not so with derivatives and specifications that they derived by logical inference, or with matters founded on traditions, and all the more so with fences and ordinances, for none of which was there any hint in the Torah; therefore for all these they were compelled to clothe them in the garment of exact and short sayings, and to fix them as set rules, so as to make memory easier. A small remnant of such sayings from the Scribes is in our hands, and among them the testimony of Yose ben Yo'ezer (Eduyot 8:4), which comprises three sayings, and also other sayings mentioned above and many like them. The form of all of them and their brevity were fit to ease memory. Some of them are marked by numbers, for number acts strongly to fix the sayings recorded by it in the memory of the students. The sages of the Talmud spoke well on this (Yerushalmi Shekalim 5:1): that the Scribes made the Torah into counted items, for example: “Five may not give terumah.” Already Simeon the Just used this method in his saying, giving it a number when he said: “On three things the world stands.” And when they expounded the Torah more and more through reasoning and reflection, and derived various derivatives and specifications in the commandments and statutes, and when they went on to enact ordinances and to make fences for the Torah, so too sayings of this kind multiplied, comprising laws, statutes and specifications. These are marked especially by their brevity and the precision of their language, and they were given a special name by which they are called in the language of the sages: halakhah.

The use of the word halakhah is very common in all the books of the Oral Torah. It is taken from Aramaic; its proper meaning is “law, custom,” and the Aramaean always says hilkheta where the Hebrew says mishpat in the sense of custom (*Musaf he-Arukh*).{v1_04_22:1} Hence every rule that comes from an act done among the people is called halakhah, as in “he saw an act and remembered the halakhah” (Sanhedrin 82a); and the rule that comes from the custom of the country is called hilkheta di-medinah (Kiddushin 38a). Halakhah is said of a matter agreed upon and received, in contrast to a matter that is argued by reasoning but on which there is not yet agreement, as in “If it is a halakhah, we will accept it; if it is a logical inference, there is a reply” (Yevamot 76b; Keritot 15b). Therefore the name halakhah came to be applied to every saying that comprises a fixed and agreed rule. And so they also used the word halakhah to indicate a decision in favor of one of two opposing views, and to fix the rule according to it, as in “and they fixed the halakhah permanently according to the words of the House of Shammai” (Yerushalmi Berakhot 1:1); and everything found in the Talmud as “the halakhah is according to so-and-so” is of this kind. By extension they used the word halakhah for fixed rules in matters that have no relation to the laws of the Torah; of this kind are “halakhot of proper conduct” (Berakhot 22b) and “halakhot of physicians” (Yerushalmi Yevamot 8:2); and likewise the rules of the combinations of the letters of the Name, by which — according to the belief of the sages of the Talmud — the world was created, they called by the name “halakhot of creation” (Sanhedrin 67b), and the rules of sorcery of all kinds they called “halakhot of sorcery” (also there). Under the concept of halakhah they also brought matters stated explicitly in the Torah, and called them “the bodies of halakhot” (Yerushalmi Shabbat 2:7), while the Babylonians called them “the bodies of Torah” (Shabbat 32a). In truth this name, “bodies of Torah,” is more common among the ancients (Ḥagigah end of ch. 1, and in the Tosefta there, ch. 2). And since they called the very words of the Torah by the name halakhah, we find that sometimes they also used the name halakhah for an explanation joined to Scripture, which we call midrash; for example: “through her a halakhah was newly established: an Ammonite man, but not an Ammonite woman” (Yerushalmi Yevamot 8:3). For this is nothing but a midrash that expounds the verse “An Ammonite or a Moabite shall not enter the assembly of the Lord” [Deuteronomy 23:4], which they explained as meaning just what is written: an Ammonite man, not an Ammonite woman. Some of the halakhot were described in short sayings, so that they were fluent in the mouth of all students, and they were called “regular halakhot” (Berakhot 22a). Similar to this is “the decided halakhah” (there, 31a), called “decided” because from the beginning of its foundation it was agreed and no one ever contested it; among the Jerusalemites (Yerushalmi there 5:1) it is called “the fixed halakhah.” The term halakhah is first found in the words of Hillel the Elder (Pesaḥim 66a, and in the Yerushalmi there); but it appears from his words that in his time the term “halakhah” was already commonly used to describe the concept now understood by it. Thus it follows from all this that every saying containing a fixed rule in the laws of the Torah — whether from what was received among them, or what is a derivative of the explanations, or a derivative of a derivative, or what was decreed and enacted to make a fence for the Torah; and likewise the decision of the sages in deciding between the views of their predecessors, or any decision in the court or in the house of study by majority, when they stood for a count and concluded the law decisively — every saying that comprises one of all these concepts is called by the name halakhah. During the generations of the Scribes the way of studying halakhah began; and from then on the halakhot were fruitful and multiplied and spread out exceedingly, and what at first was only a first trickle of water [Proverbs 17:14] became in the course of time a great and wide sea [Psalms 104:25], whose waters pass and flood over the Mishnah, the baraitot and the two Talmuds.
