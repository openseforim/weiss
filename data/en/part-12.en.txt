Chapter Twelve

Yose ben Yoezer of Zeredah, Joseph ben Yoḥanan of Jerusalem.

In those days and at that time, when the rod budded and arrogance blossomed [Ezekiel 7:10], and Yakim the high priest, whom the wicked king had made great, did wickedly and went on backsliding in the way of his heart [Isaiah 57:17], adding sin to sin, then two righteous men stood at the head of the people for the teaching and the testimony [Isaiah 8:20], namely: Yose ben Yoezer of Zeredah and Joseph ben Yoḥanan of Jerusalem. They were the first pair of the pairs mentioned in the books of the Talmuds, whose existence continued for several generations. We have already seen in early times the existence of pairs standing at the head of the people, and therefore this institution was not a new thing that had never been before; but it was renewed after it had ceased for a long time. Jehoshaphat king of Judah had already set a pair at the head of the people: one of that pair was appointed to be head in all matters of the Lord, and the second ruler in all the king's matters (above, ch. 4, in the note). And at the time of their first ascent from the exile, Zerubbabel and Jehozadak the priest were the heads; and after them Ezra, with his companion Nehemiah (Graetz vol. 3, p. 107). Although this chief office was divided between two persons, and by human nature agreement between the opinions of different persons is reached only rarely, nonetheless there was no division of opinion between them in those early days on any matter of religion. Indeed, we are told that only on one matter could they not reach a compromise, and Yose ben Yoezer and Joseph ben Yoḥanan are mentioned as the first to disagree. This matter was: whether one should lay hands on the head of the offering on a festival day, to fulfil the word of the Torah "and he shall lay his hand" [Leviticus 1:4], although the act of laying on hands is an action that they forbade as a shevut, both on the Sabbath and on a festival day; or whether we say that one should not lay hands, and the commandment of laying on hands is set aside before the shevut (Hagigah 2:2, and the Bavli and Yerushalmi there). And we are greatly astonished at the sight, when we see that on all the questions concerning the statutes and laws, even though they were divided in their opinions, they agreed in the end on one opinion, while on so light a question as this they could not reach a compromise! How did they not pay attention to the custom of their forebears? Surely at every festival they offered freewill offerings; why did they not look into the matter to see how this had been done up to their time? Especially as it appears that in matters of the offerings, all the days of the Temple, the sages of the generation did not disagree, neither in their own time nor in a later time; and this because the performance of the offerings was in the hands of the priests, and they knew from generation to generation how to act in their work. Therefore it surely did not happen by chance that they found no settlement for this matter, and it is clear that this was one of the questions urgent for their time. But what made this question urgent for that time? Perhaps its urgency was because another question also depends on this question: whether the laying on of hands is indispensable or not? Had they decided that the laying on of hands is indispensable, those living in distant lands would have had no way to send their offerings to the Temple of the Lord, since they could not lay hands on the offering; and this would easily have been a stumbling block to their goal, whose whole aim was to set as a firm foundation that the Temple in Jerusalem should be a holy bond for all who are called by the name of Israel, near and far. Therefore they left the matter undecided, dependent only on the will of the one who brings the offering, whether to come and lay hands or to send his offering (*Darkhei ha-Mishnah* p. 44). But if this were the main point of their dispute, whether the laying on of hands is indispensable, then why did they hang the dispute on laying on of hands on a festival day? Or perhaps we should say that the source from which the report of this matter came was not properly understood, and that when it says there, "this one says to lay hands and this one says not to lay hands," the intent was never about laying on of hands on a festival day; rather, the one who says not to lay hands means that the laying on of hands in general is not indispensable, and the one who says to lay hands means that it is indispensable. But this sense is refuted by what is described of this dispute in the last pair, Hillel and Shammai, from the dispute of whose disciples on this matter (ibid. Mishnah 3) it is clearly proved that the main dispute was on the matter of laying on of hands on a festival day; and so from this last pair we learn the intent of the dispute of the first pairs. Besides this, it is surely strange that in the end they decided and fixed the matter to lay hands (Beitzah 20a; Yerushalmi Hagigah 2:2); did they at that time no longer need a provision for those living in distant lands? Surely they needed it even more, since the dispersion of the Jews also kept growing greatly, and they needed it especially from the time a great community of Jews came to Rome. In truth, however, this question was urgent for them from another side. That is, because it is the axis on which the teaching of the Scribes as a whole turns; and therefore it was the urgent and fitting question for that time, which is the time of the rise of the Sadducees and of those who challenged the Oral Torah. And in this they found no settlement: if a thing that was set as a statute by the elders happens to conflict in some way with a statute of the Torah, does the statute of the sages have power to set aside the statute of the Torah or not? By the decision of this question the teaching of the elders and its force would surely be decided too. Now the act of laying on hands is not a work of labour, and in the plain sense of the words of the Torah it has no root in any way; only the elders set it as a statute to beware also of shevut, and in their view the act of laying on hands too falls under the category of shevut, and why should it be lesser? And they were in doubt whether to set aside the commandment of laying on hands because of the statute of the sages and the elders, to show the stringency of the words of the Scribes and thereby to subdue the Sadducees, or to say the reverse, that this statute has no power to stand against a statute of the Torah. Now if we consider the matter from this side, we find that it was no small thing on which their opinions were divided; rather, a great principle depends on it, which is the foundation of the whole teaching of the elders.

As we have seen in the question of the laying on of hands, so it is with all the enactments of the elders and with all the decrees that the sages of the generations decreed in those days: they were forced upon them by the time and by the events. For they never invented decrees, enactments and fences out of their own heart, to load them on people, if there was no need for them. And if for most of them the reason has been lost and we have only conjectures about them, this is only because afflictions and hardships without number have been a darkness between us and the earlier generations. From Yose ben Yoezer and Yosef ben Yoḥanan two decrees have also remained, which came in the wake of the time and the events. They decreed impurity on the land of the nations, and they decreed impurity on glass vessels (Shabbat 46b⚠1, and the Yerushalmi there). The decree of impurity on the land of the nations was without doubt due to their wish to put a check on the dispersion of the people in the lands of the gentiles. For because of the persecutions with which they were persecuted by the government, and through the troubles that came in the wake of the persecutions, many left their native land and went to live in the lands of the nations, to find a resting place for the sole of their foot. Therefore, to restrain the people from scattering in the land of the gentiles, they decreed impurity on the land of the nations, knowing⚠2 how many drew back because of impurity; and the masters of the Talmud have already testified to this (Yerushalmi Yoma 2:1, and the Bavli there), that impurity was harder for them than bloodshed. And perhaps they also had in this a hidden intention against the Ḥasidim, who at a later time were called by the name Essenes. For the first who rose up in war against Antiochus were the Ḥasidim, but not all of them had the strength to stand by their purpose; rather, many of the Ḥasidim drew back from the war and escaped for their lives to hide in the wilderness, and perhaps also escaped to foreign soil. This was evil in the eyes of the sages and the Ḥasidim who were in Jerusalem, and therefore they decreed impurity on the land of the nations, to check their dispersion. The second decree, by which they decreed impurity on glass vessels, was without doubt decreed because of new knowledge that had reached them about the making of glass and its material: it became known to them that its nature is close to that of earthenware, and therefore they treated it in several respects like earthenware and decreed impurity on it (Kelim ch. 2). From Yose ben Yoezer in particular three things are handed down concerning laws. But it was not he who invented them; rather, he held them as received from earlier Scribes, and he testified to them. He testified: "that the ayil kamtza [a kind of locust] is clean; that the liquids of the slaughterhouse [of the Temple] are clean; and that one who touches a corpse (is masav, or) mistaav [becomes impure]" (Eduyot 5:4; Sifra Shemini; Pesaḥim 15a; Avodah Zarah 37b). These halakhot are remarkable of their kind, in that they are handed down in the Aramaic language, which is not the case with any one of all the ancient halakhot; and we learn from this that this tradition is old. Without doubt the people of his generation were strict in these matters until Yose came and permitted them, and because of this they called him "Yose the Permitter." It is further clear from this that when he says dikrav be-mita mistaav, the stress is not on mistaav, as if he wanted to teach that one who touches a corpse becomes impure; for there would be no need of his testimony and his tradition for that, since it is written so in the Torah, nor would he deserve on that account to be called "Yose the Permitter," for he would have permitted nothing. Rather, the stress is on dikrav, and he means to say: only one who touches with certainty and knows that he touched shall be impure, but not one who is in doubt whether perhaps he became impure. And this halakhah was necessary for them at that time, when, since impurity was grave in their eyes, many without doubt held back from going out to war for fear of impurity, lest⚠3 they become impure through the slain of the sword. Therefore he ruled for the need of his hour that only one who certainly touches becomes impure, and against certain impurity one can guard oneself; but in a doubtful touch there is no impurity.{v1_06_12:1}

A moral saying of Yose ben Yoezer has also remained to us: "Let your house be a meeting place for the sages, and dust yourself in the dust of their feet, and drink their words with thirst" (Avot 1:4). How very fitting these words are to the events of his time, when the chief effort of the government and of its friends and flatterers among the sinning Jews was to keep the people far from the religion and from the Torah and to stop study and inquiry, until they were forced to do their deeds in the dark and to hide from the violence of the oppressor (2 Maccabees 14:5). All the books of the Torah or other holy writings that were found they condemned to burning or destruction, and whoever had them found with him, there was one law for him: to be put to death (Josephus 12:5). For this reason Yose roused the hearts of the good among the people to open their houses to be a meeting place for the sages, and not to let their hands go from the Torah, and to drink with thirst its words and the words of its teachers. From Yosef ben Yoḥanan too sayings have remained to us which also bear the marks of his time: "Let your house be open wide, and let the poor be members of your household, and do not talk much with the woman" (this too there). How precious these words are for his time and his generation, when many were pressed and forced to transgress their religion and for this reason became poor and needy; and so he warned them that each should help his fellow and strengthen his arms. And in particular there is in this a mark of the character of the sect of the Ḥasidim (the Essenes), who came out of the first Ḥasidim of that time. When we see that this Ḥasid widens the measure of the kindness that a man shows to his fellow so far as to make it a statute and a duty that his house be open wide and that the poor be counted as members of the household, is not the distance from a degree of kindness such as this to the custom of association in the manner that came about among the Essenes in the course of time no more than one step? That is, that all who entered the covenant of their society should have one law with the members of the society in their money and in all their possessions. So too the saying "do not talk much with the woman" was also a rule for the Essenes, except that they went with it to the furthest extreme and held back from association with women altogether, until most of them did not take wives. Yose ben Yoezer was called "the pious one of the priesthood" (Ḥagigah, end of ch. 2). And they said of him that his kerchief was midras [impure as a thing trodden on] for holy things. This sheds light on the character of his piety: for although he was the pious one of the priesthood, nevertheless he did not cross the bound of piety to sanctify and purify himself beyond measure, but guarded himself from impurity with the care proper for those who eat terumah, and no more. When these two sages died, the eshkolot ceased (Sotah 9:9). These were heads under whose hand were all the affairs of the people in all branches of office, and they were called ish kol ["a man of everything"] (Rav in the Bavli there). And it is rightly said that they ceased, for in the time of the Hasmoneans and after them, under the rulers of the house of Herod, the power of the sages diminished, and it was no longer as in former days.{v1_06_13:1} Of the life of Yosef ben Yoḥanan nothing has reached us beyond what has been told so far; of his origins and his death we know nothing. But the death of Yose ben Yoezer was evil and bitter, and his last words have remained to us, and they bear witness to the character of his spirit and the strength of his power in his faith. The high priest Yakim (Alcimus), the traitor to his people, was of his family (according to the account of the midrash, his sister's son), and this sinner denied even his own kin so as to carry out the evil he had planned to do. When he came with his schemes to the people, saying, "Peace shall be yours from the government!", they trusted him, saying, "Is he not of the seed of the priests? He will do us no harm and will not deceive the sons of his people." But their trust was false; for what he spoke to them with the smoothness of his tongue he did not fulfill. He laid his hand on sixty of the Ḥasidim, and by his command they were all killed (1 Maccabees 6:14–15⚠4). Even on Yosef⚠5 ben Yoezer, his kinsman, he had no pity, and he condemned him to death. And when this Ḥasid was led to be crucified, Yakim tried once more to turn him from his mind, and said to him: "See all the good and the honor that have come to me because of my ways and my deeds, and against this the great evil that has brought you to die like one of the base, for your stiff neck." But Yose answered him with calm of soul: "If it is so for those who anger God, how much more for those who do His will!" And Yakim said to him: "And who did His will more than you?" And Yose answered him: "If it is so for those who do His will, how much more for those who anger Him!" Words like these, from the mouth of a man as exemplary and pious as Yose, would break even a heart hard as rock; and indeed they did not miss their mark. For these last words of his went down into the chambers of the high priest's belly and came like murder into his bones, and he killed himself (Genesis Rabbah 65). But others told of him that he died a sudden death (the book of Maccabees and Josephus). And although the whole story is wrapped in the mantle of aggadah, nevertheless we can hold as clear truth that Yose ben Yoezer had a kinsman of his family who was called Yakim; that this Yakim is none other than Alcimus, who was high priest at that time; and that Yose was one of those killed for the sanctification of the Name who were killed at the command of Alcimus. The masters of the Talmud further mention that Yose ben Yoezer had a son who did not behave properly, and his father put him out of his inheritance and consecrated all that he had to Heaven. The son went his own way and married into [the family of] one of the servants of Jonathan the Hasmonean, and he grew very rich, for he found a precious pearl of great value and sold it to the treasurers who were over the Temple treasury. But the value of the pearl was thirteen aliyot (the name of a small vessel; see the *Arukh*) of gold denarii, and in the Temple treasury there were only seven aliyot. He took the seven that were there and left the rest of the price to the sanctuary as a gift. They stood up and wrote: "Yose ben Yoezer brought in one (referring to the property he consecrated) and his son took out seven" (Bava Batra 133a). Now it is not our purpose to investigate the basis and the root of this aggadah;{v1_06_14:1} only this much can we hold: that Yose ben Yoezer had a son who also was enticed to walk in a way that was not good, like many of the people of his generation, until his father removed him from his inheritance. And who knows whether the basis of this aggadah is not one with what is told, that Yose had a sister's son, namely Alcimus, who did not behave properly, and the two, the son and the sister's son, are one, and he is Alcimus and none other (R. A. Geiger, *Urschrift*, p. 64 and on). Besides all this we have nothing of the life of these sages. Nor do we have any matter or saying on which their names are recorded, except the sayings and the matters that we have mentioned. But when we see how careful they were to urge the people to make their houses a meeting place for the sages and to support inquiry, and also to inquire and to gain understanding with all their strength, we can judge from this that with all the hardships and troubles that befell them at that time they did not cease to inquire in the Torah; and without doubt many matters that are mentioned without the names of those who said them should be attributed to these two sages and to the sages of their time. And it is also for this reason that the generations that came after them praised them and said that from the days of Moses until their time there was no blemish in all the eshkolot that arose for Israel (Temurah 15b; Tosefta Bava Kamma ch. 8). And one of the later sages said that from the days of Moses until Yose ben Yoezer died they learned Torah like Moses our teacher, and from then on they did not learn Torah like Moses our teacher (Temurah there). The meaning is plain: until that time there were no disputes, and from their time on the disputes began, especially the disputes of the Sadducees against the Pharisees, because forgetfulness was growing. For even if it is clear that under the pressure of the troubles they did not cease to hold fast to the Torah, nevertheless it is not possible that in the days of the troubles that now came upon them some traditions were not forgotten.

Yose ben Yoezer fell before the sons of wickedness; Yosef ben Yoḥanan also died and was no more. The days of wrath and trouble that now came upon Israel held them back in their walking on the path of the Torah. The good among the people and those who cleaved to the Torah were weary of bearing any longer the pressure with which they were pressed by the government to turn them from their religion. A few men of the Ḥasidim rose up out of the zeal for the Lord of hosts that was within them, with Mattathias son of Yoḥanan the priest at their head, and they stood against a great army of the king's force and, leaning on the Lord, prevailed. Judah Maccabee his son did great valor and fought the wars of the Lord, and he prospered in all his wars. Judah fell on the heights of the field of slaughter. Jonathan his brother filled his place, and he too showed great valor; but he fell by guile into the hands of an enemy who brought him down to death. Then Simon his brother became head in his stead, and he completed the freedom of Israel. A time of upheaval such as this was not suited to increasing studies and enlarging the Torah. We do not know who the heads of the Torah were at that time, but we know of one office without the names of its holders and heads, namely the court of the Hasmoneans. In the days of Jonathan's rule there was a great court in Jerusalem; for when Jonathan sent a letter of peace to the people of the Lacedaemonians to renew the covenant between them and the Jews, the great council stood at the head of the signatories. So too, when Simon was appointed to be prince, the elders and the heads of the people are also mentioned (Josephus, *Antiquities* 13:5; 1 Maccabees 14:27). Now these heads were without doubt the court of the Hasmoneans, from whom some statutes and ordinances in matters of the Torah have been handed down to us, but they are few. The events from the beginning of the coming of the Greeks into their land had made them know that nothing harms the religion of the Jews more than association with the gentiles who are around them or who live among them. This association gave birth to the sect of the sinners who changed the holy religion and walked in the ways of the Greeks, and it turned many of Israel from the way of the Torah, to turn their hearts away from the Lord, the God of their fathers. Therefore they readily recognized that their first duty was to create a distance between themselves and the nations of the land. We have already seen that in the days of Yose ben Yoezer they decreed impurity on the land of the nations. All the more so, after they had attained their freedom and the rod of wickedness was broken, they strove greatly to keep far from them. For this reason too they decreed in the court of the Hasmoneans against any intimacy of the flesh between an Israelite and a foreign woman, with a stringency beyond what the Torah forbade (Avodah Zarah 36b). And certainly, because of this keeping apart, they found occasion to decree on other matters as well, especially in matters of impurity, for the keeping of purity was in their eyes like an iron wall between them and the nations of the land; but the decrees are not recorded under their name, for most of the decrees of the early sages are recorded anonymously, and the names of those who decreed them sank into oblivion. They further made an enactment to celebrate every year a festival of eight days in memory of the miracles that were done for them, and it was called Ḥanukkah, because first and foremost it was made for the dedication [ḥanukkat] of the altar. It runs from the twenty-fifth day of the month of Kislev onward for eight days. They established on it the kindling of lights, a light for each man and his household, and the celebration of this festival with hallel and thanksgiving to the name of the Lord (Shabbat 21b). This hallel is what had already been fixed at an early time to be said in the Temple in memory of the miracles and the salvation that were done for them (above, ch. 8, in the note), and it is called hallel because it begins with halleluyah and ends with halleluyah. And then, after Judah Maccabee had defeated his enemies and they had cleared the Temple that the Greeks had defiled, one of the poets of the time composed a psalm to give thanks to the Lord; it begins with hodu ["give thanks"] and ends with hodu, and therefore it is called hoda'ah ["thanksgiving"] (Psalm 118). In it is mentioned that the nations surrounded them to destroy them and to thrust them down, and the Lord helped them; they give thanks that God did not give them over to death, and that they came as of old to His sanctuary, and its gates were opened to them.{v1_06_15:1} This thanksgiving they established to be said on Ḥanukkah, and they attached it to the hallel that had already been fixed of old. These enactments without doubt went out from the court of the Hasmoneans. One more thing is told of them: that by their word the altar stones that the Greeks had defiled as abominations were stored away (Middot 1:6). They could not decide on their impurity, so they took a vote on them and stored them away (see *Megillat Ta'anit* ch. 8; Yoma 15⚠6; Avodah Zarah 52b) until Elijah comes and testifies concerning them (1 Maccabees 4:44–46). And all this is from the court of the Hasmoneans.{v1_06_15:2}

Besides the court of the Hasmoneans, the writers of the chronicles mention other, different names by which they call the sages of the Torah of that time, namely: Ḥasidim (the book of Maccabees, in many places) and elders (Josephus, *Antiquities* 12). The Ḥasidim were the first who were zealous with the zeal of the Lord of hosts and the zeal of His Torah, and they fought with all might and strength. In the course of the days of the war many drew back for fear, because they could not keep the religion and the commandments of the Torah properly according to their view. Little by little they went out of the measured character of piety into excess, and in the measure of their piety they went to the furthest extreme. They lived a life of hardship in solitude and spent their time in searching out the hidden. They took up customs that differed from the customs of the true Ḥasidim, their companions or their originals, from whom they had come out. Now these true Ḥasidim and the elders of this period are mostly mentioned by the names "the first Ḥasidim" and "the first elders." We have from them remnants and precious sayings. The remnants from the Ḥasidim include matters of their customs and their deeds, and the remnants from the elders are various matters in the midrash of the Torah and halakhah, and also words of moral instruction (*Darkhei ha-Mishnah*, p. 40). They said of the first Ḥasidim that they used to wait one hour and then pray, so that they might direct their hearts to their Father in heaven; and even if the king greeted them, and even if a snake was coiled around their heel, they did not interrupt (Berakhot 5:1); and they paid no heed to danger to life where a commandment was concerned.{v1_06_16:1} We see from this the degree of their piety: in every matter touching the keeping of the commandments of the Torah they stood ready to give their lives for it. Yet we are told of them in another place (Shabbat 121b) that the spirit of the Ḥasidim was not pleased with one who kills snakes and scorpions on the Sabbath. And although the title "the first" is not mentioned there, nevertheless, from what is made clear there of how careful they were in keeping the Sabbath, it appears that this refers to the first Ḥasidim, or at any rate to those who walked in their ways. Now with men for whom the keeping of the Sabbath weighed more than danger to life, it does not surprise us that they refrained from fighting on the Sabbath even when the danger was near, as happened in the days of Mattathias, when many of them were killed and did not stand up for their lives for fear of profaning the Sabbath; until afterward, when Mattathias saw that this was not the way to attain the aim of their desire, they took a vote and decided to fight and to stand up for their lives even on the Sabbath day. And if we want to know how far their piety reached with respect to the Sabbath, let us see what is told of them in the Talmud (Niddah 38a): the first Ḥasidim took care not to approach their wives on the fourth day [of the week], for if she conceived on that day, the days of her pregnancy would be completed, according to their view, on the Sabbath, and if she went into labor they would be forced to profane the Sabbath for her; and for this reason they refrained from approaching her on the fourth day. Therefore we rightly judge that the Ḥasidim whose spirit was not pleased with one who kills snakes and scorpions on the Sabbath are the very Ḥasidim known by the title "the first." And without doubt they were likewise strict in the other commandments of the Torah. We also find an excess by which they went beyond the commandments of the Torah in the commandment of tzitzit too (Menaḥot 40b). Toward people as well they conducted themselves by the measure of piety, and it is told of them that at all times they took care that no harm should come about through them. Therefore it was their way to hide thorns and broken glass in the midst of their fields, digging three handbreadths deep into the earth for them, so that the plow would not bring them up and people stumble on them (Bava Kamma 30a, and the Yerushalmi there 3:3).

But the early Ḥasidim were not the sect known by the name of Essenes. For the traits and customs that were found among the Essenes are found in their exact opposite among the early Ḥasidim. Among the Essenes it was a rule that moral preparation for perfection is not acquired by sacrifice and burnt offering, but by holiness of deeds and by extreme abstinence (Philo; and see Jost, *History of the Jewish Religion*, p. 104), and they also did not come to the Temple of the Lord to offer sacrifices. But of the early Ḥasidim we are told the opposite: that they would take nazirite vows of their own will in order to bring an offering (Tosefta Nedarim ch. 1), and that they longed to bring an offering (Nedarim 10b⚠7). The early Ḥasidim were neither a sect nor a special association. Rather, just as in all generations the people who went beyond the strict line of the law in keeping the commandments were called by the name "Ḥasidim," so they too were called by this name. But in all that is told of them we have no hint that they were among the noted sages of the Torah in their generation; rather, they were keepers of religion and commandment with great exactness, and their chief merit and excellence lay in deeds, and for this reason they were also recorded under the epithet "men of deed." One saying that is ascribed to the men of deed bears on its face the seal of its time. This saying is the one they used to say at the Rejoicing of the Water-Drawing: "Happy is he who has not sinned, and he who has sinned, may he be forgiven." And this saying fits the time after the persecution had ended and the Hasmoneans had defeated the Greeks. For in those days there were many who out of wickedness of heart followed the customs of the Greeks, and these are the "violent among the people" and "those who deal wickedly against the covenant" mentioned in the book of Daniel [Daniel 11:14, 11:32]; but there were also many who could not bear the wrath and fell into the net of temptation from faintness of heart, and turned from the religion against their will, under the compulsion of their oppressors. But when the wrath was over, they returned to God in complete repentance. And now, with regard to those who had stood in their integrity they said: "Happy is he who has not sinned"; and with regard to those who had sinned and returned they said: "and he who has sinned, may he be forgiven." And others said: "Happy is my youth, which has not shamed my old age," and by this they gave thanks to the Lord for having put strength in their hearts to stand in their integrity from beginning to end. But of those who had strayed from the Lord and returned, of them too it was said: "Happy is my old age, which atones for my youth" (Sukkah 53a). However, by the name "the early Elders" are meant the heads of the Torah and the Scribes. Most of the sayings brought in their name speak of matters of impurity and purity, and from their nature it appears that they were very strict in the matter of impurity. We also find that at times the court that came after them either itself retracted and ruled permitted in a matter that had formerly been treated as forbidden, or some of them said to be strict in a certain matter and some of them said to be lenient. And indeed it is surprising when we see that the early Elders disagreed about some matters, since we have an ancient tradition that there was no dispute in Israel except about the laying on of hands alone, and that this dispute remained the only one until, in the days of Hillel and Shammai — the last pair — three more matters were added about which they disagreed. How then shall we say here of the early Elders that they disagreed about several matters? But the matter will be settled if we consider the nature of the subjects about which they disagreed. For in truth the dispute did not come from a difference of understanding in the matter, that one said the law is so and the other said it is not so; rather, they differed in their opinions because of a difference in the degree of piety. And so that the truth may become clear to us, let us give attention to the subject of their dispute itself. Of the early Elders, some used to say half a kav of bones and half a log of blood for all purposes, and some used to say even a quarter-kav and a revi'it of blood for all purposes; and the court after them said a revi'it of blood and a quarter[-kav] of bones for terumah and for holy things, but not for the nazirite and those who perform the Passover offering (Nazir 53a; Tosefta Oholot ch. 4). Now we know that these Elders were among the Ḥasidim, except that beyond their piety they were also among the sages of the Torah whose whole occupation was the expounding of the Torah. And among all the qualities of the human soul there is none whose degrees are so many and so varied as piety. For the quality of piety does not depend on people's opinions and on the judgment of reason; rather, it is for the most part the offspring of excess in the imaginative faculty. And just as excess of the imaginative faculty has many and various degrees, so too has the quality of piety, which is its offspring. Now caution against impurity was the mark of piety in those days. Therefore those whose degree of piety was greater than the degree of piety of their fellows were also stricter about impurity than their fellows. And so we find that the difference in degree was the reason why some of them set a measure for impurity that is smaller than the measure that others set. But we have already seen that most of those who went to excess in the qualities of piety separated from their fellows and went their own way and became a special association, namely the association of the Essenes. And then, after those who went too far had separated from them, it was easy for the rest to come to agreement in their opinions and to go in one way, namely the middle way. Now we understand what is said of the decision of the court after them: they agreed to be strict where there was no harm from another side but there was a fence and a safeguard, namely in terumah and holy things; but they were lenient where the strictness would produce a burden for people, namely in the nazirite and those who perform the Passover offering. And the matter was the same in another halakhah in the laws of impurity: the early Elders were strict to declare the house impure from the sloping eave (katafras) inward (Tosefta Oholot ch. 4, and see Mishnah Oholot 3:3⚠8), and without doubt the opinion of all was not the same about this strictness. But the moderate ones had set aside their own opinion before the opinion of those who went to excess, and they returned to declare it pure after these had separated from them of their own accord and no longer exerted moral pressure on their opinions. Further halakhot were handed down in the name of the early Elders that relied on the midrash of Scripture. The first is what they decreed with regard to keeping away from the impurity of the menstruant. They said: the menstruant ought to be in her separation and kept away from her husband in every way, so that she should not adorn herself, nor paint her eyes, nor rouge her face; and they derived this from the plain sense of the verse that says "and of her that is sick in her impurity" [Leviticus 15:33] (Sifra, end of Metzora; Shabbat 64b). And this extra zeal too is one of the marks of piety. And without doubt the saying "Any woman who makes herself unsightly in the days of her menstruation, the spirit of the sages is pleased with her" (Avot de-Rabbi Natan ch. 2) was founded on this midrash. Another midrash of theirs has survived for us, and it too concerns the laws of impurity (Sifra, Vayikra Dibbura de-Ḥovah ch. 12): "'Or if a soul touch any unclean thing' [Leviticus 5:2] — one might think that even if he touched what touched impurities he would be liable; Scripture says: 'the carcass of an unclean beast, the carcass of unclean cattle, the carcass of unclean creeping things' [Leviticus 5:2] — just as these are distinctive in being fathers of impurity, so anything that is a father of impurity." Now, as for what they said, that one might think that touching anything that touched impurity makes one liable, it may be that those who were excessively strict really thought so, as we have seen that also in the days of Yose ben Yoezer there were those who wanted to be strict in the matter of contact with impurity and wished to go beyond the strict line of the law. If we examine the character of these two midrashim, we still do not find in them a departure from the plain sense of Scripture such as we find among the sages in the later generations. The second midrash is expounded by the rule of general and particular, and it explains the meaning of the general in the likeness of the particular. And we learn from this that neither Hillel, who expounded seven rules, nor R. Ishmael, who expounded thirteen, were the innovators of the midrash of the rules; rather, already long before them their rules were the key with which to expound the Torah. Further, a very precious saying is handed down in the name of the early Elders (Mekhilta Beshallaḥ, Massekhta de-Amalek ch. 2): "Thus the rule goes through all the generations: the rod with which Israel is smitten will in the end itself be smitten. Let every person learn proper conduct from Amalek, who came to destroy Israel, and the Holy One, blessed be He, destroyed him from this world and from the world to come, as it is said: 'for I will utterly blot out' [Exodus 17:14]. And so Pharaoh the wicked, who enslaved Israel — the Holy One, blessed be He, drowned him in the Sea of Reeds, as it is said: 'but He overthrew Pharaoh and his host in the Sea of Reeds' [Psalms 136:15]. And so every nation and kingdom that came to harm him, He judged them by the same judgment. Forever they said: with the measure with which a man measures, he is measured, as it is said: 'for in the thing wherein they dealt proudly against them' [Exodus 18:11]. Therefore it is said: 'Write this for a memorial in a book' [Exodus 17:14]." Now this saying as a whole is aimed especially at deeds that were done in those days, and against the efforts of the wicked kingdom to destroy the Jews and to remove the name of Israel from the land. And from the point of view of their ways of expounding the Torah, a new result emerges for us from this saying, namely: the first trace of the rule of extension (ribbui). For when the author of the saying proves that the Holy One, blessed be He, destroyed Amalek in this world and in the world to come from the fact that the blotting out of Amalek is doubled in the Torah, when it says: "for I will utterly blot out the remembrance of Amalek" [Exodus 17:14], this shows us that they expounded the doubling of the word "blot out" to mean that the act of blotting out would continue for a long time, namely in two worlds, in this one and in the one to come; and the reasoning of this midrash is the foundation of the rule of extension. And indeed the sense of doubled forms in the language for this meaning truly lies in the usage of the language, for it is clear that doubling indicates the force of the action; but in the course of time they widened this rule without limit.{v1_06_18:1} And we further learn from their words here that it was their opinion that the narrative part of the Torah was not given to tell the events alone, but that its chief purpose is to teach man knowledge and proper conduct; and they derived this from the saying of the Torah: "Write this for a memorial in a book," meaning that Moses was commanded to write in a book this event that befell them with Amalek, so that it would be a memorial for a later generation, from which it would learn to know the measure of the Holy One, blessed be He. This idea, which emerges for us from this ancient midrash ascribed to the early Elders — that the narrative part of the Torah was not given nor written except to teach man knowledge and proper conduct — is also a source opened for the sages of Israel in the later generations who strive to expound and to search the Torah to the end of its intentions: the masters of the plain sense in their way, the masters of philosophical inquiry in their way, and also the Kabbalists in their way. All of them accepted this idea, that the narratives in the Torah were not written only to tell the events, but that their purpose is more exalted, and they are told for moral and educational benefit; and in the view of the Kabbalists all the narratives of the Torah allude to exalted things and to the secrets of the Torah. But the midrash of the early Elders is the first that founded and explained this view.

In those days, when they prevailed over their oppressors who had sought to turn them away from their religion, then too a beginning was made to set up anew the ruined foundations and to restore the religion to its strength. They strove to uphold every Jewish trait, and without doubt various decrees were decreed at that time and ordinances were instituted to make a fence for the Torah. And it is self-evident that they also gave their mind to raising and exalting the Hebrew language. True, its rhetorical power was no longer what it had been in days of old, but its borders widened, so that they were able to speak of every subject; for Aramaic came to its aid, and it also took words from Greek to name various concepts that lacked fitting terms to describe them with Hebrew designations (Graetz, vol. 3, p. 84; *Mishpat Leshon ha-Mishnah*). This widening was of great use to them, for they could use the language in every respect, although the times and the opinions had now changed and new concepts had become known to them. And we have clear traces that the Hebrew language had not died out from among the people. Besides most of the songs that the poets of that time sang about the hardships that befell the nation and about the deliverance by which they were delivered, various other things have been handed down that tell us that they used the Hebrew language: in the popular proverbs that were current in their mouths, in the worship of God in the Temple and outside it, in popular song, in their written notes, and in engraving on a tablet. Such is their saying which was said in the Temple on the day when they circled the altar seven times, and at the time of their departure they said: "Beauty to you, O altar! Beauty to you, O altar!" or "To Yah and to you, O altar; to Yah and to you, O altar."{v1_06_19:1} So too the sayings of the Ḥasidim and the men of deed that were already cited above (Tosefta Sukkah ch. 4, and the Babylonian Talmud there 53a); and what they said at the Rejoicing of the Water-Drawing (Sukkah 5:4): "Our fathers who were in this place had their backs toward the Temple and their faces toward the east, and they bowed eastward to the sun; but as for us, our eyes are toward Yah." So too the pleasant phrase that was put in the mouth of the maidens who went out and danced in the vineyards on the Day of Atonement: "Young man, lift up your eyes and see what you choose; do not set your eyes on beauty, set your eyes on family" (end of Ta'anit); and this shows us that the women too used the Hebrew language in their songs. And the same appears from the saying of Bilgah, a priest's daughter⚠9, who apostatized in the days of the war with the Greeks (Tosefta Sukkah ch. 4, and the Babylonian Talmud 56b), who said: "Lykos, lykos [wolf, wolf], how long will you destroy the wealth of Israel, and you do not stand by them in the hour of distress!" So too the inscription that was written on the coins that Simeon the Hasmonean struck was in the Hebrew language and in Hebrew script (Graetz, p. 64). And in documents they used to write: "In the year such-and-such of So-and-so, high priest to God Most High" (Rosh Hashanah 18b). And in the days of the son of Yose ben Yoezer, who consecrated a large sum of his money, they wrote in the book of records that was in the Temple: "Yose ben Yoezer brought in one, and his son brought in six" (Bava Batra 133). And below the tree on which they hung the head and the hand of Nicanor they wrote: "The mouth that spoke in guilt and the hand that stretched out in arrogance" (Yerushalmi Ta'anit 2:12). And in the Hebrew language too they spoke their halakhot and their midrashim that were handed down to us in their names; from their brevity one may judge their origin, and that they were handed down in their very words. Such halakhot, marked by their brevity, are ancient, and many of them are found in the books of the Oral Torah; but because most of them were not handed down in the names of their first authors, we cannot know which of those old halakhot may be ascribed with certainty to that time. But this we can hold with certainty: that many halakhot, as well as new fences and decrees, were born at that time because of the events; and likewise they instituted many things to strike at the crown of their opponents the Sadducees, who at that time began to stand against the teaching of the Pharisees; for then the division of opinions in matters of religion began to come to light more and more.
