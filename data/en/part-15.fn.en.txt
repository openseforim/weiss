v1_08_02:1) According to the reading of the Bavli it was Joshua ben Peraḥiah who was in Alexandria and returned at the call of Simeon ben Shetaḥ; according to the reading of Yerushalmi Ḥagigah 2:2 and Sanhedrin 6:6, it was Judah ben Tabbai. Likewise there are two versions concerning Simeon's office of Nasi: according to one reading Simeon was Nasi and Judah ben Tabbai Av Beit Din, and some say the reverse (Bavli Ḥagigah 16a⚠10, and Yerushalmi there). Yet according to the reading of the Bavli in Sanhedrin it is possible to find an explanation of the matter. It is true that Joshua ben Peraḥiah fled from Jerusalem when John persecuted the Pharisees and Joshua was removed from his office of Nasi; he then went to Alexandria and stayed there until Simeon succeeded in overcoming the Sadducees. At that time Simeon wrote to Alexandria and brought Joshua ben Peraḥiah back to be Nasi as before his flight, with Simeon as Av Beit Din. And necessarily it was so, if we assume that Simeon's victory over the Sadducees was in Yannai's lifetime and that it was at that time that Simeon wrote to Alexandria; and it is not possible that this letter refers to anyone other than Joshua ben Peraḥiah. For until that time when Simeon came and brought back the Pharisees, the world was surely desolate (Kiddushin 66a); therefore it is certain that from the days of John, after he became a Sadducee, until the time of the victory there was no Nasi and no Av Beit Din from among the Pharisees, and so the last Nasi was Joshua ben Peraḥiah. If so, of whom is it fitting to say "my husband dwells with you"? This is without doubt aimed at the Nasi who is in Alexandria, and who was this Nasi if not Joshua ben Peraḥiah? Now it is proved that it was Joshua who returned because of Simeon's call, and certainly he returned to be Nasi as he had been before, and then Simeon was Av Beit Din. Afterward, when Yannai persecuted the Pharisees, many sages also fled to Alexandria, and it was a time of distress for the Pharisees until Yannai died; and perhaps during these days of distress Joshua died, for he was already old and advanced in years. After Yannai's death Simeon was Nasi and Judah ben Tabbai Av Beit Din. So, according to my conjecture, were the matters. And from this arose the difference of opinions among the later [authorities]. For it was received among them that Simeon was Av Beit Din, but they erred and thought that this was when Judah ben Tabbai was his colleague, and from this was born the mistaken reading that Judah ben Tabbai was Nasi. Likewise, since they knew that in the days of Yannai sages fled to Alexandria, as Josephus tells, they thought that Judah ben Tabbai was among those who fled; and believing that Judah was Simeon's colleague, they thought that the letter Simeon wrote was aimed at Judah; and from this arose the error in the Yerushalmi.

v1_08_04:2) The warning "thou shalt not decline from the sentence which they shall shew thee" is said in the section on the rebellious elder. But it is evident that this section was already expounded in early times in two directions. For although it was expounded of the rebellious elder, the plain sense of the verse shows that it speaks of every single person in Israel who rebels against the teaching of the sages and does not act according to their words, and it commands every single one to act "according to the sentence, which they ... shall shew thee" and "according to the sentence of the law which they shall teach thee" [Deuteronomy 17:10–11], and not to decline from the sentence which they shall show. This is the basis for what the later authorities said, that we are commanded concerning all the words of the Scribes by "thou shalt not decline." The proof that the matter was so understood in early times is that they expounded that everything said in the section applies also to the court of Yavneh (Sifre Shofetim §153, and in the Yerushalmi there); and this was not expounded with reference to the rebellious elder, for there is no death penalty for a ruling of the court in Yavneh (Sifre, ibid. §154). This midrash was without doubt expounded immediately after the destruction, when they set up their academy in Yavneh. See part 2, ch. 5, p. 38,⚠11 what I wrote on the first founding of this midrash.

v1_08_06:1) I will remark here on an aggadah told about Simeon: Once he saw a man running after his fellow into a ruin. Simeon ran after him and saw a sword in his hand, its blood dripping and the slain man writhing. He said to him: Wicked one, who killed this man, I or you? But what can I do, since your blood is not given into my hand, for the Torah said: "At the mouth of two witnesses ... shall he that is worthy of death be put to death" [Deuteronomy 17:6]. So is the reading in Sanhedrin (36b);⚠12 but in the Yerushalmi the reading is: What can I do, since your blood is not given into my hand; but He who knows thoughts will exact punishment, etc. Now in this incident there are two reasons for exemption: first, that Simeon is only one witness; second, that it is testimony by inference (umdana). But from Simeon's words one cannot prove in any way for which reason he said "your blood is not given into my hand": whether because he was only one witness or because his testimony was by inference. Therefore the baraita there, which says, "What is inference? For example, the incident of Simeon ben Shetaḥ," necessarily does not intend to prove that inference is not valid testimony, for this is not proven from the incident. Its intent is only to explain the halakhah in the Mishnah, which says "lest you say [from] inference," that this is not testimony; and this is a settled halakhah and needs no proof. The baraita rather brings an illustration of what testimony by inference is, and says: like the incident of Simeon ben Shetaḥ, where even had there been another witness with Simeon we could not have accepted their testimony. There is no need for the forced explanation of the Tosafists, who wrote that there was another witness with Simeon.

Is not one who is disqualified from testifying disqualified from judging (Niddah 49b)?

v1_08_06:3) See the commentary on the Torah by the scholar Knobel, on Exodus 21, who cites there that among the Indian and Greek lawgivers the law of retaliation was literal, not monetary; and according to the testimony of Diodorus, the ancient Egyptians had a statute that plotting witnesses were punished with the same punishment that would have fallen to the accused according to their testimony, and this agrees with the statute of the Torah as the Pharisees understood it. According to the statutes of the Romans in their Twelve Tables, the law of retaliation was literal if the litigants did not wish to settle for money; and later, monetary payment became a fixed statute.

v1_08_07:1) The Pharisees expounded "and they shall spread the cloth": the matters shall be made as clear as a cloth, and it is a figure of speech (Mekhilta, Mishpatim ch. 6 and ch. 13; Sifre Ki Tetze §236; Yerushalmi Ketubbot ch. 4); or they explained: the witnesses of this side and of that side come and make the matter as clear as a cloth, and this too is a figure of speech (Ketubbot 46a; Sifre, ibid.). The words "and spit in his face" they expounded by the rule of likeness and analogy: just as everywhere that the word panim is joined with the letter bet the meaning is not that the act is done on the surface of the face but before the face, as in "stood before you" (amad be-fanekha) and "stand before you" (hityatzev be-fanekha), so in "and spit be-fanav" the meaning is before the face. From this we learn that they sometimes expounded the words of the Torah figuratively; and we learn further that they interpreted by the rule of likeness and analogy, and this is the basis of the gezerah shavah, which is one of the hermeneutical rules (middot).

v1_08_10:2) We have already said that the traces of the opinions of the Sadducees are embedded in the midrashim of the Pharisees. For the most part they are found in the salka da'ta ("it might have entered your mind") of the midrashim, when the author of the midrash says "one might think," or "it is not so," and similar expressions. Therefore it is possible that the opinion of the Sadducees was that when the Torah said "Only the silver and the gold, etc." [Numbers 31:22], the intent is not to vessels but to raw lumps of metal. And there is a hint of the matter in Sifrei, portion Mattot, section 158: "Only the gold, etc.: vessels" (so the reading in the edition of R. Meir Friedmann, following the reading of a manuscript); "you say vessels, or does it mean only raw lumps? Behold, you reason, etc." And therefore this salka da'ta is perhaps the interpretation of the Sadducees, and the Pharisees said that it is not so, but that the impurity of vessels is learned by an a fortiori argument. And according to the Tosefta Kelim Bava Metzia chapter 2, Tosefta Ḥullin chapter 1 and the Bavli there (25a), raw metal vessels are pure and raw wooden vessels are impure; and it seems to me that the root of this halakhah too lies in the reason I have written, that the opinion of the Sadducees was that the verse "Only the silver, etc." refers to the raw lumps; therefore they changed⚠13 the law of raw metal vessels deliberately, declaring them pure against the opinion of the Sadducees.

v1_08_11:1) So, it seems to me, is the sense of the baraita in the Yerushalmi, which agrees with the baraita in the Bavli. And it is clear that the last ordinance was that the husband should do business with it; and since he does business with her ketubbah, there is no longer anything set aside for her ketubbah, and of necessity he must pledge all his property to her and write for her: "All the property I have is surety and guarantee for the ketubbah"; and this is also the sense of the Tosefta, chapter 12. And I have seen that the scholar Graetz, 533, emended the text of the Yerushalmi and read thus: "At first her ketubbah was deposited with her parents, and it was easy in his eyes to divorce her; they enacted that her ketubbah be with her husband — and they enacted that a man should buy with his wife's ketubbah cups, bowls and dishes of silver and gold (?), and still, when he was angry with her, he would say to her, 'Take your ketubbah'; and therefore they went back and enacted that a man should do business with his wife's ketubbah, until Simeon ben Shetaḥ came and enacted that he should write for her: 'All my property is surety for her ketubbah.'" This implies that already before Simeon they enacted that he do business with her ketubbah, and afterward Simeon enacted that he write "all my property, etc." But this is not so, for it is impossible to enact that he do business with her ketubbah unless he pledges his property to her, as is known to every student of the Talmud; and in the Tosefta too it is explicit that her ketubbah should be with her husband (that is, that he should do business with it) and that he should write, etc. — all of it the ordinance of Simeon; and in the Yerushalmi they said explicitly that Simeon enacted that he do business with her ketubbah. Therefore there is no room for Graetz's emendation, and the words of the Yerushalmi need no emendation, only explanation.
