v1_08_13:1) The scholar Graetz, p. 538, wrote that the expression "he spoke to them in Greek wisdom" refers to the diplomatic stratagems that they learned from the Greeks. We must remark on his words: (a) The word la'az fits only speaking in a foreign tongue. (b) Even if we say that the Greek language is one thing and Greek wisdom another, as the Gemara holds in Bava Kamma (83a), still we do not find in the whole Talmud two kinds of Greek wisdom; and if Greek wisdom in this place is the diplomatic art of the Greeks, then Greek wisdom in the other places of the Talmud too can only be explained as diplomacy, and that is impossible, for in Bava Kamma there they speak of teaching Greek wisdom to small children, and one does not teach diplomacy to boys. (c) In the Talmud in Bava Kamma it is shown explicitly that it made no distinction between the Greek wisdom in which the old man spoke and the Greek wisdom that the boys learned in the house of R. Gamaliel. (d) From the baraita itself it is shown that the Greek wisdom in which the old man spoke is the Greek wisdom that they forbade teaching to children, as is clear to one who studies it there. As for the objection that Greek wisdom was forbidden only at a later time, it is no objection; for we have already seen in several places that such enactments were enacted for their hour and for a passing cause, and they grew lax about them when the cause passed. Therefore we should not wonder if we find that they forbade Greek wisdom at a later time, although it had already been forbidden long before (see there in the Tosafot), just as we do not wonder about other decrees and enactments which, though founded in early times, were renewed in the later generations; and of such cases they said, "they forgot them and founded them again," or "they enacted and it was not accepted, and they enacted again and it was accepted." And therefore I have decided according to the plain sense of the baraita that the decree against Greek wisdom was first founded at that time.

v1_08_14:1) In three places it is told that Shemaiah and Avtalyon were of the seed of proselytes (R. Z. Frankel⚠10, in *Darkhei ha-Mishnah*, p. 37, wrote this of Shemaiah alone, and I do not know his source). In Yoma (71b) it is told that the high priest called them "sons of the gentiles." In Eduyot ch. 5 we learn that Akavya ben Mahalalel said about them, concerning their giving the proselyte woman the waters of the suspected wife to drink, "dugma [those like her] gave her to drink"; and the Yerushalmi, Mo'ed Katan 3:1, explains it as "dekhavatah," that is, that they were proselytes like her, and so Maimonides explained it. And in Gittin (57b) it is taught: "Of the descendants of Sennacherib some taught Torah in public. And who are they? Shemaiah and Avtalyon." But Graetz, p. 540, rejects this for two reasons: (a) that if so he should have said "dekhavatehon" [like them, plural] and not "dekhavatah"; (b) surely a proselyte is unfit to judge. The baraita of Gittin he did not mention, without doubt because the words "who are they? Shemaiah and Avtalyon" are not part of the baraita. But the proof from Yoma is surely strong; why did he not rely on it? And his fine point that he should have said "dekhavatehon" is a poor man's fine point, for the meaning of the Yerushalmi is: those who were like her gave her to drink. And so Rashi explained in Berakhot (19a), and it is stated explicitly in the Tosafot there, s.v. dugma, as we have explained. And even granting that there is no proof from there, there is also no proof for the opposite; so why should we push aside the words of the Gemara, which said explicitly, "And who are they? Shemaiah and Avtalyon"? And why should we close our eyes to the baraita in Yoma, which said explicitly that they were sons of proselytes? To this Graetz said that the baraita was adjusted according to the corrupt opinion of those who mistakenly thought they were proselytes, and perhaps originally there was a different reading in the baraita. But I do not understand why we should corrupt an explicit baraita because of a fine point in the Yerushalmi, and why we should say that the opinion that they were proselytes was founded on the Yerushalmi, which said "dekhavatah gave her to drink." Why not say the opposite: that the account in the baraita is true and the words of the Gemara in Gittin are true, and from this was born the explanation that explained the saying of the Mishnah, "dugma gave her to drink"? But Graetz thought that the baraita and the Gemara are corrupt, because it is impossible that they were proselytes, since proselytes are unfit to judge. But this is no proof, as we have written in the text, and it is made quite clear in Yevamot (45b, 102a) and Kiddushin (76b and Rashi there); and we learn in the Sifre that they said to Agrippa, "You are our brother," because his mother was of Israel. And according to the explanation of Rav Hai Gaon in the *Arukh*, entry dgm, one could argue much, for he explained "a likeness of the waters of the suspected wife" and not the actual waters of the suspected wife; rather they frightened her so that she would confess, in the way Solomon said, "Divide the living child" [1 Kings 3:25]. Graetz agreed with this, but I do not understand: if so, why did they excommunicate Akavya ben Mahalalel? Is it not just to use such a stratagem so that the truth may come out? And see Maimonides, who combined the two explanations.
