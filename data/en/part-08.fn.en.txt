v1_04_16:1) In the Yerushalmi, Berakhot 1:7, it speaks of words of the School of Hillel and calls them divrei soferim. And so in the Bavli, Rosh Hashanah (19a), it calls the days of Megillat Ta'anit divrei soferim, and so it is in Ta'anit (17b). And in Tractate Yadayim 3:2 they said that [the rule] that Holy Scriptures render the hands impure is from the words of the Scribes, and in truth it is one of the eighteen matters; see there in the commentators on the Mishnah. And in Niddah (69b) they said of the impurity of the flux of idolaters that it is from the words of the Scribes, and the essence of this decree is only from Hillel. For this decree is one with the decree mentioned in Shabbat (83a), that they decreed that they be like zavim in all respects, so that an Israelite child should not become familiar with them in sodomy (see Niddah 34a and Rashi's commentary); and if so, it is the very decree that they decreed on an idolatrous child, that he be impure through flux for this reason, and in the opinion of the Talmud (Shabbat 17b) it is one of the eighteen matters. And in the Sifra, parashat Shemini, ch. 8, it is stated: R. Yose says, there is no impurity of liquids by Torah law, only by the words of the Scribes. And so Rabbi proved, according to the words of R. Yose; and R. Eliezer⚠6 says there is no impurity of liquids at all, and his proof is from Yose ben Yo'ezer, who testified concerning the liquids of the slaughterhouse that they are clean. Now R. Yose, who said there is impurity of liquids by the words of the Scribes, necessarily did not mean the early Scribes, since in the days of Yose ben Yo'ezer the decree did not yet exist. And you will find much more of this kind.

v1_04_16:2) In *Bikkurim* for the year 5624, published by R. Naphtali Keller, there is a special essay by me, "Iyyun Tefillah," in which I spoke of the antiquity of the Eighteen Benedictions. My inquiries there are aimed against the author of *Hegyon Lev*, who assigned most of the prayers to the time of the Maccabees and some of them to the time after the destruction; and against this I said that the foundations on which he built are shaky, since we can just as well uphold the tradition of the Talmud that all the prayers are an ordinance of the Men of the Great Assembly, and that some of them were enacted while Israel was still in the Babylonian exile, namely those benedictions whose content shows that they were founded at a time when the Temple was destroyed and Israel was in a bad state. And my intention was to show what the sages of the Talmud saw that led them to say that they are so ancient. But for myself it did not enter my heart, and I did not think, that this was my opinion absolutely. And because in places I was not precise in my language in that essay, I gave the reader occasion to think that I had written my own opinion; and many have already asked me, and many more besides⚠7, for an account of this matter. For this reason I saw fit to remark on it here and to reveal my true intention.

v1_04_17:1) The verses that point to the time of the exile or after it are "Not unto us" etc. up to "He is their help and their shield" (115:1–11) and from "I love" up to "All men are liars" (115⚠8:1–11). Therefore it is possible that these twenty-two verses are a late addition from the time of the exile and after, alluding to the troubles that befell them and to the deliverance by which they were delivered from them. All the rest was composed earlier, and was composed first and foremost for the festival of Passover, and the time of its composition was in the days of Hezekiah, according to the opinion of one of the sages of the Talmud; and it is hinted also in Scripture (2 Chronicles 30:21): "and they praised the Lord day by day." And this is the explanation of the practice of skipping the verses of the addition on the last two⚠9 days of Passover, in memory of the ancient custom. The second part was composed first and foremost as thanksgiving for the deliverance in the days of the Maccabees, and therefore it belongs chiefly to Ḥanukkah; and in this you will find the answer to the question of the Gemara (Arakhin 10b), why Hallel is not said on Purim while it is said on Ḥanukkah.

v1_04_17:2) The benediction Avodah in the version before us is already corrected and is not the old one; see Berakhot (11b) and Yoma (68b), with Rashi's commentary. And the author of *Hegyon Lev* wrote that also the second benediction, namely Gevurot⚠10, did not remain in its original form, but several sayings were added to it which repeat again and again the mention of the resurrection of the dead, in order to remove [it] from the heart of the Sadducees, who denied the resurrection of the dead; and this sect of deniers arose only at a late time, in the days of Antigonus of Sokho. Thus far the words of the author of *Hegyon Lev*. But I am not comfortable with this effort to find in every saying of the early sages a special intention to oppose holders of other opinions. It is true that from the words of the sages of every generation, whether written or oral, we can judge the opinions that ruled at the time they were said or composed; and therefore from the second benediction too, "Atah Gibbor," which speaks from beginning to end of the principle of faith in the resurrection of the dead, one may judge that at the time it was composed the heads of the generation strove to establish faith in the resurrection of the dead in the heart of the people. But we see in this no necessity to conclude that they repeated the mention of this belief with great precision because its deniers were already a separate sect. Know for yourself: in the first benediction, "Avot," they also repeated the mention of the merit of the fathers, and this is certainly not aimed against a sect of deniers; rather they wished to strengthen trust in God's deliverance for the sake of the fathers of the world; so too they wished to strengthen trust in the reward of the world to come in the second benediction.

v1_04_18:2) The custom of bringing palm branches and willow to every rejoicing was an old custom, as is explicit in the Torah, which links the rejoicing to the taking of the four species. And in the days of Simeon the Hasmonean, when they captured the citadel in Jerusalem, they entered it and rejoiced with song and with palm branches (1 Maccabees ch. 3, 53⚠11); and in 2 Maccabees (ch. 10, 7⚠12) willow is mentioned in the rejoicing. And the custom of the water libation seems to have been founded on an aggadah which tells that the priests hid the fire of the altar, and when they came up from the exile Nehemiah found the place, but they found no fire in it, only water; and they took of the water and poured it on the altar, and a miracle happened, that the fire of God came and consumed the sacrifice. And that day they made a festival in memory of the miracle. So it is told in Maccabees (2 Maccabees 1:22–41⚠13). We do not wish to inquire into the nature of the miracle. But this emerges for us from it: that at that time they celebrated a festival on which, according to the story of the miracle, Nehemiah found the fire and poured water on the altar; and therefore this is the foundation of the custom of the water libation.

v1_04_19:1) They said of the decree on handling vessels on the Sabbath that according to the first decree (Tosefta Shabbat ch. 15) vessels may not be handled on the Sabbath except three: a knife for cutting pressed figs, a zomalistron [ladle] for a pot, and a small knife on the table. And they ascribed this (Shabbat 123b) to Nehemiah son of Hachaliah. Without doubt they relied on what they found in the book of Nehemiah 13, that he warned with great emphasis about the Sabbath; therefore they ascribed this decree to him as well. But if we consider the matter we easily find that it is not so. For this word zomalistron, which is a Greek name, already shows that this decree is late and cannot be earlier than the time of their acquaintance with the Greeks. For without doubt they did not know this utensil before the time of the Greeks, and it reached them only from them, together with its name; for had they known it, would it not have been called by a Hebrew or Aramaic name? Why did they name it with a Greek name? This being so, it follows that the decree too could not have existed before the time the Greeks came to the Land of Israel.

v1_04_21:1) The measure of derivation has its foundation in Scripture, and the expounders derived one derivative from another. For example: the Torah said that whoever eats leaven shall be cut off [Exodus 12:15] — the derivative from this: “no leaven shall be seen with you” [Exodus 13:7]; and from this another derivative: “but on the first day you shall remove leaven” [Exodus 12:15]. These derivatives are written explicitly in the Torah. The derivative from “you shall remove” is: on the night of the fourteenth one searches for leaven; the derivative from this: a place into which leaven is not brought; the derivative from this: the law of the rows in the wine cellar. I have no proof that when the Scribes taught the verse “but on the first day you shall remove leaven” they had already derived all these derivatives; but it is hardly possible otherwise, for if the teacher instructs his student that it is a commandment to remove leaven from the house so that none will be found there on Passover, he will at once tell him to take care to search in all the places where one usually brings leaven, so that he will not find it on Passover. So one thing came out of another, whose first root was Scripture, and in its derivatives the root was no longer recognizable.
