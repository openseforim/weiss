Book Five

The state of the Oral Torah at the end of the Temple period; midrash aggadah; the external books; the views of the Alexandrian Jews; the principles of beliefs and opinions; the relation of the Oral Torah to the religion of the Messianic Jews and to their writings; the destruction of the Temple.

Chapter Twenty-One

The state of the Oral Torah at the end of the Temple period

The bearers of the Torah in general were the sages of each and every generation, and in particular the great beit din in Jerusalem was the chief office from which instruction went out to all Israel. From the time Israel became a nation, judges stood at the head of the people to judge between blood and blood, between plea and plea, and in all matters of dispute. The number seventy for these heads is hinted at in the Torah (Numbers 11). And when in the Second Temple they founded the office of the great beit din, they set its number at seventy, with one as head over them (Sanhedrin 2a). The great court was called at first the Great Assembly, and later, when they came under the rule of the Greeks, its name was changed to Sanhedrin. In the course of time the heads of the Sanhedrin became two: the Nasi and the Av Beit Din. It is proper that priests and Levites sit in the Sanhedrin, but this is not required (Sifrei Shofetim, pisqa 153; Sanhedrin 14b), and any Israelite distinguished in Torah, in wisdom and in languages is fit to sit in the Sanhedrin (Menaḥot 65a). But they do not appoint those of flawed priestly lineage (Sanhedrin 32a).⚠1 A proselyte is unfit for capital cases unless his mother is an Israelite (Yevamot 45b and the parallels cited there), and so too a mamzer is unfit (Kiddushin 76a). In the days of Herod they enacted that the king does not judge and is not judged (Sanhedrin 18a). They do not seat in the Sanhedrin an old man, a eunuch, or one who has no children (Tosefta Sanhedrin ch. 7), nor any who are related to one another or to a litigant (Gemara there 27b, and Yerushalmi ch. 3, halakhah 6); and all the more so those unfit because of transgression (there 24b). The judge shall be wise and humble, with a good eye, a modest soul, a lowly spirit, a good heart, a good inclination and a good portion (Yerushalmi there ch. 1, and Bavli 88b; Tosefta ch. 7). The Sanhedrin of seventy-one was called the Great one; from them instruction went out to all Israel, and it sat in the Chamber of Hewn Stone (Bavli 86b and in many places). They judge a tribe, a false prophet and the high priest; by their word they go out to a discretionary war, add to the city and to the Temple courts, set up lesser Sanhedrins of twenty-three, and make an apostate city (there 2a); by their word they go out to measure for the slain (Sotah 45a), and they deal with a rebellious elder (Sanhedrin 86b); to them the suspected wife is brought up to be given to drink (Sotah 7a); and they appoint a court from among themselves to examine the lineage of priests and Levites (Middot 5:4; Kiddushin 76b; Tosefta Ḥagigah ch. 2). For these examiners of priestly lineage there was a special place behind the house of the curtain (Sifrei Koraḥ), and they were called the court of the priests; it was founded in the days of Herod. Their manner of sitting was like half a round threshing floor, with three rows of disciples of the sages before them, so that if one of the Sanhedrin was missing they could complete it (Sanhedrin 36b). If one needed to go out, he went out, provided there were at least twenty-three there (also there). The greatest of the Sanhedrin sat at the head, the Av Beit Din at his right, and the next greatest after him at his left (there, Tosefta ch. 7). Their sitting lasted from the morning daily offering to the afternoon daily offering. On Sabbaths and festivals they entered the house of study on the Temple Mount (there). Their give-and-take in judgment was deliberate. They begin with the youngest (Sanhedrin 32b), stand for a count and conclude the judgment by the majority, and according to the agreement of the majority the halakhah was established in Israel (there 88b, and Tosefta there). Great honor was given to the Nasi: when he entered, all the people stood and did not sit until he told them "Sit"; and to the Av Beit Din too they gave honor when he entered (Tosefta there). From this we see that the province of the Great Sanhedrin was mostly the founding of general statutes, or judging particular cases that touch the nation as a whole; only by exception did they judge cases of individuals; and theirs also was the right to decide all the doubts that arose in the lesser courts. Two kinds of court stood below it. The first: the court of twenty-three, which was called the lesser Sanhedrin; and the second: the court of three. The province of the court of twenty-three was to judge capital cases and every matter touching capital cases, while all other cases were judged in a court of three. The seat of the court of twenty-three was in the towns of Israel (Sanhedrin 88b and in many places), but not in all of them (there 2b; and see Tosefta ch. 7 and Tosefta Ḥagigah ch. 2). A court of three was set in each and every town. It seems that this court of three was part of the town's officers, who were seven (Tosefta Megillah ch. 2; and see Bavli there 26a⚠2), and who were also called the good men of the town; they dealt with the needs of the community, and this number for the town's officers already existed in early times (*Antiquities* 4, ch. 8). And although this office of the officers is also called a court of seven (also there), this is still no proof that [three] do not suffice to judge monetary cases.⚠3 At times they were accustomed to add up to seven, and this in matters that depended on the great court, and they chose from among themselves a special court to judge the matter (Sanhedrin 2a). Whoever is fit to judge capital cases is fit to sit in the Great Sanhedrin, but not everyone fit to judge monetary cases is fit to judge capital cases (there 32a). Only the courts of three that sat on the Temple Mount were all chosen and marked with all the qualities that the sages listed for the Great Sanhedrin. There were two courts of three each there, one sitting at the rampart and one on the Temple Mount. The judges among them were raised step by step until they were joined to the Great Sanhedrin. From among the chosen men of the towns they would raise [a man] to the court on the Temple Mount, from there to the rampart, and from there to the Chamber of Hewn Stone (Bavli there 88b; Yerushalmi ch. 1; Tosefta ch. 3; and Shekalim end of ch. 3). And so it is self-evident that the court of three on the Temple Mount had to be distinguished in the qualities of the members of the Great Sanhedrin. One special qualification was required of each of the members of the Sanhedrin, and that was ordination (semikhah). The ordination of elders was by three (Sanhedrin 2a). It is true that formerly each one appointed his own disciples, and it was enough that one ordained man ordained; but to publicize the matter the one ordaining joined two more to himself, even if they were not ordained. They called ordination "appointment" (minnui), because they did not practice laying their hands on the head of the one ordained as in the days of the prophets, but gave permission to judge and to instruct, and for this the title "appointment" fits. But later they gave honor to the house of Hillel and enacted that if a court appointed without the knowledge of the Nasi, its appointment is no appointment, while if a Nasi appointed without the knowledge of the court, his appointment is an appointment; then they enacted again that the court should appoint only with the knowledge of the Nasi, and the Nasi only with the knowledge of the court (Yerushalmi Sanhedrin 1:3). Ordination was required of all members of the Sanhedrin, both great and lesser; and a court of three likewise needed appointment for particular matters, such as judging cases of fines. These are the statutes and ordinances that were stated concerning the Sanhedrin and the courts, and by which they acted in the days of the Temple.

One of the matters with which the Sanhedrin were most occupied was the intercalation of the year and the sanctification of the month. From the very beginning Israel reckoned the months of the year by the renewal of the moon. The name ḥodesh [month, literally 'renewal'] already shows this, and so does yom ha-ḥodesh, the Hebrew name for the beginning of the month. Knowledge of the solar year and the lunar year is old, and the Torah hints at them: "and let them (the sun and the moon) be for seasons, and for days and years" [Genesis 1:14]. "Seasons" refers to the festivals, which are the appointed times of the year, and "years" refers to the reckoning of years made by the luminaries, whether by the sun or by the moon (Genesis Rabbah ch. 6, and see the commentators on the Torah). The Jews reckoned by the moon from of old, and this is hinted at in Psalms (104:19): "He appointed the moon for seasons." Ben Sira said (43:7) that the festivals of the Lord are reckoned by the moon. But since the solar year exceeds the lunar year by about eleven days, in the course of the years a disorder would arise in which the festival of Passover would not fall in the month of spring, nor the festival of Tabernacles in the month of ingathering. Therefore already in early times they enacted the intercalation of the year, and by this they reconciled the lunar year with the solar year (Jost). An old aggadah ascribes knowledge of the secret of intercalation to the early prophets (Ketubbot 112b; Yerushalmi Sanhedrin 1:2), and this at least is a sign of the antiquity of the enactment.{v1_11_03:1} The beginning of the religious year was Nisan, and from it they began to count for the festivals and for kings (Rosh Hashanah 2a). And since intercalating the year was a religious act and for a religious purpose, they said that one adds only Adar, which is the end of the religious year (Sanhedrin 12a). The reasons for intercalating the year were various. They were: on account of the spring, the fruit of the trees, and the tekufah [the solar season] (there 11b). Likewise if it was needed because of the roads, because of the rains, because of the Passover ovens, and because of the exiles of Israel who had set out from their places and had not yet arrived (there 11a). The intercalation of the year was by the court and with the consent of the Nasi, and this enactment already existed in the days of Rabban Gamaliel the Elder (Eduyot 7:7; Sanhedrin there; and Tosefta ch. 2). The intercalation of the year was by three, and they added to them up to seven when they could not agree, but all of them had to be invited (Bavli there). The place of intercalation was Jerusalem and the land of Judah, and only for a compelling reason did they intercalate in the Galilee as well; likewise, under necessity they intercalated from Rosh Hashanah onward and announced that in this year there would be two Adars. They intercalate only by day, and not in years of famine, nor in the Sabbatical year, nor on the eve of the Sabbatical year, nor in the year after it, nor from one year for the next, nor three years in a row (there). Just as the intercalation of the year was by the court and with the knowledge of the Nasi, so the sanctification of the month was done by the head of the court, and he says, "It is sanctified" (Rosh Hashanah 24a). They sanctified the month on the basis of sighting by two valid witnesses, with examination and inquiry. They used to light beacons on the mountaintops to inform those far away, and after the Kutim [Samaritans] spoiled this they enacted that messengers go out for six months: Nisan, Elul, Av, Tishrei, Kislev and Adar. In places the messengers did not reach, where they were in doubt whether the month had been made thirty days and full or twenty-nine days and defective, they kept the festivals two days because of the doubt (Rosh Hashanah 18a, 22b, 23b). Many enactments they made and great care they took in matters of the sanctification of the month, since the order of all the festivals depends on it; and therefore the matter was in the hands of an ordained court in the land of Israel. The power of the court in this was as strong as in the intercalation of the year; both depended on the act of the court and on its will. And they had a great principle: that the testimony of the month was entrusted to them (Rosh Hashanah 22a), and so too the intercalation of the year (Mekhilta Bo ch. 2), even if they acted in error, deliberately, or misled (Sifra Emor chs. 9 and 10, and Rosh Hashanah 25a). Yet although they relied on sighting witnesses, they did not refrain from making calculations, and they tried to bring agreement between their calculation and the testimony of the witnesses; only if the witnesses' words matched the calculation did they confirm and accept their testimony (Rosh Hashanah 23b). From this it appears that already in early times they were expert in the calculation of the calendar and reckoned at all times by the calculation. The foundations of this calculation were written in a hidden scroll and were called the secret of intercalation. But it was necessary that the intercalation of the year and of the month depend on the court, since it is a precious thing and not every hand should handle it. Without doubt this treasure was a tradition handed from Nasi to Nasi, and therefore intercalation was in the hand of the Nasi. Rabban Gamaliel the Second, who was Nasi after the destruction, preserved for us two of the rules of the calculation, which he had received from his father's father, Rabban Gamaliel the Elder: [first,] sometimes it [the moon] comes by a long course and sometimes by a short course; and the second: the renewal of the moon is not less than twenty-nine and a half days, two-thirds of an hour and seventy-three parts. These rules are remnants of the scroll of the secret of intercalation. It was very, very necessary to publicize the authority of the court to fix the month and the years, that is to say: that the Torah gave the sages the power for this. For the festivals and all their commandments depend on their fixing, and this is impossible without authorization from the Torah. They drew their authority from the words of the Torah: "which you shall proclaim" [Leviticus 23:2]. They interpreted it thus: "These are My appointed seasons: if you have proclaimed them, they are My appointed seasons; if not, they are not My appointed seasons" (Sifra Emor ch. 9). Thus he made the festivals depend on their proclaiming and their fixing. Yet it cannot be denied that in the eyes of the sages themselves the fixing of the month and the years was difficult, so that they said that one of the three things that were hard for Moses was the fixing of the month (Mekhilta Bo ch. 1; Rosh Hashanah 20a, and in many places). And no doubt among the people too there were some whose hearts troubled them over the matter, for fear lest Rosh Hashanah and Yom Kippur, which were in their eyes the holiest times, be fixed in error. To set their own minds and the minds of the people at rest in so great a matter, they searched and inquired and found that the Torah had given them this authority. Already in early times they believed that the fixing of the festivals was entrusted to the leaders of the generations, and we find an old aggadah: when the court above sees that the court below sanctifies the month, they too sanctify it (Yerushalmi Sanhedrin 1:1); and the later aggadists adorned this aggadah with various buds and blossoms, but all those aggadot go to one place: that the Torah gave the fixing of the festivals into the hand of the court. Yet there were also some among them who contested the view of the Pharisees in this matter. The author of the Book of Jubilees tries to uphold a fixing according to the reckoning of the solar year, and says of our reckoning and of the custom of intercalation that it disorders the years and the festivals and exchanges the holy for the profane and the profane for the holy.{v1_11_04:1} Besides these there were opponents of the sanctification of the month, namely the Kutim and the Boethusians, who tried to mislead the sages and the people in the sanctification of the month (Rosh Hashanah 22b). Surely the practice was evil in their eyes, and being unable to rise against the sages openly, they chose to do their deeds in darkness and by deceit. But as opposition to the teaching of the Pharisees never made them turn back from their way, and the reverse happened, for they gave still more care to a thing that their opponents denied and gave it publicity even at the cost of uprooting a matter of Torah, so they did in this matter too: the belief grew strong among them in their authority from the Torah to fix the years and the months, and by them also the festivals of the year; and they intercalated the year not only for a purely religious reason but also for other reasons that touch only remotely on matters of religion and the commandments. Therefore they kept this forever and at all times: that the court has the power and the authority to intercalate the year and to fix months, and whoever disputes them is as one who disputes the Torah (Rosh Hashanah 25).

So too instruction went out from the great court in Jerusalem in all matters of religion. If we examine with attention the broad literature of the Oral Torah, that is, the Mishnayot, the baraitot and the Talmuds, we find that all religious matters reached great completeness at the end of the Temple period, and that they were handed down by the leaders of the generations of each and every age. All the statutes and laws, the conditions of the commandments and their general limits, in blessings and prayers, in the commandments of the Sabbath and the festivals, in matters of marriage and forbidden unions, in monetary law and fines, in forbidden foods, and in some of the gifts to priests and to the poor, and the impurities and purities that apply in every place and at every time, and all the more so in the commandments that depend on the Land, and especially in matters of the sacrifices, and in what concerns impurities and purities, and capital law: all of these were established and standing in the days of the destruction; and all the traditions, derivations and statutes that came from the instruction of the Sanhedrin of all the generations were already set out in the form of fixed halakhot. Whoever looks into the Talmudic literature with broad and settled examination will easily find the fixed halakhot that can in no way be dated later than the destruction of the Temple. It is not our duty, nor is it needed for our purpose, to set them all out here; but it is fitting and needed to present some examples, to serve us as a line and a measure, so that by them we may recognize which of the halakhot belong in this class. The principal halakhot: on the time of the evening and the morning Shema{v1_11_04:2} and their blessings, and the manner of its reading (Berakhot ch. 1); the fixing of its sections (Tamid ch. 4); on the blessings over enjoyments (Berakhot ch. 6), the zimmun (ch. 7), the kiddush of the day, havdalah and the washing of hands for eating (ch. 8). Likewise the principal halakhot of the commandment of pe'ah: that pe'ah has no fixed measure (Pe'ah 1:1), and that one should not give less than one-sixtieth (mishnah 2), from what place in the field one leaves it (mishnah 3), and from what kind (mishnayot 4, 5); the things that divide a plot of a field to make it two with regard to pe'ah (2:1), whether for sown crops or for trees (mishnah 3); that in pe'ah everything depends on the kind of seed (mishnah 5); that the obligation of pe'ah is in the standing grain (mishnah 7); the measure of a field to be liable for pe'ah (3:6);{v1_11_05:1} that the commandment of pe'ah is from what is still attached [to the ground] unless there is danger in gathering it (4:1); how the poor reap it (mishnah 4), and at what time of day they gather (mishnah 5). In all these matters we find anonymous and fixed halakhot, and they are principal and old halakhot. The branches that spread out from the principles are mostly the work of the later Tannaim, who debated them in order to know and to make known their extension and their limits; the principles are mostly sunk within the branches, but to the eyes of sharp examination they are recognizable, and with open eyes we will soon learn to distinguish and separate everywhere the old from the new, and the primary categories from the derivatives. The disciples of Shammai and Hillel began this give-and-take, and after them went all the generations of the Tannaim after the destruction, who made those principles the foundation of all their teaching. Guided by this rule we will go securely in our examination of the whole Talmudic literature, and by it we can easily find the order of the halakhot that existed at the time of the destruction of the Temple; and we shall know that at that time the Oral Torah had already reached the level of completeness on all its sides: in matters of the gifts to priests and Levites, the rest of the land, the rest of the weekly Sabbath, the festivals, marriage and forbidden unions, capital law, monetary law and fines, the service of God in general and the sacrificial service in particular, and in all the laws of impurities and purities. Yet from the give-and-take of the later [sages] we cannot always take a decisive proof of how things were in the days of the Temple. For the latter days are not like the former days. While the Temple stood, the axis on which all their study turned was only practice. For their study was for the purpose of instruction, which went out as final from the great court, and by it the halakhah was established and became human action. But from that time onward a great part of the practice ceased, and their inquiry dealt also with matters that were no longer practiced. In their study they produced many things by way of conjecture; and when the ancients said (Yevamot 76b and the parallels cited there), "If it is a halakhah, we will accept it; but if it is a logical inference, there is a reply," we learn from this that they did not conceal that there are things they decided by reasoning and not from halakhah, for since these were no longer practiced, the halakhah had become hidden from them. Such decisions have only a theoretical value. We have already seen in several matters of capital law, which ceased a long time before the destruction, that the later [sages] set up conjectures in them that do not fit with the events that actually took place; and all the more so in the laws of the sacrificial service. Rabban Yoḥanan ben Zakkai, who had seen the Temple and was a priest, was once asked about one of the laws of the service, and he answered them: it is thus and thus. His disciples said to him: "Our master, you taught us the opposite." And he said to them: "You have spoken well; deeds that my hands did and my eyes saw, I have forgotten" (Sifrei Ḥukkat; Tosefta Parah ch. 3). Had it happened to his disciples too as it happened to him, that they forgot what they had heard and could not recall it, would they not have decided the law the opposite of what the halakhah and the custom had been in the time of the Temple? And sometimes the sages fix the law in the sacrificial service, which was not practiced in their time, by their reasoning, even against witnesses who had seen the Temple and testified that the custom was the opposite (Temurah 18a), or they weaken the testimony against the force of their reasoning (Eduyot 2:2). R. Ḥanina the Prefect of the Priests used to say of the priest who burned the [red] heifer that they sprinkled on him all seven [days]; for seven days before its burning they would separate the priest who burned the heifer from his home to the chamber facing the Birah, and sprinkle on him all seven days from all the sin-offerings that were there (Parah 3:1). Without doubt R. Ḥanina knew all this, for he saw the burning of the heifer with his own eyes, since it is known that at the end of the Temple period they made a heifer of purification (Tosefta there ch. 2). And yet R. Yose, who lived about four generations after him, paid no heed to his words, and said: "They sprinkled on him only on the third and the seventh [days]." And why? Because he was confident in the force of his reasoning and thought that so is the truth and so it ought to be. And Judah ben Bava⚠4 offered a suspensive guilt-offering every day except the day after Yom Kippur, one day, and swore by this abode that if they had let him he would have brought [one then too], but they told him: "Wait until you come into a [case of] doubt." None of this agrees with the sages after the destruction, who said that one brings a suspensive guilt-offering only for a matter whose deliberate violation incurs karet (Keritot 28a).⚠5 Therefore it follows for us from all this that in many matters on which the Tannaim decide the law, matters that were not practiced in their days and that they did not see, this is no proof that it was so in practice in the Temple period, and their decision has only the value of a theoretical conjecture. Even they themselves would not wish to say that it was so, but only that it ought to have been so. This is like what R. Akiva and R. Tarfon said: "Had we been in the Sanhedrin, no man would ever have been put to death" (Makkot end of ch. 1), while with this they cast no blame on the Sanhedrins that passed sentences of death. So it is in this matter: when the later sages speak of things that were practiced in the Temple period, they conjecture that the thing was thus and thus, because by their reasoning and their interpretation it ought to be so; but even if it was not so in practice, they cast no blame on the deeds of the ancients, for the rule stands firm: "You have only the court of your own days."

After we have found the straight path that will lead us to the place where the principles of the halakhot that embrace the whole Oral Torah, according to its divisions, are revealed to our eyes; and after we have made known that a reasoned examination of these principles will show us the quantity of old halakhot from the time before the destruction that are sunk in the branches, and that their total is so great that each and every part of the Oral Torah reached a fitting level of completeness, it is self-evident that there is no need to say much against the opinion (Jost)⚠6 that the statutes of civil law were not completed at all in the generation before the destruction. On the contrary: by our measure and our rule we find that the statutes of civil law stood at that time in a state of completeness on all sides. And so that our words may be believed, we will set out here, by way of example and sample, the principles of the halakhot on the laws of judicial proof, namely: proof by witnesses, the proof of the oath, the proof of the deed, and the proof of presumptive possession (ḥazakah), which have reached us clearly from the time before the destruction of the Temple. Proof by witnesses: testimony in civil cases is not less than two, to impose a money obligation by it; but the witnesses must be known men whose moral way of life is a surety for them that they will testify only the truth (*Antiquities* 4, ch. 8). Therefore they did not hand over testimony to the ignorant (amei ha-arets) nor accept it from them (Pesaḥim 49b). So too one may not accept testimony from one who is suspected of not telling the truth out of fear and dread or out of lightness of mind; therefore the woman and the slave are disqualified from testimony (Shevu'ot 30a; Bava Kamma 88a; *Antiquities*, ibid.). So too they do not accept testimony from one who cannot discern with a reasoned discernment the matters that he saw or knew, and the minor is included in this rule (Sanhedrin 52b), except that in some matters they accepted testimony about what a man saw when he was a minor (Ketubot 28a). Witnesses who are relatives of the litigants, or of each other, are disqualified (Sanhedrin 27b). And the rule in this was: his uncle and his uncle's son and anyone fit to inherit from him, and anyone who is related to him at that time; one who was a relative and became distant is fit (ibid., the first mishnah). Some are disqualified on account of wickedness. In the first rank of these is anyone who transgresses the religion out of greed for money, such as the dice player, the pigeon flier and the lender on interest: in their eyes these were like robbers, who defraud people of their money unlawfully. So too those who gather the produce of the Sabbatical year, who transgress the religion for the sake of money; and after they were compelled by the government to gather the produce of the Sabbatical year, and were under compulsion, they set the rule on the traders in Sabbatical produce. To these they also added one who is suspected of robbery and violence, even if he is not a robber and violent man known with certainty; and they further added the herdsmen, the tax collectors and the customs collectors (ibid. 25a). This last addition dates from the time of the decree that the Romans decreed, that the land of Judea should be like one of their provinces, and they set up tax collectors and customs collectors to collect the tax and the customs (above, ch. 19). Needless to say, a known and notorious robber, violent man and thief are disqualified, for according to their midrash these are stated explicitly in the Torah in the words "a violent witness" [Exodus 23:1]. One is disqualified because his testimony touches himself, and a man does not testify about himself (Ketubot 27b). That an interested party is disqualified from testimony was a settled and known halakhah, and the sages of all the generations spoke of it as of a clear and decided matter (Bava Batra 43b, and in many places). There are also those disqualified because of a defect in their bodily or mental make-up: the deaf-mute, the imbecile and the blind (Bava Batra 128a). A single witness does not stand in a money matter; but Rabban Gamaliel the Elder enacted that he should stand for the testimony about a woman [that her husband died], even though through this a benefit in a money matter also follows, in the collection of the ketubbah (Yevamot 122a). So too a single witness imposes an oath where two impose money (Shevu'ot 40a). This principle served as a foundation for the early heads of the Sanhedrin, and they founded their enactment on it: that a single witness who testifies about a deed that it has been paid, his testimony stands to impose an oath on the holder of the deed (Ketubot 87a, and see Shevu'ot 45a); and they already acted so before the time of Rabban Gamaliel the Elder (Gittin 34b). A great rule from of old was that a witness does not become a judge (Rosh Hashanah 25b and Makkot 12a), and the sages of the first generation after the destruction speak of it as of a matter long known and decided; without doubt this rule also held for civil cases. So too it was already decided in the house of study of Hillel that an agent does not become a witness (Kiddushin 43a). The witness must testify that he saw or knew the matter of the testimony; but if he testifies from the mouth of another witness from whom he heard it, they do not listen to him (Sanhedrin 29a). From this we can judge that the halakhah "from their mouth and not from their writing" (Yevamot 31b) already existed then, for this is very close to a witness from the mouth of a witness. This is also hinted at in another halakhah that is in any case old: a man writes his testimony on the deed and testifies about it after several years (Tosefta Ketubot ch. 2, and the Bavli there); and it appears from this that his written testimony is of no use unless he himself testifies by his own mouth. At the taking of the testimony the court would warn the witnesses with awe (Sanhedrin 3:7), and examine and question them (4:1) with seven inquiries (5:1); and once their testimony has been examined they cannot retract and say "we are liars"⚠7 (Tosefta ibid. ch. 6, and Yerushalmi Ketubot ch. 2). If they were found to be liars they were called plotting witnesses (edim zomemin), and they do to them as they plotted to do to the litigant. There is no law of plotting until they make the witnesses themselves plotters (Makkot 1:4), and until the verdict is given (1:5), and until both of them are proved plotters (1:6). A litigant may make witnesses who know testimony for him swear that they will come to the court to testify, but only those fit to testify (Shevu'ot 4:1); and most of the conditions of the oath of testimony are settled and fixed halakhot from the time of the Temple. Included in proof by witnesses is proof by a man's own admission; and a great and ancient rule in this is: the admission of a litigant is like a hundred witnesses (Tosefta Bava Metzi'a ch. 1; Gittin 64a, and in many places). Even witnesses who did not see the deed but testify that the defendant admitted before them that he owes, the defendant is held liable (Sanhedrin 29a); and in this the admission of a litigant is greater than the testimony of witnesses, for testimony of witnesses from the mouth of witnesses is not accepted, but from the mouth of a litigant it is accepted. And even if the admission does not come out of his mouth but is recognizable from his deeds, this is a full admission; this is learned from the halakhot of Admon (Ketubot 109a, 110a). But the admission of a litigant is of no effect in a matter in which he is liable as a fine (Bava Kamma 1:4), for anything that is on account of a fine one does not pay on his own word (Tosefta Bava Kamma ch. 1; Bavli 74b). But it appears that this halakhah was not widespread and known, for Rabban Gamaliel the Second, in the first generation after the destruction, did not know of it until R. Joshua reminded him (Bava Kamma, ibid.); and the payments of plotting witnesses, which is certainly a fine, were paid on their own admission until R. Akiva came and taught that they are not paid on their own word (Makkot 2b). The proof of the oath. If there are no witnesses, the oath decides. "All who take an oath prescribed in the Torah take the oath and do not pay" (Shevu'ot 7:1); and these are the ones who take an oath prescribed in the Torah: the four guardians (8:1), one who admits part of the claim (ibid. 6:1; Ketubot 108b), and one who denies everything when a single witness contradicts him (Shevu'ot 40a). Already in an early time they enacted oaths for other matters as well, in all of which the claimant swears and takes, and they called them "great enactments" (ibid. 44b; Ketubot 87); and they enacted several oaths without a claim (Shevu'ot 45a), and these enactments too are old (Ketubot 9:4). In the details of all these oaths we find early halakhot clarifying their conditions (Bava Metzi'a ch. 8 and Shevu'ot ch. 6). The proof of the deed. Writing a deed as security in buying and selling is a very ancient custom; it is found already in Jeremiah, and from the technical terms "the deed of the purchase, the sealed and the open" [Jeremiah 32:11, 14] we learn that the custom of writing deeds was a usual thing from days of old. Deeds of loan too are known in the time of the author of the book of Tobit (4:21), who in any case is not later than the time of Alexander the Great. At first the borrower would write the deed, and afterwards the sages permitted the lender to write it and others to sign; this permission was at the latest in the last half-century before the destruction (Eduyot 2:3). Ketubbah deeds in any case preceded the time of Simeon ben Shetaḥ (Ketubot 82b). They also used to write deeds of betrothal and deeds of tenancy and contracting (Bava Batra 167b), and we find settled and old halakhot on the precise wording of the deed (ibid. 165b), on its form (ibid. 160a), on its conditions (ibid. 167b) and on its validation (Ketubot 18b, 28a); and with bills of divorce Rabban Gamaliel the Elder made an enactment on the signing of the witnesses, so as to make validation easy (Gittin 34b, 37a).⚠8 The deed is strong, in that one collects with it from encumbered property (Bava Batra 175a), but an oath is required (Ketubot 86a). Certainly they wrote a guarantee explicitly, for if he did not write "all my property is pledged" he cannot collect from encumbered property (Bava Metzi'a 13b, according to the Sages), just as Simeon ben Shetaḥ enacted for the ketubbah that the husband should write "all my property is pledged for the ketubbah," for if he did not write so she cannot collect from encumbered property (Ketubot 82b, and see there 56b in Tosafot). With the ketubbah deed in particular they were very exacting, and they did not let anyone sign the ketubbot of women of proper lineage except priests, Levites and Israelites who marry their daughters into the priesthood (Yerushalmi Sanhedrin ch. 1, and Tosefta ch. 7). Remnants of the wording of the ketubbah deed have survived for us: the people of Jerusalem had a special wording, and the people of Galilee did as they did, but the people of Judea not so (Ketubot 52b). A divorced woman could not collect with a ketubbah deed unless there was a bill of divorce with it; and so with a deed of loan, one does not collect after the Sabbatical year unless there is a prozbul with it (Ketubot 89a). Besides these deeds, there were in use at that time deeds for every matter of law and dealings between people, such as wills, gifts, receipts, hypothecs, deeds of valuation, deeds of maintenance, deeds of refusal (mi'un) and deeds of selection of judges; and in the examination of all of them we find halakhot and laws whose origin is from the time of the Temple.{v1_11_08:1} The proof of presumptive possession (ḥazakah). This kind of proof is found in various matters and forms. A presumption: all that is in a man's hand is his, and one who wants to take it from his hand needs proof by witnesses. On this is founded the rule that runs through all civil law, "the one who takes from his fellow bears the burden of proof," and the Tannaim call it a great rule in law (Bava Kamma 46a); and an anonymous Mishnah treats it as a known and accepted premise and says: "for the one who takes from his fellow bears the burden of proof" (Ḥullin 134a; Teharot 4:12). Likewise R. Eliezer, who lived in the days of the Temple and after it, treats this rule as a known premise (Bikkurim 4:10).⚠9 And already the House of Hillel built several halakhot on the foundation of this rule, although they did not mention it explicitly (Ketubot 80b; Sotah 24a; Bava Batra 157a, 158a). There is no number to all the settled halakhot of which it is recognizable and clear, from their content and form and from the way the first sages after the destruction discuss them, that they are old and all rest on this rule. A presumption that comes from reasoned judgment: that is, when there is a doubt whether an act was done in such and such a way, and experience shows that at a certain time or under a particular condition people usually do it, then they relied on this reasoned judgment and held the matter to be done with certainty. On a presumption of this kind many great halakhot in civil law were founded already in an early time. Such is the old halakhah that a woman who comes and says "my husband is dead" is believed if there is peace between him and her and also peace in the world; and this is from their reasoning that a woman does not say "my husband is dead" of one with whom she lives in peace unless she knows for certain that he is dead; and they relied on this presumption also to take out the ketubbah from her husband's property (Yevamot 15:1, 2, 3). So too the halakhah: "[If a woman produced] two ketubbot and one bill of divorce ... she collects only one ketubbah," since one who divorces his wife and takes her back takes her back on the terms of the first ketubbah (Ketubot 9:9); in this they explained that one relies on what experience teaches. Many old halakhot in civil law are founded on this rule (Bava Batra 1:4; 2:6). If we look at most of the laws of buying and selling of houses and movables, on whether, when one sold these or those, some of the things subordinate to them are sold with them or not sold with them, we find that all their principles and their anonymous rules are early, and already the first Tannaim after the destruction discuss them; and the foundation of all of them is experience and reasoned judgment, which went down to the depth of the intention of people who sell an object, what they mean to sell with it (Bava Batra chs. 4 and 5). Close to this is the foundation in civil law of believing the plea of a claimant when he is like one who returns a lost object. Among the early sages it was also called "the mouth that forbade is the mouth that permitted"; the masters of the Jerusalem Talmud gave it the name "since" (me-aḥar) (Yerushalmi Shevu'ot 6:1 and 7:1), and the Bavli calls it miggo or mi-tokh. Old halakhot are founded on the miggo (Shevu'ot 6:1); and the enactment that one who finds a lost object does not take an oath (Gittin 48b), which in any case cannot be dated later than the time of Rabban Gamaliel the Elder, also had its basis in this reasoning, although they said: "for the sake of the good order of the world." A clear hint of it is found in the words of the sages of the generations of the Temple period, when R. Judah ha-Kohen⚠10 and R. Zechariah ben ha-Katsav testified about a daughter of Israel who was taken as a pledge in Ashkelon, and her family kept her away, and her witnesses testified about her that she had not secluded herself and had not been defiled; and the sages said: "If you believe that she was taken as a pledge, believe that she did not seclude herself and was not defiled" (Ketubot 26b⚠11). By this they hint that it is right to believe that she was not defiled, for only from the mouth of the witnesses who say she was not defiled do we know that she was taken as a pledge. The presumption by which some object is held to be a man's property because it has been in his hand for a known length of time and no one contests it: on this foundation are built great and settled halakhot, and all of them are old. The halakhah on how long the object must be in his hand for him to have presumptive possession of it, and which objects are subject to presumptive possession (Bava Batra 3:1); in which lands they applied this law of presumption (3:2); that the presumption must be founded on a plea (3:3); who are those who have no presumption in an object in their hands (ibid.); which things are not subject to presumption (3:5 and 3:6). Of this kind of presumption various branches are found, and on the foundation of all of them there are old halakhot; but it is not our purpose to go into every detail. Since all the halakhot that have passed before us are clearly old and their origin is from before the time of the Temple's end, as is sometimes made clear from the name of their authors, or from their content and form, and above all from the fact that they are fixed premises for the discussions of the Tannaim of the first generation after the destruction, it is therefore clear to us from these details of the statutes of civil law that this area had already risen to the level of completeness in the days of the Temple. From this area we shall judge all the parts of the Oral Torah, and know that their principles and roots were in full completeness at the end of the days of the Temple. In matters of forbidden foods concerning non-sacred things, the old halakhot are few compared with the great quantity to which this area had already expanded at the end of the first century after the destruction and onward. All the matters on which the Sadducees disputed with the Pharisees concern only matters of impurity and purity, the service in the Temple, the Sabbath and the festivals, capital and civil law; there is not even one matter in their dispute that concerns the forbidden and the permitted in non-sacred things. So too in the whole mass of disputes between the House of Shammai and the House of Hillel we find in this area only two or three disputes. And of all the sages named by name who lived before the destruction of the Temple we have only a small remnant on this. So too the old settled halakhot are only a very little compared with the halakhot in the Talmud; but on the whole, the Oral Torah at the end of the days of the Temple was broad and complete in all its aspects.

However, their work and their great occupation were not only in the practical and halakhic part; they also turned their faces to the part of beliefs and opinions. From the many sayings and the mass of aggadic midrashim that have survived for us from that time we can estimate the completeness of this area. It appears that the effort to widen the bounds of learning in beliefs and opinions was great, for only so can we understand the wonderful sight that we meet in the last century of the days of the Temple. In this century the Jewish religion found lovers among the gentiles, so that many of the peoples of the lands were drawn after them. Little by little it became known among the gentiles that their idols were vanity, and they could no longer find rest for the feelings of their hearts, which longed for something exalted and for a true faith, in the beliefs that their fathers had left them as an inheritance; and a strong longing came upon the good among them to seek the true God, and they found Him in the religion of the Jews. In the land of Adiabene Queen Helena became a Jew, and after some time her son Izates, who reigned in his father's place, also entered the religion of the Jews. In Rome and in Damascus, and likewise in all the places where Greeks lived among the Jews, many of the people of the land became Jews. Without doubt there were many whose hearts were not set on proselytes: Shammai and all who followed him did not want them, and they held that "proselytes are as hard for Israel as a scab." Of many they said that they were only "proselytes of the kings' table," who became Jews only for some other motive, for the sake of power and honor; and even of those who became Jews for the sake of Heaven they said: What use is it if they enter the religion of the Jews, if they know no more than the foundation and do not know its laws and halakhot? And if they learn, who knows whether they will want to learn it according to the teaching of the Pharisees, with all the minutiae of the Oral Torah? Therefore it is better not to accept them and to push them away. But Hillel and his house loved proselytes and strove to bring many into the religion of the Jews, and he paid no heed to all these complaints. For his rule was: even if one becomes a Jew for some other motive, and even if he has learned only the essential foundation of the religion, and even if he does not know how to conduct himself at once according to the whole teaching of the Pharisees, nevertheless, if he wishes to become a Jew with a pure and understanding heart, we accept him, for in the course of time he will become a true Jew. There is no doubt that this is the foundation of the three aggadot that are told of Shammai and Hillel. The first: that a gentile came to them to become a proselyte on condition that they make him high priest. The second: that a gentile wanted them to convert him and teach him the whole Torah while he stood on one foot. The third: that one wanted to become a proselyte on condition that he accept the Written Torah alone and not the Oral Torah (Shabbat 30b, 31a). All three Shammai rejected, and Hillel accepted them. The foundation of these aggadot is to depict Hillel's practice in accepting proselytes: that he did not heed the complaints of Shammai and his house. Even if the proselyte has some other motive, and knows only the essential foundation of the religion, and does not know how to conduct himself as the Pharisees do, nevertheless it is right to accept him and bring him into the religion of the Jews, and in the course of time he will mend everything.{v1_11_10:1} Most of the proselytes were women, but for men the acceptance of the religion was hard because of circumcision. We do not find that at any time they accepted proselytes without circumcision; for how could they bring him into the covenant when he would at once break a commandment laid upon him? That would be like one who immerses with a creeping thing in his hand. Nevertheless, circumcision was not with them an essential without which there is no Judaism. For we see that some of the early sages held that a proselyte who immersed but was not circumcised, for some compelling reason, is a proselyte and is obligated in all the commandments (Yevamot 46b). And the difference of opinion on this already existed at the time when Izates became a Jew: one of the sages of his land taught him the religion of the Jews and told him that he could accept the religion even if he was not circumcised, so as not to arouse thereby the jealousy of his people; for it is possible to worship the true God even while uncircumcised, if only he cleaves to the teaching of the Jews with all his strength. But a sage from Galilee said to him: How long will you neglect circumcision? For it is not enough to know the ways of God and His teachings; one is also bound to keep His commandments, and circumcision is a commandment from which there is no exemption in any way. Had circumcision been considered an essential on which Judaism depends, such a difference of opinion would have been impossible. So too we have seen that in the opinion of some of the sages immersion alone was enough to bring a gentile into the covenant of the religion. Nevertheless they never accepted proselytes without circumcision, and therefore fewer men than women became Jews. Now if we ask what it was that moved the hearts of many of the peoples of the land to become Jews: certainly they did not long to enter the covenant with the Jews because of their worldly success, for the political situation under the Roman oppressor was very bad. Nor can one believe that the multitude of practical commandments and warnings, decrees and fences drew their hearts after them. It can only be that the right beliefs and opinions that they found among the Jews did this. Therefore we say rightly that just as the sages of the generations set their heart on the practical part of the Torah, so they also expounded and inquired into the moral and speculative part, to winnow and purify⚠12 the duties of the heart and the beliefs and opinions that are the foundations of the Jewish religion; and these drew the hearts of the gentiles after them. And in truth the inquiries in these matters, they and their results, reached a high level, as will become clear before us in the following chapters.
