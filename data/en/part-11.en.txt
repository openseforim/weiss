Book Three

The events after the death of Simeon the Just; Yose ben Yoezer and Yosef ben Yoḥanan; Pharisees, Sadducees, Essenes; Yoḥanan the High Priest; Joshua ben Peraḥiah; Nittai of Arbel; Simeon ben Shetaḥ; Judah ben Tabbai; the Kutim [Samaritans]; the victory of the Pharisees; Shemaiah and Avtalyon; the Benei Bathyra.

Chapter Eleven

The events after the death of Simeon the Just.

The first period, which extends from the time of their return from the exile until the end of the Great Assembly, is like a wasteland without a way, which no human foot crosses. Thorns and nettles over the whole of it keep us from going hither and thither. The history of the Torah and of wisdom is wrapped in a thick cloud; only from time to time a small spark of light tears through the clouds, flashes and shines a little, but like lightning it is spent and gone, and in a moment — it is no more. Then the darkness returns, and as at the beginning we grope for the wall like the blind [Isaiah 59:10]. The second period too, toward which we now turn our faces, has not changed its black appearance at its beginning. Although it is plain before our eyes how many were the works of the sages who worked during the time that the Great Assembly lasted, as we perceive the great deeds that suddenly come to light and are seen, nevertheless in darkness it comes and in darkness it goes, and in darkness are covered the names of all the sages who worked and labored on the holy soil [cf. Ecclesiastes 6:4]. And this darkness has stretched out its hands further, from the first period into the second. We have indeed seen the affliction of the people and its distress, which befell it at the hands of Ptolemy I when he went up to Jerusalem and conquered it and multiplied in it mourning and lamentation [Lamentations 2:5]; the days of this king's rule lasted forty years. In the course of these days Onias, son of Jaddua, and after him his son Simeon, called Simeon the Just, stood at the post of the high priesthood (Josephus, *Antiquities* book 12, ch. 2), and in the days of Simeon the Great Assembly ceased. But although the oppression that pressed upon them grew heavy, so that because of it the assembly was abolished, nevertheless it did not find the strength to turn the sages of the Torah away from the goal set before them. The new ideas, too, that the Greeks brought into their land, which found favor in the eyes of the people and spread among them little by little, did not overpower their faith and the teachings of the Torah. Rather the reverse: as the new ideas grew and prospered, so they too girded themselves with strength to hold fast to the Torah and the tradition of their fathers, so that the degenerate shoots of a strange vine [Jeremiah 2:21] should not entwine themselves and climb over them. And as is done with bees — though one guards oneself from their stings, one does not for that reason loathe their honey — so they guarded themselves from the harm of Greek wisdom, but they did not reject it entirely; rather they were sated with its good and planted all that is useful in it in their own soil, so that already in the second half of the first century after the Greeks came to the land of Judah there were in it wise and well-known men who knew Greek wisdom and knew their language and its ways like one of them.

For not many days passed after the death of Ptolemy Lagi, and a new king arose over Egypt [Exodus 1:8], Ptolemy Philadelphus, the son of the first, who did not know the oppression of his father and did not acknowledge his evil. This king did many good things for the benefit of learning, and it was also at his request that the Torah was translated into Greek. According to the testimony of Josephus (*Antiquities* book 12, ch. 2) and Philo (*Life of Moses*), the translators were sages from the land of Israel. The work of translation was done at one time, not piece by piece and book after book; it was made public when it was completed, and the day on which it was completed they made a festival for all generations. Even in the days of Philo the Jews in Alexandria celebrated the day of the deed (Graetz, vol. 3, 478). R. Judah too, in the Talmud (Megillah 9a, and Yerushalmi there), said that our masters permitted writing (the Torah) in Greek; from this it appears that his view is that it was written by the sages of the land of Israel and that our masters permitted the thing. We do not wish to decide that the translation was made in the land of Israel, as the legend has it; but this appears clear: that it was made by sages of Israel and with the consent of the sages in Jerusalem, and that they permitted the translation and its use for every sacred matter. And we cannot imagine their consent without their knowledge of the writing and language of the Greeks and of their wisdom. The legend of seventy or seventy-two elders who translated the Torah is very old. The letter of Aristeas, which contains the story of the translation, already mentions the name "the translation of the Seventy," and surely not long after its appearance it became known by this name. The story of the bringing of seventy-two elders into seventy-two separate places, where each one translated the whole Torah, and that it was a miracle from heaven that all agreed on one meaning (Megillah there, and Josephus),⚠1 is nothing but an aggadah, and one does not learn from aggadah. But the root of the legend, that the translation was made by seventy elders, is true: namely, that the translation was confirmed by the Sanhedrin in Jerusalem and they agreed that it should be used for every sacred matter, as R. Judah and Rabban Simeon ben Gamaliel testified (Megillah, Bavli and Yerushalmi, there) that our masters in the land of Israel permitted writing in Greek; and since the number of the Sanhedrin was seventy, they called it the translation of the Seventy. Yet for all that, it is sometimes spoken of with disparagement. The aggadah tells of five elders who wrote the Torah in Greek, and that day was as hard for Israel as the day on which the calf was made (Tractate Soferim ch. 1). This narrator admits that it was made by the sages of Israel, for without doubt he knew the tradition of R. Judah mentioned above, and nevertheless he speaks in disparagement of the translation. This is only because in changing times opinions changed regarding the study of Greek wisdom. The opinions of his time overpowered this narrator himself, for in his time perhaps the ancient decree from the days of the Hasmoneans was in force, which was redoubled at a later time, that one must not study Greek wisdom, as will be made clear further below; and therefore the translation was displeasing to him. But against this it is related of Rabban Simeon ben Gamaliel (Yerushalmi Megillah ch. 1) that when the sages permitted writing the Torah in Greek, they examined and found that the Torah can be translated adequately only into Greek. This is because R. Simeon was one of the sages who loved the Greek language and their wisdom, as all the patriarchs from Hillel onward loved it, and therefore his mouth was full of praise and glory for the Greek translation. And perhaps for another reason as well some sages disparaged this translation, because it translates according to the plain sense and paid no heed to the Oral Torah; and therefore the later ones said the opposite of the words of R. Simeon. He used to say that they examined and found that the Torah can be translated adequately only into Greek, and they said that the Torah cannot be translated adequately, because the translation does not heed the interpretations of the Oral Torah, and in their view any translation that is not according to the interpretations is not an adequate translation.

If we pay attention to the periods of ancient times, we will find that opinions about the Septuagint changed from time to time. At first their mouth was full of praise and glory for it, for in the work of translation they saw that honor was given to the Torah, and in the honor of the Torah they saw their own honor; and at that time the Jews did not begrudge any other wisdom, but honored all wisdom. Opinions reached another stage from the time when the interpretations of the Oral Torah were fruitful and multiplied; from then on many disparaged the Greek translation. To this the aggadah alludes: "Moses asked that the Mishnah too should be in writing, but because the Holy One, blessed be He, foresaw that the nations of the world would in future translate the Torah and read it in Greek, and they would say, 'We are Israel' .... The Holy One, blessed be He, said to him: You say that you are My children; I know only the one who has My mysteries with him. And which is that? It is the Mishnah, which was given orally" (Tanḥuma and Exodus Rabbah, portion Ki Tissa, and in many places). The idea of this aggadah is to disparage the Greek translation, because in it all the fine points of the Torah and the fine points of the Scribes, which are the substance of the Oral Torah, are impossible. At a still later time they rejected it altogether because of the heretics; for the heretics disputed with Israel over words of Torah, and also other scholars from among the sages of the nations reproached them and opposed the teachings of the Torah, such as the gentile sage Celsus, against whom the Christian Origen stood up. And who brought both of these into the secret of the Torah if not the Greek translation? Therefore it was as thorns in the eyes of the Jews.{v1_06_03:1} So indeed it was in ancient days. But times have changed, and with them our opinions on this matter too. Through the Septuagint the springs of the wisdom of the Torah were opened to the nations as well, and the prophecies of our prophets and the wisdom of our sages became known to them; and it was almost the means that helps us to fulfil our mission in the world according to the promises of the prophets: to bring the knowledge of one God to all the peoples besides us. We have no reason to disparage this translation any more, but rather to praise and glorify it, as did the ancients, the sages of that generation in the land of Israel, who were pleased with it when it appeared and approved it. It is true that in one respect this translation did harm in recent times too, for some interpreters of Scripture, on its basis, laid hands on our holy writings and emended them and spoiled their pleasant words, saying that according to the translation the translators had such-and-such a reading before them, not like our reading. But this is not the fault of the translation; it is the fault of those emenders, who did not consider the matter with faithful care and did not think that perhaps the error is in the translation and not in the text of Scripture, or that perhaps the translator translated and altered for the sake of a clearer explanation and turned aside from the main reading, as Onkelos did in his translation — according to the statement of Menaḥem ben Saruq in the *Maḥberet* (entry eitan) — that he came only to broaden the explanation so that it would sit well; and especially since it is known that busy hands have touched this translation and corrupted it, deliberately or by mistake, from some other motive or unknowingly.

Now, after we have seen that this translation, made in the days of Ptolemy Philadelphus, came from the hands of the sages of Israel and that our masters approved it,{v1_06_03:2} we understand why in several places in this translation there are clear traces of words of tradition. And even though the hands of falsifiers have touched the Septuagint that is before us, nevertheless everything in it that agrees with the tradition testifies of itself that it is original, as it came from the hands of the first translators, and that neither the hands of falsifiers nor the lying pen of scribes [Jeremiah 8:8] have touched it. The Jews in the land of Israel were never suspected of falsifying the translation, for it was not in their hands; and the Alexandrian Jews, had they been falsifiers, would not have done so for the benefit of the midrash of the sages of the land of Israel; and all the more so other falsifiers, who do not believe in the tradition and in the interpretations of the sages, would not falsify and alter the translation so that it would agree with the tradition. Therefore what is found in this translation in agreement with the tradition certainly came from the hands of the first translators and is the interpretation received from of old. For example, their translation of "on the morrow of the Sabbath" [Leviticus 23:11], said of the offering of the omer, which they interpret as the day after the first day of the festival of unleavened bread; this agrees with the interpretation of the Talmudic sages (Menaḥot 65a). Likewise they translated "and has no son," said in the law of levirate marriage (Deuteronomy 25:5), as if it were written "and has no seed," and this too agrees with the halakhah that a daughter also exempts from levirate marriage. Moreover, they sometimes added words in their translation to agree with the halakhah. "And the master of the house shall come near to God" (Exodus 22:6),⚠2 said in the law of the bailees, they interpreted to mean that coming near to God is that he shall come near to God to confirm his word by an oath, and they added the word "and he shall swear"; this agrees with the interpretation of the rabbis (Mekhilta Mishpatim ch. 15, and in the Gemara in many places), who also interpreted it as an oath.{v1_06_04:1} Thus it follows for us that there are in the Septuagint interpretations that the translators gave according to the tradition and according to the well-known halakhah, and they interpreted so wherever the way of translation and its purpose would bear it, that is, where it does not need an expansion of words if one wishes to translate according to the received interpretation. Now, when we find in it translations that are opposed to the interpretations of the Oral Torah, we cannot judge from this that at the time it was made those interpretations were not yet known. Only in a case where, had he wished to translate according to the interpretation of the Oral Torah, he would not have needed to expand the words — then we will say: had the interpretation been known in their time, they would surely have put it into their translation, as they put in other interpretations of this kind, as we have seen. But if he would have needed to expand the words, then we will say: even if he did not translate in agreement with the interpretation of the Oral Torah, this is still no proof that that interpretation was not known in their time; for it is possible that it was known and nevertheless he did not use it, since the task of the translator is only to carry the words of the book he translates over into the language into which he translates, not to expand the words with interpretations, by which he would be forced to add many more words than the words of the translated book. Therefore, if we find in the Septuagint that it translated "an eye for an eye" [Exodus 21:24] according to its plain sense and its wording, and not according to the interpretation of the Oral Torah, which explained this verse as monetary compensation, this is no proof that the interpretation of the halakhah was not yet known, for we will say that he refrained from translating according to the received interpretation because he would have needed to expand the words, and this is against the purpose of the translator. But from what is found in the Septuagint, that it translated "as he had schemed to do to his brother" [Deuteronomy 19:19] as if it were written "as he had wickedly sought to do to his brother," explaining zamam in the sense of zimmah [wickedness], from this there is certainly proof that it did not interpret according to the interpretation of the halakhah, which explained that they are punished only for the intention, and if they have killed they are not put to death. And although this halakhah is not clearly from the tradition, and in our opinion the truth is that according to the tradition they are put to death even if they have killed, only that they are punished for the intention as well; at any rate, according to the translation it appears that its intention is that there is punishment only for the deed, not for the intention.

This Greek translation was used by the Jews living in Alexandria and by those living in the cities on the coast of the Mediterranean Sea, the cities in which the Greeks had settled and the Jews lived among them. From of old there was an ordinance in Israel to read portions of the Torah when they gathered together for the service of the Lord. It was also the custom that men of understanding translated the Torah into Aramaic, the language understood by the mass of the people. Therefore, for the purpose of this reading, which was ancient, and for the purpose of instruction in the Torah of the Lord, the Torah was written in Greek in Alexandria. But a doubt arose: was this Greek translation proper according to the religion, to read from it to fulfil the obligation of the reading? This question was put to the rabbis in the land of Israel, and they permitted the thing. This follows explicitly from the account of R. Judah, who relates that our masters permitted Greek because of the incident of King Ptolemy (Megillah 9a). The sages examined and found that the Torah can be translated adequately only into Greek (Yerushalmi there). From this it appears that the sages confirmed the translation, and by their will the permission went forth to write the Torah in Greek and to use it for every sacred matter. On the basis of this permission it came about in practice that the study of the Torah and the public reading were all in the Greek language, so that within a short time they had no Hebrew copy, for they did not need it and did not understand it. The grandson of Joshua ben Sira (about one hundred and forty years before the Christian era) testified in the preface to his Greek translation of his grandfather's book that when he stayed in Alexandria he found that the Alexandrian Jews differed greatly in their spiritual character from their brethren in the land of Israel; and not only is there a great difference between the one and the other with regard to the Holy Scriptures, but there is also a difference between them in their understanding of them. And what was this difference between them with regard to the Holy Scriptures? It is only that the Jews in the land of Israel had the Holy Scriptures in their own language, which is Hebrew, while in Alexandria they had only a Greek translation. At that time, when the sages saw the need to publish their ruling concerning the reading of the Torah in the Greek translation, surely at the same period they also decided the law regarding the various portions of the Torah that by statute and custom were fixed to be recited, such as the portion of the suspected wife, the declaration of the tithe, and the reading of the Shema. Likewise the question arose concerning the fixed prayer and the grace after meals: is there in all these any strictness as to the language in which they are said? And a halakhah dealing with these matters has also been preserved for us (Sotah 7:1): "These are said in any language: the portion of the suspected wife, the declaration of the tithe, the reading of the Shema, the prayer, the grace after meals, the oath of testimony and the oath of deposit." It is very likely that at the time when they permitted Greek because of the incident of King Ptolemy, this halakhah too was newly made. And although the sages of the generation already had great reason to speak of this at the beginning of the days of the Scribes, since already then there were many who understood only Aramaic, nevertheless it appears from the language of the Mishnah that it is aimed at other languages; for in saying "these are said in any language" it hints that it speaks of yet another language besides Aramaic. And which is it? It is Greek. This deficiency in knowledge of Hebrew grew very strong in Alexandria, and it came upon them from their mingling with the Greeks, the inhabitants of the land. And the same happened to them in all the places in which the Greeks had settled, and especially in the cities on the coast of the Mediterranean Sea. Those places were called by the general name "the province of the sea" (*Moreh Nevukhei ha-Zeman*, gate 12). And since they knew in the land of Israel that the Jews living in those provinces were not expert in matters of religion and in the fine points of the laws, by the measure of the land of Israel, and also on account of their levity they were not trusted by the sages of the land of Israel in several matters touching religion, they therefore prepared and also founded a new ordinance (Gittin 1:1): that every agent who brings a bill of divorce from the province of the sea must say "It was written in my presence and signed in my presence." For in any case there were fixed courts in those provinces; and even if at a later time it was not so, nevertheless at first there were fixed courts in Alexandria (Tosefta Pe'ah ch. 4, Ketubbot ch. 3, and Bavli there 25a),{v1_06_05:1} and this was at a time when they still clung strongly to the religion of their fathers. Therefore, if the agent said that the bill was written and signed in his presence, they knew whether it had been written according to its law, for he could tell them whether it had been done in a court or not (see Gittin 2b, and Rashi's commentary). And although a bill of divorce need not be given before a court, at any rate it must be made under the direction of an expert; therefore a court is needed when it is made in a province whose people know nothing of the matters of the Torah and its fine points. And perhaps this itself was included in the ordinance: not to rely on a bill of divorce that was not made in a court of experts. Such an ordinance was necessary for another reason too: since the language used by the inhabitants of these provinces was Greek, and this language was not understood by every court in the land of Israel, they therefore ordained that at any rate the agent who brings a bill of divorce from these provinces should testify — for without doubt it was written in their language — to the truth of the writing of the bill and of its signing in a court of experts. This was the foundation of the ordinance in the first place; but at a later time its foundation and its reason were already forgotten, so that the sages of the Talmud were already in doubt about its reason and the grounds of its foundation.{v1_06_06:1}

However, in the Land of Israel too the study of the Torah was at times held back in its course, but this delay did not cause them to draw away from religion and from the Torah. Rather, at all times their longing for it grew stronger in the face of the misfortunes of the time. The heavy war, too, that took place between Antiochus the Great and Ptolemy the Third and the Fourth, kings of Egypt, brought many and grievous troubles upon Israel. But these troubles did not befall them because they were Jews, for Antiochus the Great was not an enemy of the Jews out of hatred of their religion; rather, the evils came in the wake of the war and harmed the Jews as they harmed all the inhabitants of the land of Syria and all the other lands around it over which the war spread its wings. Then too, when Antiochus prevailed and the land of Judah remained under his rule as before, they rested from their hardships and recovered from their troubles, and all the children of Israel now had relief and peace. At that time the head was the high priest Simeon son of Onias the Second, son of Simeon the Just the First. To Simeon the Second, too, the people of his generation gave a title of praise and called him: Simeon the Just. In his days or a little after him lived Joshua ben Sira. This Joshua praises in his book the excellence of Simeon, and calls him great among the Jews and one who sought the good of his people. He tells of him that he founded the holy service and did many good things for Israel by completing the building of the Temple (Ben Sira ch. 50). And he succeeded in doing all this with the help of Antiochus. He gave them permission to complete the building of the House, and commanded his general, who was in Judea at that time, that everything needed for completing the building of the House be given them free, without money. These days of tranquillity were fit for working to strengthen the religion. The great court in Jerusalem, too, was at that time in its full strength. For in the letter that Antiochus wrote to his general, Antiochus praises the Jews who received him with honour when he came to Jerusalem and went out to meet him with all the men of the council at their head. He also wrote that it should be a statute not to be transgressed that the Jews should have the right to conduct all their affairs according to the law of their Torah, and that the men of the great council should be exempt from the poll tax (Josephus, *Antiquities* 12, 3). It follows that at that time the great court was in its full strength. And indeed, if we are told that in the days of Simeon the Just the First the Great Assembly ceased, this was only a temporary cessation. In a short time it returned to its former state, but under another name: for instead of the name Great Assembly its name was changed to be called by the name Sanhedrin. For since this was the highest office in the nation, it is self-evident that they were forced to call it by a name understood by the Greeks, who were the rulers of the land. However, this too is self-evident: that at the time of the great persecution of the Jews in the days of the son of Antiochus the Great, when the government tried to abolish the Torah and the religion as a whole, this office too was abolished for some time.

For the days of tranquillity under the rule of Antiochus the Great did not last long. Antiochus Epiphanes, who reigned after him, was not like his father; from the day he came to the throne until his end he was an enemy of the Jews whose like had not been before him, nor arose after him among all the Seleucid kings of Syria and all the Ptolemaic kings of Egypt. At the beginning of the reign of this oppressor, Antigonus of Sokho stood at the head of the Sanhedrin. True, according to what is related in the Mishnah he received [the tradition] from Simeon the Just, who was one of the last of the Great Assembly, and this Simeon the First lived a long time before the reign of Antiochus Epiphanes. But if we consider the chain of high priests who lived and worked after Simeon the First, we find that in the days after Simeon there was no reason to transfer the headship of the Sanhedrin from the high priest to another man from among the people. For after Simeon's death his brother Eleazar became priest, and after him Manasseh, his younger brother, who held his priesthood only for a short time, for he died, and in his place came Onias, the son of Simeon; and after his death Simeon the Second became priest, who was also given the epithet "the Just." All these high priests were faithful to the Lord and to Israel. And all the historians of the Jews of that time, when they mention the heads of the great court, mention only the high priest, for the high priest was always the first and the head in the great court of every period. And now we see Antigonus, who was not a priest, rise and become head. This is impossible without a compelling cause, and this fact will shed light on the time of Antigonus; we will judge from it that Antigonus was not head after Simeon, nor during all that time from Simeon the First to Simeon the Second. But after the death of Simeon the Second, when Antiochus Epiphanes came to the throne, and not long after the beginning of his reign this oppressor appointed high priests one after another, all of whom rebelled against the Lord and forsook the Torah and tried to support the government's wish to destroy the Jewish religion: at that time we see a great cause that moved them to set Antigonus at the head of the Sanhedrin; or at any rate he was the man who held fast to the Torah of the Lord. And indeed, if we are told in the Mishnah that he received from Simeon the Just, who was one of the last of the Great Assembly, and handed on to Yose ben Yoezer, we will hold from this report two things: one, that he received from Simeon the Just, but not from the first, rather from the second; and the second, that he handed on to Yose ben Yoezer, whose time falls together with that of Antigonus in close succession. But we will not hold that he received from the Simeon who was one of the last of the Great Assembly; and we say that the sameness of the names caused the compiler of the Mishnah to describe Simeon the Second with the title said of Simeon the First.

The personal history of Antigonus is hidden from us, and of his teachings no more than one saying has reached us, namely (Avot 1:3): "Be not like servants who serve the master on condition of receiving a reward, but be like servants who serve the master not on condition of receiving a reward; and let the fear of Heaven be upon you." From the content of this saying one may judge that the days of his headship were evil; and therefore, to strengthen the hearts of the faltering who asked, Where is the reward hoped for by those who do good?, Antigonus taught them that one should not serve the Lord for the sake of the reward, but we should do the good because it is good in itself. And this is not a contradiction of the promises of the Torah (Jost p. 106), which promises good and recompense to those who do good; for Antigonus too never thought that the doing of good is not the cause of reward, but he believed with perfect faith in recompense; only his warning is that our doing of good should not be for this aim alone, and that we should not cease to do good if we see that the hoped-for reward has not reached us, but we should do the good even on condition of not receiving a reward. And it is very likely that the spirit of the opinions of the Greek sages spoke through his tongue when he said that we should not do the good out of hope of receiving a reward; this is exactly the view of the Stoics, who say that there is no merit in doing good and righteousness when they are done for the sake of the pleasure that comes to a person from doing good, or for some other motive, for we do the good because it is good in itself, and in the doing of good is found the reward of good. However, as befell the sages at all times and in all generations, whose words the perverse of heart twisted, whether from folly or from malice, and saw in them thoughts and opinions that the sages had not thought, so it befell Antigonus too. Two of his disciples twisted and corrupted his pleasant words, saying that he meant by his words to teach that there is no judgment and reckoning above; and by this they were caught in the net of heresy and went astray. These disciples were called by name Zadok and Boethus, and from them came the sect of the Sadducees. So the aggadah went and spread (Avot de-Rabbi Natan ch. 5). Whether this is only an exaggerated aggadah or the plain truth, one thing we can hold, and it is that in the days of Antigonus a sect arose that opposed the Pharisees. Which of the events of that time was the cause of its rise has not been preserved for us, and we would seek something certain in vain. Nor should one wonder that a cloud rests upon so great an event as the separation of the Sadducees, which left its mark on all matters of religion and lasted several hundred years. The hardships of the time did this. The evil and the troubles from without grew greater day by day, until a man's tongue grows weary of describing them in words: oppression, plunder and murder spread over the full breadth of the land of Judah "as the waters cover the sea" [Isaiah 11:9]; the religion of the Torah was trampled and those who studied it were persecuted; and in the abyss of such events this event sank, as other events concerning religion and the Torah no doubt sank. It is indeed very possible that at the outset there was no separation of sects, but the basis of the dispute was the rivalry of one man with another. For already from of old the high priesthood had been reserved for the Levitical priests, the sons of Zadok. And it befitted them, for at all times they kept the way of the Lord, and when Israel went astray from the Lord they kept the charge of their office (Ezekiel 44:15 and in many places). And if we consider the chain of high priests from the beginning of the Second Temple to the days of Antiochus Epiphanes, we find that all of them were of the sons of Zadok. But from the days of Antiochus this chain was no longer kept: Onias the Fourth, who was also called Menelaus, was not of the family of Zadok and rose to the high priesthood (see Hitzig, sec. 65)⚠3; and perhaps Yakim too, who is called Alcimus, who was appointed high priest after him, perhaps he too was not of the sons of Zadok; and after that the high priesthood passed to the family of the Hasmoneans, and they too were not of the sons of Zadok. Such a change, overturning an old-established statute and custom, could certainly be a cause of division of opinions and hearts. Therefore it is very possible that from the time the priests of the sons of Zadok were pushed out of the high priesthood, then too a sect began to grow that called itself Sadducees, after the name of the head of their father's house, Zadok.{v1_06_08:1} But all this will not shed light on their character, and we still do not know the cause of the matters of their opposition in the principles of the Jewish religion. For the main point of their opposition is that they opposed the teaching of the elders, which among us is called the Oral Torah; and since they were opposed to this Torah, it is self-evident that their grievance against the teaching of the elders was also the cause of their turning away from following them. And if we say in particular that they denied the resurrection of the dead, this is only because this principle too flows from the teaching of the elders. But this separation was a natural outcome of the change and transformation in opinions and in learning in the course of that time. Even though we know that the way of interpretation of the sages who carried the Oral Torah had not yet widened so much in that early time as in the later time, nonetheless we also know that already in ancient days the Torah began to be interpreted according to the ways of their midrash, which turn away from the plain sense of Scripture; and on the other side, various interpretations of the Torah based on scientific inquiry, in harmony with the wisdom of the Greeks, had already spread at that time. In the days of Ptolemy Philometor, the contemporary of Judah Maccabee, commentaries on the Torah in this manner had already been composed by the Jewish philosopher Aristobulus. And we will see below that the Jews in the Land of Israel too inclined at times to go in this way. And indeed from the conduct of the Alexandrian Jews and their ways it appears that this became a stumbling block for many, until they quickly turned away from keeping the Torah in practice, thinking that the statutes of the Torah are only like hints at intellectual matters, and to these they turned their gaze, but the practice of the statutes was light in their eyes and they were not careful to keep it. Philo the Jew of Alexandria already alluded to these in his book *On Abraham* (p. 401). Now if we consider the character of many of the men of the Torah in our nation, from of old and in all times, we find that division of opinion was mostly born when they saw that a new way in beliefs and opinions caused some light-minded people to turn aside from the Torah. If they found that some of those who sought wisdom went astray through wisdom, then wisdom in general became a sin in their eyes, and they utterly rejected all external wisdom to the very last extreme, and found fault with every new thing that had been revealed to sages who came of late. So it was of old, in general with respect to the sciences and in particular with respect to the interpretations of the Torah. And so it is very possible that the danger that could come to religion through their being drawn after Greek wisdom in understanding the Torah was what caused many not to go even a step beyond the meaning of the words as written in interpreting the Torah; and it is self-evident that whoever holds in this way to the words as written is likewise opposed to the midrash of the sages of the Oral Torah. Now what we are told in the aggadah, that the separation of the Sadducees took place in the time we are dealing with, will be correct. But the thick cloud spread over all matters of the Oral Torah in this period does not allow us to recognize the traces of the separation and the strong force with which the opposition of the Sadducees acted upon the opinions of the Pharisees⚠4; and only after we pass on further along the path of time and consider the course of this Torah and its development up to the end of the period we are dealing with, will a bright light shine for us, to recognize the character of the sects and to know how they acted upon one another.

Indeed, this division of hearts in beliefs and opinions is a sure sign that the study of the Torah was at that time a matter that occupied them. But soon days came when this study was held back. From the day that Antiochus Epiphanes ascended his royal throne, men already began to cry out "violence" against his rule. At the beginning of his reign Israel could still endure the pressure of the oppressor, and their strength to bear it did not fail. For a nation that has learned from its youth to bear a foreign yoke is not easily crushed to the dust so that it cannot rise; but as this oppressor did ever more mightily, the glory of Jacob grew poor in proportion, until this evil rule became a stone of stumbling to the Temple and to the Torah, and a trap and a snare to the inhabitant of Jerusalem [cf. Isaiah 8:14]. The evil scheme he devised against Israel found strong support within the weary nation. The lawless sons of the people lifted themselves up and went whoring after the laws and customs of the Greeks, and their aim was to turn the people of the Lord away from the Torah and to plant the degenerate shoots of a foreign vine [cf. Jeremiah 2:21] on the holy soil. The high priest who stood to serve in the Temple of the Lord when Antiochus came to the throne was called Onias, and he was faithful to God and to his people. His brother was Jeshua [Jason], a man of intrigues and of evil deeds, a traitor to his brother and to the ways of his fathers. This treacherous brother denounced his brother to the king and succeeded in his schemes, and by the power of the bribe he gave the king he became priest in his brother's place. His aim was to plant the laws of the Greeks on the soil of Israel. To reach this aim he contrived to obtain from the king, for the Jews of Antioch, the rights of citizens of the city in Jerusalem⚠5, for they had learned to act as the Greeks and their wanton ways; therefore he hoped through their help to reach his goal in Jerusalem. He also obtained permission from the king to set up and establish houses of games, to train the youths of Israel in the games of the Greeks. With these games he began, but their end was evil and bitter; for not many days passed before they already scorned the holy covenant and covered their nakedness from the reproach of the Greeks, until many forsook this sign of the covenant and did not circumcise their sons. True, this priest did not last long in his priesthood, for three years later he fell from his greatness. But the one who came after him was worse than he. The government removed him and installed by force a man who was not of the seed of the priests fit to be high priests; he was called Onias, and by his Greek name Menelaus. With his help and by his counsel all the abominations were done in Israel, as is told in the books of the Maccabees and in the books of Josephus ben Gorion the priest.{v1_06_09:1} Israel in its myriads fell plundered at the feet of the mad king. He robbed and despoiled the treasury of the House of the Lord, profaned the Temple, took away the daily offering and set the abomination that makes desolate (Daniel 11:31, 32) on the altar of the Lord. Keeping the Torah and the commandment became a capital crime, and "they that are wise among the people, who instruct many," fell by fire and flame, by captivity and by spoil (ibid. 33). But the fall of this false priest too came quickly, and the fruit of his deeds was returned upon him. Yet the one who came after him was no better than he, for he too walked in the ways of the Greeks, and his hand was with the government's decree to cut off religion and Torah; he shed much innocent blood, and many of the sages of the Torah and of the pious of the Most High fell slain on the high places of Israel. Yet with all the upheaval that Antiochus Epiphanes wrought in the land of Judah, his wish did not prosper in his hand to cut off the Torah, nor its study. Who would believe our report when we say that the persecutions with which Antiochus the wicked persecuted Israel did not have the power to weaken faith and the study of the Torah among many of the people? And yet so it was. As from of old pressure and compulsion have never had power to accomplish anything against the character of people of strong qualities, but rather the reverse (for the more their opponents and oppressors compel them and try to turn them by force from their opinion and their faith, the more they too add strength and might to hold fast to their opinion and their faith and to turn back all pressure and compulsion by their strength and the firmness of their spirit), so it was at the time when Antiochus rose up to turn Israel from their religion by compulsion and cruel pressure: strong groups bound themselves together and took courage to reach this goal. On the one hand the government pressed them, through the religious decree, to dissolve⚠6 the Torah and to remove the spirit of the Jewish religion from the land, and it was helped also by the lawless among the Jews, for many of the people, and also of the priests, followed it and tried to carry out its counsels by holding to the laws of the Greeks. The high priest called Yakim, and by his Greek name Alcimus, who rose in place of Menelaus, was the worst of all in strengthening the hands of transgressors, in breaking the Torah and in pursuing in wrath those who held to the religion. He made a great slaughter among the sages and the pious of the Most High, and slew all that were pleasant to the eye [Lamentations 2:4]. But against these groups stood righteous men, pious ones of the living God and sages of the Torah, and they took their stand like an iron pillar at the head of the people and kept the charge of religion, so that root and branch should not be cut off. These scorned their lives unto death on the field of slaughter to sanctify the honoured and awesome Name; and those took their lives in their hands to transgress the cruel decrees of the king and to hold to the Torah. They guarded it as the apple of the eye, and led the people with understanding and knowledge not to turn aside from the way of the Lord and the judgment of their God.

We have now passed in this chapter through a great chapter of the periods of time. It is great in the number of its days and years, and mighty too because of the many events that took place within it in respect of the political state of the Jews; yet the development of the Oral Torah took only small steps forward in it. And how very wonderful is the sight that in the midst of the din of the war that they now waged under the leadership of the Hasmoneans, to stand up for their lives and their religion, now, in the midst of this upheaval, the chain of this Torah grows longer, and for us too the traces of it grow and multiply, which allow us to recognize its nature, its course and its events more and more.
