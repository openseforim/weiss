Book One

General introductory inquiries

The first traces of an oral tradition; the period of the Judges up to Samuel; the period of the Kings up to the destruction of the First Temple; the period of the exile up to the return.

Chapter One

The first traces of an oral tradition.

A firm belief took root in the Jewish nation already in ancient times: that besides the Torah written in a book there were also various traditions as old as the written words. These traditions were not written in a book but went from mouth to mouth, and for this reason they were called by the special name "the Oral Torah."{v1_02_01:1} If we want to know on what foundations this belief was sunk, we will not seek to learn it from reports of a late time. For who will tell us whether people in that late time did not have a particular purpose in making the existence of this Torah known? Rather, we must reach the origin of the matter at its first source. In the history of peoples and lands, the narratives written for the purpose of telling the events of the times do not always suffice, because exaggerated matters have usually mixed into the narratives that come from the ancients: either they are described according to the views of the narrator, or they are wrapped in aggadic figures of speech, or the narrator shapes his story in various ways for some particular aim, so that he sometimes tells things in a manner far from reality, because he has an external goal in view in his narratives. Therefore, if we want to reach the truth, no traces will help us except the traces found on the various remains that survive from the time whose history we want to know: whether an inscription engraved on a tablet with an iron pen, or an ancient proverb, figures of speech, songs and legends that are clearly children of that time, and above all the statutes of law that were enacted for that people or land and have come down to us in writing. From these traces we draw, with much clarity, the knowledge needed to recognize the ancient time. They are like lamps that light up the darkness of antiquity, and they show us the laws and the opinions of the time and the way of the spirit that moved in it; for they give us the form of the time that gave birth to them as it truly was, without disguise and without a changed face. Now in the same way, and with the same experience, it will happen to us when we come to investigate and inquire into the history of the Oral Torah, to know the origin of the belief in it and its development through the ages. For the reports given to us about it by Talmudic writers, who tell us of the existence of traditional teachings from the beginning of the religion of Moses, still raise various doubts in us. Doubts have already been cast on them: whether they truly have a basis and support in an ancient tradition that truly survived in its own form and likeness from the time when the statutes that we have in Scripture were given, and went from mouth to mouth until it reached us; or perhaps all those reports have only the value of aggadah, or the sages at some time had a compelling reason to give all the teachings and expositions of the sages the value of tradition, in order to strike down the opponents of the Torah that they taught orally, and to stop the mouth of those who spoke arrogantly against their understanding of the laws of the written Torah. For do we not know that in every generation there stood opponents against them, who threw their interpretations and enactments behind their backs? Therefore it is right for us to look for traces of tradition in reliable remains from the ancient time. Those remains are the laws of the Torah that were written down; and they are likewise to be searched for in the other holy writings that we have, for in them too a great number of traces will be found of how Scripture was understood, and also of laws that were not written in the Torah. And such traces, since they speak in innocence and without an intention to testify, certainly deserve more belief than witnesses who intend to testify.

These doubts gain strength if we consider the religious condition of Israel in the first periods: from the day the Torah was given, through the days of their encampment in the wilderness, in the days of the Judges and after them in the days of the Kings, and also from the day of the exile from the land until their return. It cannot be denied that in all these periods the religious condition was not as it should have been according to the character of the religion that the Oral Torah gave it. And from all the sections of this period no word, great or small, has reached us in an honest writing that tells anything of this Torah and its existence. How can we think that so great a matter as the existence of a received Torah apart from the written one would not be mentioned or recalled by even one of the prophets and sages who lived during those days and generations? And in the days of Ezra, when they made a new covenant on the Torah, it says only that they "entered into a curse and into an oath, to walk in God's Torah, which was given by Moses" [Nehemiah 10:30], and only a few of the commandments written in the Torah are specified there. At that assembly they certainly should have mentioned, at least by a small hint, something of this Torah. Not few are the things told in our holy books about the conduct of their religious life in ancient years, and there is no trace in them of the conduct of religion according to the measure of this Torah. Apart from all this, are not all the reports in the books of the Oral Torah about the tradition from Sinai mostly of the character of aggadah, or else the speakers' own opinions, according to what came out to them, by their understanding, from the verses? And if we consider them carefully, do they not almost contradict one another? For how can we rely on what we are told, that the Torah was given with its laws, its fine points and its interpretations at Sinai (Sifra, Be-ḥukkotai, ch. 8), and that besides Scripture the Mishnah and the Gemara were also told to Moses at Sinai (Berakhot 5a), and on many aggadic midrashim like these, when we see that in one place one of the masters of the Talmud already found this difficult and asked: how is it possible that in the forty days that Moses was on Sinai he learned the whole Oral Torah, "whose measure is longer than the earth and wider than the sea" [Job 11:9]? To this he answered that in truth the Holy One, blessed be He, taught him only the general rules (Exodus Rabbah, ch. 41). Is this not a clear contradiction of all the sayings that we mentioned? And in another place they said: even what a diligent student will in the future innovate was given to Moses at Sinai (Yerushalmi Ḥagigah 1:8 and Megillah 4:1).⚠1 How then are we to understand here the words "given to Moses at Sinai"? As written and in their plain sense? Shall we truly think that the sages believed that the Holy One, blessed be He, handed to Moses everything that any rabbi or student would innovate until the end of all generations? Sound reason will not bear this; and how could it be called a new thing if it had already been given to Moses? Necessarily, the words "given to Moses at Sinai" are not to be understood exactly and in their plain sense. Rather, they meant by it that everything the sages of each generation innovated lay within the meaning of the Torah; or it is to be understood as agreement with the original intention. However it is understood, it is in any case not understood exactly. Now who will tell us that this phrase, where it is mentioned in other places, is not also to be understood in one of these senses? Since we have seen that they used this phrase loosely and as hyperbole, why should we not say that they kept to the same way in other places too? And if we wish to bring proof from the opposition of those who denied the tradition in an early time, and say that when the deniers, who are the Sadducees, first opposed it, the belief in a tradition from Sinai was necessarily already widespread among the Pharisees: apart from the fact that the proof is weak in itself, we must also pay attention to this, that we have never heard that in the first quarrels and disputes between the Pharisees and the Sadducees, which reached force and strength in the days of Simeon ben Shetaḥ, the Pharisees said to the Sadducees, "We rely on tradition for our interpretation and our Torah." And Josephus too,⚠2 when he tells of the opposition of the Sadducees against the Pharisees, has not one word in his language that hints that the Pharisees relied on a tradition from Sinai; he only said that the Sadducees opposed the teaching of the elders. And who can vouch that the matter is not the reverse: that because of the opposition of the Sadducees there was born, in the course of time, the belief in an ancient tradition going back to Moses at Sinai?

The opponents of the tradition will gain further strength when they show that this was the manner of the early sages: they sometimes attributed things to the most ancient of the ancients, not because they had a tradition, but from a conjecture or for another reason. How many halakhic matters they put in the mouth of Samuel the seer (Berakhot 31b), and in the mouth of David king of Israel (Shabbat 56a), and of Mephibosheth son of Saul (Berakhot 4a), and of Ira the Jairite (Eruvin 63a); and there is almost no end to all the things that were attributed to the ancients. In particular we find many statutes and enactments that they attributed to great men of renown in an early time. If we look into them, we see that in this they did not rely on a tradition from man to man but on conjectures, mostly subtle and far-fetched ones. The reasons for this habit lay mostly in the forgetting of the sources of things. For many things existed and lived among the people, and in the course of time their source and beginning were forgotten. Then someone came and thought, in their manner, to find a hint and support for them in the words of one of the ancients, in which, by his conjecture, such-and-such an enactment or statute was hinted. For the one who came after him the conjecture was already a clear matter, and in the course of time a received matter. Although in the proper place (below, chs. 7 and 10) these matters will be clarified and made plain to the full, it is right for us to consider one example here. They attributed decrees and enactments to King Solomon. If we ask where they got this, the answer is not that they received it from man to man; rather, it came out to them from the sense of the verses, according to the ways of their midrash, as it says: "he weighed, and sought out, and set in order [tikken] many proverbs" [Ecclesiastes 12:9] (Eruvin 21b; Yevamot 21a). They interpreted the word tikken as referring to enactments [takkanot], and so it came out to them that Solomon enacted enactments, and they attributed to him enactments whose source and beginning were already hidden from them. There are also things whose enactor himself hung his enactment on a great tree and attributed it to one of the ancients; and over many days the belief grew strong, and people believed that the thing truly belonged to that ancient. In this manner we must not charge the enactor with wrongdoing, for mostly he did not think, and it did not enter his heart, to mislead the generations to come; rather, they misled themselves. This is clearly seen in the book of Ecclesiastes. This book was hung on King Solomon; but from its language and its opinions, and also from the methods of inquiry in it, every thoughtful person will understand that Solomon was not its author. Rather, the true author put his words in Solomon's mouth, because this wise king was fit, by his wisdom, his character and his history, to be the man whom the author would choose to put his inquiries in his mouth;{v1_02_03:1} just as another author chose Job and his friends to carry his inquiries on their lips. But it never entered the heart of the true author to mislead us. Later readers, who took the words as written, misled themselves.

Yet it also cannot be denied that sometimes they did so willingly and with intention, attributing things to the ancients on purpose, so that their words would be sanctified and more readily heeded among the people. This is clearly seen in the prophecies included in the book "The vision of Isaiah."{v1_02_03:2} Who is the inquirer into the depth of a matter who will not admit that these prophecies, which speak of the state of the nation and of their hopes in Babylon, were composed together and also prophesied during the days of their exile in the land of Babylon. It is also clear that these prophecies were attributed to Isaiah already in an early time. Indeed Ben Sira in his book (chapter 48) attributes them to him. And perhaps this was done already in the first period, when they came to light. And if we ask what the cause was, first and foremost, for attributing these prophecies to a prophet of an early time, the words of Josephus in his *Antiquities* (book 2, ch. 1)⚠3 will tell us: he relates that the Jews showed Cyrus the book of prophecies in which Isaiah prophesied two hundred years earlier that Cyrus would be king over many peoples, and that the Lord chose him to restore the captivity of Judah and to build the Temple; and these words took hold of Cyrus and bore fruit (Ezra 1). Now we see from this that besides the purpose these prophecies had with respect to Israel, to strengthen their heart and make firm their hope, they had yet another purpose: to show Cyrus that he was the one chosen by the Lord to fulfil His holy will. For this reason the prophet does not speak even one word of the establishment of a new kingdom; whereas had Isaiah written these prophecies, he would surely have had no compelling reason to beware of speaking about the renewal of the kingdom. On the contrary, in the prophecies of Isaiah this point, the maintenance of the kingdom of the house of David, was the chief one. Therefore we learn from this for what reason these prophecies were attributed to Isaiah in those days; and once they had been hung on Isaiah, the matter stayed in the belief of the people and was handed down so from generation to generation. But in any case we see that they hung things on the ancients with intention and willingly, for some reason. In the Talmud too it is told that once they attributed a halakhah to R. Yose because they wanted that halakhah to be accepted; therefore they said it in the name of R. Yose, knowing that his words were heeded and welcome, so that it would be accepted (Eruvin 51a). Likewise it is told of R. Abbahu that he permitted a man to teach his daughter Greek because it is an ornament for her, and hung his permission on R. Yoḥanan his teacher, although R. Yoḥanan never said it; and one of the sages (Simeon bar Va) said of him: because he (R. Abbahu) wants to teach his daughters Greek, he hung the permission on R. Yoḥanan (Yerushalmi Pe'ah 1:1). And they explicitly permitted hanging [a saying] on a great man, relying on the verse that the sons of Jacob too said to Joseph, "Your father commanded" [Genesis 50:16], and we do not find that he commanded (Yevamot 65b). Of this kind also is what they said: that in the books of prophecy too there are things not true in themselves but written according to the opinion of the multitude, as though the prophets thought it permitted to alter the truth because of dispute (*Me'or Einayim*, ch. 6, from the Yerushalmi). It is also clear that some of the sages went with this permission beyond the limit and hung their words on others in order to win hearts for things that in themselves were perhaps not right; for otherwise they would not have warned about it with so exaggerated a warning, saying: "Whoever says a thing not in the name of the one who said it causes the Shekhinah to depart from Israel" (Tractate Kallah, at its end); and they said of one who says a thing in the name of the one who said it that he brings redemption to the world (Avot, and Megillah 15a). The exaggeration of such warnings is a faithful sign that this was in truth done in an improper way, and therefore they went to extremes in praising the one and blaming the other; for it lies in the nature of things that exaggerated praise of a thing and exaggerated blame of its opposite are a sign of the habit of that opposite. One more thing was strong in those ancient times and was a cause for attributing things to one who did not say them: certain things spread under the name of one man and were attributed by mistake to another who also bore that name. So it is seen in the prophecies in the book of Zechariah from chapter 9 to the end of the book: from their language and their content, and from the events at which the prophecies aim, it is seen eye to eye that they are not Zechariah's, who prophesied in the days of Darius; rather they are earlier prophecies that another prophet called Zechariah prophesied, and it is very likely that this was Zechariah son of Jeberechiah (Herzfeld I, 289) mentioned in Isaiah (5:2).⚠4 Now if we look at these phenomena, how can doubts not arise in us also with regard to the Oral Torah? For we could think in the same way that its whole essence is not truly founded on tradition, but that it too is only attributed to a tradition from Sinai for one of the reasons we have mentioned. And now it is clear to us that the report of the masters of the Talmud is of no use to us for proving from it the truth of the tradition, when the reports themselves are not clear and contradict one another, and the very report that is to be our witness itself needs proof whether it is truly founded on tradition.

But the root of the concept of the tradition that we call the Oral Torah lies in the statutes of the Torah that are written in our hands. For if we consider them, we find that they went hand in hand with various traditions, so that a great many of these statutes cannot be conceived by us unless we assume foundations of existing [practice]⚠5 that were never written, but on which the Torah of the Lord relied. Such traditions, which lived in the spirit of the people and in its mouth, were the root of the concept of oral statutes and teachings; and in this way the belief in the existence of an absolute Oral Torah was born. Let us go on to clarify the matter with fuller explanation. From the very beginning, before Moses set statutes and teachings before the children of Israel, there were known statutes and judgments living in the mouth of all who were joined to the house of Jacob. According to the narrative of the Torah, Abraham and his seed were distinguished in keeping statutes founded on principles of justice and righteousness (Genesis 18:19; 27:5).⚠6 These statutes, whose keeping is attributed to the house of Jacob, were good practices to make straight the way of life, and they were in use among the members of this family. Likewise there took root in them practices and customs to which they had become accustomed in Egypt; for it is impossible that two peoples should dwell together for several hundred years and not act strongly upon one another in their way of life, in their customs and in their livelihoods.⚠7 Now all these practices and customs that they had from their home and from the source of their origin, as well as those to which they had become accustomed in Egypt, good and bad alike, were among the foundations on which the giver of the divine Torah built his statutes and judgments. To the good ones he gave permanence and survival, yet with limitations and extensions and with various conditions fitting his aim; and against the bad ones he gave statutes to keep them away and to be a guard, so that they would not be ensnared by them. The case of the statutes of the Torah is like the case of the statutes enacted of old by various lawgivers, for whom the habit of keeping uprightness among people was the line and the measure, and this was the source of every positive statute and law (jedes positiven Rechtes); and so it was too with the statutes of the Torah. These foundations are not found in the Scriptures; but a great number of the commandments and statutes in the Torah are found that cannot be understood unless we assume the existence of some ancient statute or custom that lived in the people and was known and famous, on which the giver of the Torah relied without mentioning it explicitly when he gave the statute, and of which there is no record in writing in a book. And this statute and custom, which were neither mentioned nor written, are what help us understand the written statute. We see in this that an unwritten received matter spreads light on the written matter and explains it; and here before us is the concept of the Oral Torah, for it too has this function: to explain, or to limit, or to extend the written statutes. Therefore we have found in this the first trace of the Oral Torah.

A few examples that we will pass before us will confirm and establish this great matter. Concerning the Hebrew slave the statute was given: "If you buy a Hebrew servant, six years he shall serve — if he is married, then his wife shall go out with him — if his master gives him a wife — the wife and her children shall be her master's" [Exodus 21:2–4]. Now if we consider this statute, we find that it presupposes statutes and customs fixed in the people, famous and known to all, so that there was no need to spell them out in writing within the statute of law. The first: we must presuppose a permission that an Israelite has to be sold as a slave. This permission was not stated and written in a statute of its own, for the statute speaks only in the language of condition, "if you buy"; necessarily it relied on an ancient statute concerning the permission, which was not written but was a thing known from custom. Likewise the statute is presupposed that a married slave brings his wife with him into the master's domain; this statute too was not written, and necessarily it was known of old and there was no need to spell it out, so widely was it known. The statute "if his master gives him a wife" is also written in the language of condition, and surely relied on another statute, that his master may give him a wife; for how is it possible to make a condition and say: if the master does such-and-such, his law shall be such-and-such, unless we establish that he has permission to do such-and-such? So too we see in the statute of the sale of the maidservant, which is also given in the language of condition: "And if a man sells his daughter to be a maidservant" [Exodus 21:7]; necessarily we must presuppose a statute that a father has permission to sell his daughter, and this statute was not written. This is seen still more in the statute that speaks of the designation of the maidservant to her master or to his son. For this statute we must presuppose a statute about the permission the master has to designate his maidservant for himself or for his son; but it does not do so, relying on what was known and on the custom that was in the people, as we see with the patriarchs Abraham and Jacob. Without doubt this custom remained among them, and the divine lawgiver relied on it.{v1_02_06:1} It follows explicitly from the words "And if he designates her for his son" [Exodus 21:9] that he surely relied on an ancient and known custom that a father would give his son a maidservant to treat as a wife, in order to guard him from lewdness and from the adulteries of a harlot. This custom still remains and persists among the Arabs (Niebuhr, on the Arabs, p. 74).⚠8 And concerning one who seduces a virgin girl and lies with her, and her father utterly refuses to give her to him, it was set as a statute: "he shall pay money according to the dowry of virgins" [Exodus 22:16]. It does not mention what and how much the dowry of virgins is. Who can cast doubt that the divine lawgiver relied on what was famous and known? The statute of divorcing a wife was in use of old; and the Torah added to the statute in use that if the divorced woman had become another man's, her first husband may not take her again. This clause is the new statute. For besides the fact that this comes out clearly from the composition of the clauses of this statute, there is a further proof from the term "bill of divorcement" [sefer keritut], which is obscure: it is not explained what this document is and what its nature is. No lawgiver would speak so obscurely unless it was recognized and known in the nation, its matter and its nature. Some of the sages of the Mishnah already sensed this and thought that even before the giving of the Torah the statute of divorcing a wife was in use. Indeed R. Joshua ben Ḥananiah expounded in this direction the words of the Torah: "And Jethro, Moses' father-in-law, took Zipporah, Moses' wife, after he had sent her away" [Exodus 18:2], and said: after she had been released with a bill of divorce. So he holds that the custom of divorce preceded the statute of the Torah (*Mekhilta*, Yitro, ch. 1; and see there in *Middot Soferim* and *Me'ir Ayin*). We also find that the statute of the Torah relied on a statute and law in use among the nations in those days, and since they were accustomed to it, it was set down in the Torah as a tradition known to all. This is in the statute the Torah gave concerning one who kills his fellow by guile, that he too shall be killed with none to save him, where it says: "you shall take him from My altar, that he may die" [Exodus 21:14]. In this statute lies the famous statute that the altar saves those liable to death; but it was not explained and is not found written anywhere in the Torah. We see that the Torah relied on a statute in use among the nations. Nor can it be denied that this statute had some validity even in a later time, as is seen from the affair of Joab (2 Kings 2).⚠9 We have seen from all this that there were matters of tradition on which several statutes in the Torah relied, and only through them do we attain the sense of those statutes. And since we have seen clear traces of old traditions that are not explicit in the Torah, yet explain the text, so that without them the words of the text would be obscure, this itself is also the first trace of an oral tradition.

Explanations and interpretations of commandments and statutes were also given already, or at the time they were given. The Torah tells us that in the plains of Moab Moses began to expound the Torah to all Israel (Deuteronomy 1:5), and that Moses himself was compelled to ask the explanation of some of the statutes, as in the case of the wood-gatherer and of the blasphemer, and in the law of making the Passover offering for those unclean by a corpse, and the like. And in the law of the outer sin offering Aaron explained by the rule of a fortiori, and it was good in the eyes of Moses (Leviticus 10:19, 20). From these one may judge that the rest of the statutes too were explained so where they needed explanation (*Moreh Nevukhei ha-Zeman*, gate 13). But we should consider whether this may not be a proof of the opposite. For these explanations the giver of the Torah wrote in a book. Also what was explained in Deuteronomy, that is, what is said that Moses began to expound the Torah, this too was written in a book. Therefore one could draw from this the conclusion that whatever was not explained in writing and not expounded in Deuteronomy was not explained at all, since we see that whatever needed explanation was explained and written in the book. But we should know that the explanations written, whether in Deuteronomy or in the other books, are of a particular kind. The nature of these explanations is to complete some legal statement in order to teach us a new matter, which would in no way become clear to us from the statement of the main statute. It is self-evident that it cannot be completed without adding words; for the aim is not to explain the sense of the words of the statement, but its aim is to clarify the matter through a new condition, or a limitation, or an extension. Such is the explanation of the commandment "that they make them fringes [tzitzit] on the corners of their garments" [Numbers 15:38]: here the word tzitzit is doubtful in its meaning and the word "corners" in its number. For tzitzit is a general name for all kinds of locks at the edges of a thing, and one also says "a lock [tzitzit] of the head" [Ezekiel 8:3]. Therefore it was necessary to explain in Deuteronomy (22:12): "You shall make yourself twisted cords upon the four corners of your covering." This teaches us a new thing, that these fringes shall be twisted threads called gedilim, and it teaches us further that the phrase "the corners of their garments" refers to the number of four corners. The statute concerning the clean animal is recorded only in general terms, saying: "These are the beasts which you may eat ... whatever parts the hoof ... that you may eat" (Leviticus 11); and in Deuteronomy this statute is limited, and the particulars of the general rule are explained: "the ox, the sheep and the goat, the hart, the gazelle and the roebuck, the wild goat, the pygarg, the wild ox and the chamois" [Deuteronomy 14:4–5]. These shall be the example for recognizing the cleanness of an animal for eating. So too we find in all the commandments repeated in Deuteronomy that conditions, limitations or extensions were added to them. We have shown that the nature of the explanations in Deuteronomy is that the explanation teaches us a new thing that would not be understood from the statement being explained, and such an explanation must necessarily be written in a book. But the explanation that wishes to teach only what is meant by the written phrase, and whose aim is only to explain its sense, was not written in the book and did not need to be written; for once the sense of the phrase and the word is known, the explanation is contained in the word and the phrase itself. Of this kind is the explanation of "And you shall take you on the first day" [Leviticus 23:40], that the taking be by hand. If this is true, then we shall have the measure which of the explanations can be fitted to the original intention and which not; and we shall say that any explanation that adds a condition, a limitation or an extension, and is not contained in the sense of the word or the phrase, cannot be how the statute was explained from the very first; for if so, it would surely have been handed down to us in writing.

After this truth and these words, we see that a great number of the statutes exist whose keeping cannot be conceived unless a tradition preceded them. But there are also conjectural proofs that the sages, the advocates of the truth of the tradition, have brought us, and we must be very deliberate in judgment. For among them are some that may perhaps suffice for one who relies on the opinion of the ancients because it is ancient, but will not suffice for the inquirer who examines every opinion of the ancients, even though it is ancient. One of these conjectural proofs is from what is said in the Torah: "then you shall kill of your herd and of your flock ... as I have commanded you" (Deuteronomy 12:20);⚠10 this teaches that Moses was commanded orally concerning the gullet and the windpipe (Ḥullin 28a), and that there is a command concerning slaughter, how one is to slaughter, and these are the five laws of slaughter; and they thought that "as I have commanded you" alludes to this. Now surely this proof would be valid if we assume that Moses was commanded orally about this, or if we say that "as I have commanded you" may allude to that command. But even in that way the proof would not be compelling, since "as I have commanded you" may refer to another command stated elsewhere, and the proof is only a possible one. But now that we want to show that Moses was commanded orally concerning the manner of slaughter, and we ask: from where do we have it that he was commanded concerning the details of the matter? What shall we answer? If the answer is: the Torah informed us of this by saying "as I have commanded you," which alludes to some command it has already given, and we do not find anywhere that it gave a command, so that necessarily its intention was an oral command — then we will say that it is not so; rather "as I have commanded you" refers to the principle and not to the manner of slaughter. That is, after it said "Notwithstanding you may kill and eat flesh in all your gates, after all the desire of your soul" (ibid. 12:15), the statement "then you shall kill — as I have commanded you" refers to this, and alludes to the command that was given in the statement "And he shall kill the bullock" [Leviticus 1:5], to make it fit for the eating of the altar, the priests and the owners. He means to say: just as I commanded you to slaughter for the holy offerings, so you shall slaughter also an unconsecrated animal when your soul desires to eat flesh. And the plain sense of the verse is surely so. Then we have nothing left but to say that we rely on the midrash of the sages, who expounded "as I have commanded you" as referring to the manner of slaughter. Then we return to the first question and ask for proof of this midrash itself! Especially since the sages themselves showed us that it is possible to explain "as I have commanded you" as referring to another command, concerning the principle of slaughter, and to say: "just as holy offerings require slaughter, so unconsecrated animals require slaughter" (*Sifre*, Re'eh, §75). Therefore, in the end, this proof is below conjecture. There are many such proofs, and they can be drawn from several statutes in the Torah of which only general rules were recorded in writing while their details were not recorded, and we can conjecture that the details were known orally from of old. Such is the keeping of the Sabbath from doing work, about which only the general rule is stated, "you shall not do any work." We have in the Torah only four details explicit in the text, and they are: plowing, harvesting (Exodus 34:21), sowing, pruning (Leviticus 25:4). But it is clear that other kinds of work were also forbidden, since we see that on a festival it permits work for preparing food, and this proves that on the Sabbath this too was forbidden. Therefore surely, already at the time this statute was given, it was known what the details of the kinds of work were that it permitted on a festival and that are forbidden on the Sabbath. And we have a hint of this in the affair of the man who gathered sticks on the Sabbath day, whom those who found him gathering sticks brought to Moses and Aaron and to all the congregation (Numbers 15:32, 33). It is clear from this that those who found him knew that the act the gatherer did was an act forbidden on the Sabbath because it is work; and surely he did not know this⚠11 from a written statute, but they knew what was understood under "work" in their times.

In the likeness of such conjectural proofs another will grow for us from the contradictions between several verses in the Torah. For we find verses that contradict one another. How is it possible that in a book of statutes that came from one source two contradictory statutes should be found, and that they should have actual validity at any time, without a decision between them to reconcile them? Therefore we are compelled to establish that from the day such verses were written in a book, they were reconciled in some way so that there would be no contradiction between them. And even if it is not in our power to give proof, or even a hint, of the nature of the decision in an early time, what harm is that to us? For in any case we have seen the necessity of the decision, whatever it may be; and this is enough for our purpose now, to prove the existence of explanations that were not written in a book. True, those who deny the unity of the Torah and its statutes, and think that the Torah in its completeness, as it lies before us, was not written by one hand and at one time — and moreover think, in their error, that this is the meaning of the Talmud that said "it was given scroll by scroll" — these will tell us and say that the proof from the contradictions in the Torah is valid only by our assumption of the unity of the Torah, from which we set out; but by their view, what can such a proof do, since the assumption itself needs proof? Yet those drawn after this mistaken view should also consider that all these different scrolls — as they say — were, by their own view too, joined together at some time and became one. Surely they will admit that they were joined by one shepherd and by agreement, and necessarily, by their system, this compiler was the last lawgiver. Therefore we will return and ask of this lawgiver what we asked at first: how is it possible that a book of statutes that came from the hand of one, whether the first lawgiver or the last collector, that such a book of statutes should contain within it different statutes that contradict one another, and that they should have actual validity at any time without a decision between them to reconcile them? True, according to what one of the ancients (R. Ishmael) thought concerning such contradictions, saying that if such a case comes before us then a third verse decides between them, one might think that every decision was written in a book. But necessarily this was never his view, and we cannot believe of any of the sages of the Talmud that he would establish that the divine lawgiver would give two statutes that contradict one another and leave them without decision. Yet we see in the Torah several contradictions that have no decision in the text; necessarily they had a decision from of old. And even if the manner of the decision was hidden from the later generations, the Torah came to help also this lack in our knowledge, in that it sometimes decided by means of a third verse, to teach us that this way should be our line and measure: if the tradition forsakes us and we do not know how the contradiction was decided, we shall do so by our own knowledge and reasoning, so that we do not hold one verse to be an error, but devise a middle way to decide (commentary of Rabad on Sifra). This view is not a new view; rather the sages of the Talmud already upheld it, who said (Yerushalmi Ḥagigah 1:1) of two verses that contradict one another: "Put [the decision] between them," that is, we devise a middle way and decide. And we shall not heed the words of the one who says (*Korban Aharon* on Sifra) that we may decide only when and where the Torah itself decides by means of a third verse, "and let us fear in our souls that the hand of our intellect should not touch deciding between two verses that contradict one another, but let us wait until a third verse comes, and it will decide." Who will consent and who will listen to these words, which have no light? Why should we fear in our souls to use our intellect? Such fear is not founded on the enlightenment of the soul but on narrowness of spirit. The God who gave us this soul — did He lay on it to sit alone and silent and to trample under its feet the desire to inquire to the very end, or did He give it to us to guide us in the way of understanding in all our knowledge and deeds? And if we consider the practice of the sages of the Talmud in this and their ways, we find that they never did so; rather they decided between contradictory verses by their own knowledge and reasoning, without restraint.{v1_02_09:1} For if we abandon this way, we easily risk falling into the trap of error and mistake. For if we always wait until the third verse comes and decides, what shall we do if we wait in vain? And how shall we deal with the contradictions for which the decision of a third verse is lacking to us? And mostly no third verse comes to decide! Shall we say that one of them is an error, and let go of the one and hold on to the other? Whom shall we hold and whom let go? Shall we choose between the two according to our opinion and the inclination of our mind? That too is a decision, and then it is better for us to decide in a way that upholds both. And even if it is possible that we do not attain the truth as we wish, in any case our inquiry is acceptable and our intention acceptable, for through our inquiry we strive to agree with the original intention. And perhaps by reasoning we will find the ancient decision that has already been hidden from us. But whether we find it or not, we are permitted to decide, and it is also our duty to decide.

From all these considerations we can hold it as clear that from the very first, that is, from the time when the statutes and teachings of God were written in a book, various traditions were known by which a number of statutes in the Torah were understood; and we will say even more: that they were the pillars on which a number of statutes were established. But these traditions were not written in a book. We have likewise shown that from that day the statutes were interpreted; the interpretations meant to make us understand the sense of the written word or phrase were not written, but the interpretations that add a condition, a limitation or an extension, and that do not lie in the sense of the word or phrase, were written; this is the character of the interpretations in Deuteronomy, and a few of them also in the other books. By this measure we can recognize the age of a given interpretation and know whether it is to be held as received or as new. We have also seen that the form of some statutes required some interpretation already at the time they were given, since they cannot be fulfilled unless we assume that their obscurities and generalities were made clear by the announcement or the knowledge of their details. Every seeker of the plain truth will admit that these judgments are just. But if in spite of all this we find many of the inquirers who deny everything, the blame does not lie with them but with the advocates of tradition and its lovers; for they kept no bound and went beyond measure. The words of the Talmud that ascribe the Mishnah and the Gemara and the sum of all the halakhot, the interpretations and the fine points to Moses at Sinai: all this they took literally. They did not consider whether this literal sense can stand against the contradictions that fall in it, and against the possibility that all the ancients said so for some particular purpose and reason. They were further strengthened in their view when they saw that some of the Geonim too understood the words as written, so that they said that even the discussions of the Amoraim were not of their own minds but were received (Epistle of R. Sherira Gaon), and that all the thirteen rules by which the Torah is expounded are received from Sinai (Rashi in the name of the Geonim, and Maimonides). And they did not consider the matter: that the Geonim too did not say so from a particular tradition, but on the strength of the proofs they brought from the words of the Talmud as written and in their plain sense. And if we look at the words of those who put questions before Rav Sherira Gaon on this point, we find that they too were entangled in doubts on this matter; necessarily, then, there was no public and known tradition. From this to extending tradition also to the halakhot of the later authorities, who derived consequences from the Talmud, is only one step. For since the whole body of the halakhot (and perhaps also of the aggadot) is tradition from Sinai, necessarily every consequence that comes out of it is in some measure tradition and counts as from Sinai. And it has already been said of R. Eliezer, author of the book *Roqeaḥ*, that he received the basis of the [laws of] penance that he composed from his teacher, and his teacher from his teacher, back to a halakhah [given] to Moses at Sinai (Responsa of Maharam, Prague edition, 113). But the enlightened will understand that such exaggeration did not help to strengthen the belief in the Oral Torah, but brought on it the opposition of every enlightened man, who will not be content with proofs of the truth of a thing when the proof itself still needs proof. Not so the proofs that we have laid down: they come from the content of the statutes themselves and from the time they were written in a book, and therefore they compel us to admit the existence of matters of tradition also from the side of inquiry and criticism. Yet how great is the number of the interpretations that survived from the first time? From the side of historical inquiry it is not in our power to decide, for we lack clear reports from the ancient times. But this we know: that in different times the statutes were interpreted in interpretations different from one another, as we shall see in the following chapters, and that they always fulfilled the statutes in practice according to their understanding of them; and that all of these rested on tradition is a thing that experience denies. But for the purpose that we set ourselves in this chapter it is enough that we have seen that the existence of traditions and oral matters in the early time has been confirmed for us, and that we have found the root and foundation for the spread of the belief in the Oral Torah.
