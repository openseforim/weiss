v1_02_01:1) R. Naḥman Krochmal, in *Moreh Nevukhei ha-Zeman*, gate 13, gave the reason for this name, and he means it in two ways. First, because the core of these matters was handed to Moses at Sinai orally. Second, because during all those generations the sages of the Torah were not permitted to set down anything of the oral matters in writing. But it seems that this name was coined at a late time. The first to mention it was Hillel (Shabbat 31a), and there too it seems that later writers put it in his mouth. One should also remark on the phrase she-be-al peh, which is strange by the rules of the language, for the shin is not needed and spoils the sense. But the basis of this phrase was only their midrash, which expounded "for after the tenor [al pi] of these words I have made a covenant" [Exodus 34:27]: "oral matters you are not permitted to say in writing." What underlies this midrash is that the words al pi were expounded as referring to tradition; therefore, when they say "the Torah that is she-be-al peh," they mean the Torah hinted at in the verse by al pi, that is, which is "by mouth." From this it is clearly proved that this name cannot be earlier than that midrash.

v1_02_03:2) With proofs that cannot be moved from their place, almost all recent commentators, and also R. N. K. mentioned above, demonstrate that the second part of this book, from chapter 40 to 66, is in no way Isaiah's. I have added a few proofs to theirs, and I list most of them here briefly. (a) The prophet speaks of the exile as of a thing present in his time (42:22; 52:2), and of the loss of the kingdom as of a thing that is past (42:22; 64:9–10).⚠13 The whole book consists of consolations about the future redemption. Nowhere do we find that Isaiah prophesies about a Babylonian exile in the future, and his words in chapter 39 are obscure and cannot be explained as referring to the exile. (b) In the whole second part, Babylon and the Chaldeans are one; according to the historians this was not so until the time of Nebuchadnezzar. And Isaiah, when he mentions the Chaldeans (23:13), calls them "a people that was not; Assyria founded it for the desert creatures." (c) The prophet knows the political state of affairs in the lands of the East at the time of the exile; he calls Cyrus by name and describes his power (ch. 45), and hints at his plans against Egypt (45:14). Where, then, have we found that any prophet speaks in this way, with the limit of a set time, exactly, and with the details of deeds and events, and with hints at the names of people and places, when his prophecy is for a time far off and for conditions far from the present condition of the prophet? (d) The prophet proclaims that he came on the mission of the Lord "to bring good tidings to the humble," etc. (61), and says that some of his prophecies have already been fulfilled: "Behold, the former things are come to pass, and new things do I declare," etc. (42:9), and more explicitly in ch. 48. How could it enter our mind to put such words in Isaiah's mouth? (e) Even more than all this, his picture of the Messiah proves it. For Isaiah has no other picture than the establishment of the kingdom in strength, with the king Messiah at its head, "a shoot out of the stock of Jesse" [Isaiah 11:1]; whereas with this prophet we do not find even one word about the establishment of the kingdom, and there is no mention of a king from the root of David. I have already hinted in the text at the reason for this. Added to all these proofs is the language of these prophecies and its character. Its expressions and figures of speech differ greatly from the expressions and figures in the prophecies of Isaiah. We find in them words that show that the power of the Aramaic language had already grown strong over the author; and there are also ideas in them aimed against the opinions of the Persians, which were unknown to Isaiah. But since this inquiry is not especially within the aim of our work, I touch on it only slightly, and I refer the reader to what was written on this matter by R. Naḥman Krochmal in *Moreh Nevukhei ha-Zeman*, ch. 11, and by Gesenius, Hitzig, Knobel, Ewald, Umbreit, Herzfeld and others, some in their commentaries and introductions and some in their inquiries and studies on the books of the Prophets.
