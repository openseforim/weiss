1) R. Naḥman Krochmal, in Moreh Nevukhei ha-Zeman, gate 13, gave the reason for this name, and he means it in two ways. First, because the core of these matters was handed to Moses at Sinai orally. Second, because during all those generations the sages of the Torah were not permitted to set down anything of the oral matters in writing. But it seems that this name was coined at a late time. The first to mention it was Hillel (Shabbat 31a), and there too it seems that later writers put it in his mouth. One should also remark on the phrase she-be-al peh, which is strange by the rules of the language, for the shin is not needed and spoils the sense. But the basis of this phrase was only their midrash, which expounded "for after the tenor [al pi] of these words I have made a covenant" [Exodus 34:27]: "oral matters you are not permitted to say in writing." What underlies this midrash is that the words al pi were expounded as referring to tradition; therefore, when they say "the Torah that is she-be-al peh," they mean the Torah hinted at in the verse by al pi, that is, which is "by mouth." From this it is clearly proved that this name cannot be earlier than that midrash.

2) See what R. N. Krochmal wrote in Moreh Nevukhei ha-Zeman, gate 11, who narrowed the date of the book of Ecclesiastes to the end of the days of the Persian kings; and see also the introductions to Holy Scripture by Christian scholars. And in the monthly journal of Mr. Graetz, of the year 1889,⚠3 Mr. Graetz dates it later, to the reign of Herod. But all of them answer as one that it is not Solomon's.

3) With proofs that cannot be moved from their place, almost all recent commentators, and also R. N. K. mentioned above, demonstrate that the second part of this book, from chapter 40 to 66, is in no way Isaiah's. I have added a few proofs to theirs, and I list most of them here briefly. (a) The prophet speaks of the exile as of a thing present in his time (42:22; 52:2), and of the loss of the kingdom as of a thing that is past (42:22; 64:9–10).⚠4 The whole book consists of consolations about the future redemption. Nowhere do we find that Isaiah prophesies about a Babylonian exile in the future, and his words in chapter 39 are obscure and cannot be explained as referring to the exile. (b) In the whole second part, Babylon and the Chaldeans are one; according to the historians this was not so until the time of Nebuchadnezzar. And Isaiah, when he mentions the Chaldeans (23:13), calls them "a people that was not; Assyria founded it for the desert creatures." (c) The prophet knows the political state of affairs in the lands of the East at the time of the exile; he calls Cyrus by name and describes his power (ch. 45), and hints at his plans against Egypt (45:14). Where, then, have we found that any prophet speaks in this way, with the limit of a set time, exactly, and with the details of deeds and events, and with hints at the names of people and places, when his prophecy is for a time far off and for conditions far from the present condition of the prophet?
