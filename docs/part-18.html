The ways of midrash and halakhah of Hillel and Shammai. Akavya ben Mahalalel.

דרכי המדרש וההלכה להלל ושמאי. עקביא בן מהללאל.

3

From the event that befell Hillel with the elders of Betera it follows for us that the study of the Torah was in a very bad state at that time. For we saw that by reason and by the ways of midrash Hillel's proofs were conclusive and compelling from the sense of the words of the written Torah. Nevertheless the Benei Betera refused to listen to his proofs, and their minds were not at rest until he told them that he had received it so from his teachers. Experience teaches us that reliance on what is received without examination and criticism, and clinging to the utterance of the ancients even when compelling proofs point to the opposite, is a sign of slackness in study and of the lack of any scientific effort. But for this reason we shall not say that the cause why the halakhah was hidden from the Benei Betera was none other than that they inclined to the way of the Sadducees, to interpret the verses according to the strict depth of their plain sense (R. Menaḥem ha-Meiri, in the introduction to Avot). Rather, they were Pharisees to the utmost degree, and kept the teaching of the elders with precision and great care; but because they clung with all their strength to the teaching of the elders, it was enough for them to accept a halakhah if its foundation was a tradition from the elders, and they paid no attention to whether one could bring a proof for the tradition from the midrash of Scripture or not. But the widening of the midrash of Scripture, which came forth from the house of study of Shemaiah and Avtalyon and reached perfection under the hands of Hillel, was caused precisely by the complaint of the Sadducees, who said to the Pharisees that their whole teaching, which they had received from the elders, was the work of human hands. Therefore they strove to show them that the teaching of the elders has a foundation and a root in the written Torah, and in this they succeeded with the help of the middot and the ways of their midrash; and perhaps they hoped that the Sadducees too would now consider with understanding and their minds would be at rest. To reach this aim, Hillel's first way was to train the students and the learners to set all their attention on the study of the Torah, to understand and to know how most of the halakhot are founded in the Torah, and to remove the perverse lips of the Sadducees from their teaching. And the later generations rightly compared the work of Hillel in his generation to the work of Ezra in his generation (Sukkah 20a). Yet Hillel's charge was harder than Ezra's charge. For as a rule it is harder to repair the breaches of a great building and to heal its breaks than to found the building and set it up from the foundation to its upper stories.

מן המאורע שאירע להלל עם זקני בתירה, יצא לנו שדרישת התורה היתה בעת ההיא מאד ברע. כי ראינו שלפי הסברה ולפי דרכי המדרש היו ראיות הלל נצחות ומוכרחות ממובן דברי התורה הכתובה. ובכל זאת נמנעו בני בתירה לשמוע אל ראיותיו ולא נחה דעתם עד אשר אמר להם שכך מקובל מרבותיו. והניסיון יורנו שמאז הסמיכה על המקובל מבלי בחינה ובקרת והדבקות במוצא פי הקדמונים אף אם ראיות מכריחות מורות ההיפוך, זה הוא אות על ההתרשלות בדרישה ועל חסרון כל השתדלות מדעית. אבל בעבור זה לא נאמר שהסבה אשר נעלמה הלכה מבני בתירה איננה אחרת רק מפני שנטו אל דרך הצדוקים לפרש את הכתובים על עומק פשוטם (הרב המאירי בפתיחה לאבות), אמנם היו פרושים עד הקצה האחרון, ושמרו בדקדוק ובזהירות רבה תורת הזקנים, אך יען כי דבקו בכל עז בתורת הזקנים על כן היה די להם לקבל הלכה אם יסודה קבלה מן הזקנים ולא השגיחו על הדבר אם אפשר להביא ראיה לקבלה ממדרש הכתוב ואם לא. אבל הרחבת מדרש המקרא אשר יצאה מבית מדרשם של שמעיה ואבטליון והשיגה שלמות תחת ידי הלל, היא היא שנסבה מפני תרעומת הצדוקים אשר אמרו לפרושים שכל תורתם אשר קבלו מן הזקנים מעשה ידי אדם היא, על כן השתדלו להראות להם כי תורת הזקנים יש לה יסוד ושורש בתורה הכתובה וזה הצליח להם בעזרת המדות ודרכי מדרשם, ואולי קוו כי גם הצדוקים יתבוננו עתה בינה ותנוח דעתם. למען השיג התכלית הזאת היתה ראשית דרכו של הלל להרגיל את התלמידים ואת הלומדים להשים כל מעיניהם בדרישת התורה, להבין ולדעת איך רוב ההלכות נוסדו בתורה ולהסיר לזות שפתותיהם של צדוקים מעל תורתם, ובצדק השוו דורות האחרונים את מעשה הלל בדורו למעשה עזרא בדורו (סוכה כ'.). ואמנם פקידת הלל היתה קשה מפקידת עזרא. כי לרוב יקשה יותר לחזק את בדקי בנין גדול ולרפא את שבריו מייסוד הבנין ומכינתו מן המסד עד עליותיו.

4

To reach his aim he used the rules by which the Torah is expounded. Such rules had already served the ancients as aids in their midrashim. These rules were called middot, and they are the foundations of midrash. Hillel gathered them into the number seven and gave them technical names.1 These middot are: kal va-ḥomer, gezerah shavah, binyan av, kelal u-ferat, two verses, ka-yotze bo be-makom aḥer, davar ha-lamed me-inyano.2 One of the later sages, R. Ishmael, set the middot at thirteen. This number is usual among the masters of the Talmud (Sanhedrin 86a). Of the source from which the middot came, nothing is told us in the Talmud, nor in the other ancient books of the Oral Torah. But in the responsa of the Geonim they ascribed the middot to a halakhah [given] to Moses at Sinai (Sefer ha-Keritut, Batei Middot, Bayit 1:9), and after the Geonim many of the commentators followed this view3 without considering how it is possible that the masters of the Talmud, and before them the masters of the Mishnah, held this view and did not hint at it even with a slight hint in any place. If they found a place to publish that black straps are a halakhah [given] to Moses at Sinai, how did they not find a place in the great and wide sea of the Talmud to publish likewise this great principle on which the whole teaching of the sages in the Talmud rests? It is true that one cannot deny their antiquity with regard to their essence, since most of them rest on reasoning, and reasoning is the offspring of the intellect; therefore, from the time the Torah was written, the intellect was surely the instrument that would help us reach its nature. But with regard to their being technical rules, gathered into a particular number and under particular names, the matter is clear: Hillel or his teachers investigated and inquired into the midrashim of the Torah that had been expounded up to their time, considered the manners of the expositions and their nature, and found that these expositions were founded on such-and-such a course of reasoning; and to make it easier for the expounders they collected and arranged these foundations and gave them fitting names, and their number came out to seven. In the course of time, in proportion as exposition and inquiry increased, various rules also increased, and each rule by itself widened until it passed its first boundary; and because of this we find that as exposition went further in time, the nature of the midrashim changed more and more. This passing of the boundary is found in the middah of gezerah shavah in a remarkable manner. This middah in its essence is a principle laid down in the logic of language, for the likeness of the words is the faithful witness to the likeness of the matters, and this is the nature of gezerah shavah in essence. But this middah needs much caution, for there is no middah by which we can become entangled in errors as much as by gezerah shavah, because similar words are many in the Torah (Yerushalmi Pesaḥim 6:1). For this reason the ancients decreed that a man may not expound a gezerah shavah on his own, but it must be received [by tradition] from the great court of the generation (Kin'at ha-Emet by R. Ḥ. Ḥagiz, root 2), because what is agreed in the great court comes from the opinions of the great men of the generation, and on them we can rely that they surely did not pass their bounds. But the sages of the later generations loosened the strap and expounded by gezerah shavah in a way that goes beyond the bounds of the rule of logic, and from this were born strange midrashim that the plain sense of Scripture cannot bear. And what happened to gezerah shavah happened also to all the other middot, and especially to the exposition of ribbui and mi'ut [inclusion and exclusion].4 Besides the middot that were counted, they had further sound rules that served them as aids in the interpretation of the Torah. These rules are scattered in the books of the Oral Torah, and some of them are hinted at in the midrashim of the ancients.5 One is the middah of hekkesh [analogy]. By it Hillel expounded before the elders of Betera (Pesaḥim 66a, and the Yerushalmi), and Hillel did not count it among his middot because it is included in the middah of davar ha-lamed me-inyano (Sefer Keritut, Batei Middot, Bayit 2). And this hekkesh that Hillel expounded is a rational exposition. For he proves that the Passover offering overrides the Sabbath by comparing the Passover offering to the daily offering: just as the Sabbath does not stand in the way of the daily offering, since it is a public offering, so the Passover offering, which is a public offering, should by right override the Sabbath. There are many such analogies in the midrashim that are rational and agree with the plain sense of Scripture.6 But in the course of time matters were compared to one another that have no likeness in their nature and are sometimes opposites, and there is no inner reason to equate them, only that they are written next to each other in the Torah.7 From the middah of hekkesh came forth the middah of semukhim [juxtaposition], of which the ancients knew nothing.8 Such was also the lot of other middot: not only were they moved from their first, rational nature, but they were fruitful and multiplied and gave birth to new middot that the ancients had not imagined. The middah that was moved furthest from its nature is the middah of ribbui and mi'ut. This middah is in essence rational. For it is clear that if there is found in the Torah a superfluity of some statement or of a word that is understood by itself, this superfluity was not written by chance and without a particular intention, especially in statements of statutes and judgments; and if such a superfluity is found, every interpreter will try to understand the particular intention that the lawgiver intended by that superfluity. To make the matter clear to us, let us consider as an example one statute in the Torah, the statute of the forgotten oath, of which it is said: “Or if a soul swear, pronouncing with his lips to do evil or to do good, whatsoever it be that a man shall pronounce with an oath, and it be hid from him ... and he shall bring his guilt offering unto the Lord” (Leviticus 5:4). Now the words “to do evil or to do good” cannot be understood in any other way than either that he swore to do evil or to do good to others, or that he swore to do evil or to do good to himself (Talmud and Sifra). But as for what Scripture added, saying “whatsoever it be that a man shall pronounce with his lips,” ⚠1 it is clear that by this addition it meant to include also things in which there is neither harm nor benefit, and that it spoke of harm and benefit only as the usual case. This interpretation is the plain sense of Scripture and is founded on the middah of ribbui. Likewise the middah of mi'ut is a natural feature of the style of speech in general, and of the style of statutes and judgments in particular, for the lawgiver speaks with precision, and if he specifies particular details he wants to exclude other details that were not specified. And are there not words in the language whose essential meaning is nothing other than the exclusion of something from things, such as the words rak, akh and the like? Therefore it is clear that ribbui and mi'ut in their root are natural features of language. But in the course of time inclusions and exclusions were made “that we know not” ⚠2 [cf. Deuteronomy 32:17],9 and new derivatives came forth from them, which were also counted as middot and were an aid to the midrash of the Torah. These derivatives are: “There is no inclusion after inclusion except to exclude; there is no exclusion after exclusion except to include” (Sifra Vayikra, parashah 10; Menaḥot 74a). But the derivatives are no longer rational like their fathers in their root. In short, if we pay attention to the rules and the ways of exposition that the later sages had, we find that they flowed from the middot of the earlier ones and are nothing but widenings; only the widenings went so far from the nature of the root that their root is no longer recognizable.

אשר גם הן נחשבו p. 158 כמדות, והיו עזר למדרש התורה. אלה התולדות הן אין רבוי אחר רבוי אלא למעט, אין מעוט אחר מיעוט אלא לרבות (ת"כ ויקרא פרשה י' מנחות ע"ד.) אבל התולדות אין עוד מושכלות כאבותיהן, בשרשם. סוף דבר אם נתן לב לכללים ולדרכי הדרישה אשר היו לאחרונים נמצא שנבעו מן המדות של הראשונים ואינם אלא התרחבות רק שההרחבות התרחקו כל כך מתכונת השורש עד ששרשן לא ניכר עוד.

The sages who came after the Talmud considered the expositions of the masters of the Talmud and found how strange their interpretations were and how far they were from the plain sense of Scripture, and they found no defense except by saying that they did not intend to interpret Scripture, but that all the strange midrashim that depart from the plain sense are asmakhtot [mere supports].10 But whoever considers the language of the ancient sages will find that it is not so. For in all the halakhic midrashim we find that they used the expressions “What does Scripture teach us,” “Scripture comes to teach you,” “Therefore it is said,” “From where do you say,” “Why is it said,” and many expressions like these; and how can it enter the mind that they intended only to give a support for a matter? Likewise we see that in most of their expositions the sages disagreed, each one expounding the verse in a different way, or even opposing one another from one extreme to the other; and how is it possible that the main point of the midrash was said only as an asmakhta? What use is there in whether one supports the matter on the verse in this way or in another? Likewise we find in many places one technical phrase that is usual among the sages of the Mishnah: when they bring a halakhah together with the midrash that points to it, their way is to say, “From here they said such-and-such.” The intention of this is plain: from the midrash of that verse flows the halakhah such-and-such. So the midrash is the source of the halakhah, and if the verse had not been expounded in this way the halakhah would not have been said. Thus we see that the midrash is not an asmakhta but the interpretation of Scripture. Indeed, where the sages themselves thought that the midrash was only an asmakhta, they did not refrain from saying so explicitly, and they said: “From here the sages found support for this matter from the Torah,” or they would say: “A hint for such-and-such a matter from the Torah.” Therefore it is clear that the midrash of Scripture was for them the interpretation of Scripture, and every expounder thought that this was truly the intention of the text. And let it not be a wonder in our eyes that most of the midrashim seem to us like strange interpretations. For it is not right for us to judge their ways of midrash and the plain sense of Scripture in their times by the measure we have for the nature of the plain sense of Scripture. For the recognition of the plain sense changes according to the state of knowledge of the language in which the interpreted book was written, according to the recognition of the matters and rules needed to understand the book, and above all according to the level of enlightenment in the time and place of its writing. Because of this, the plain interpretation changes in proportion to the change of that state. If we consider the ways of interpretation of the commentators up to this day, we find a great difference in the understanding of the plain sense of Scripture, and as a rule what was called the plain sense in one generation was strange in the eyes of the generation that came after it. Even among the sages of one generation the opinions were not always the same in recognizing the plain sense, for the nature of the interpreter's soul also gives its nature to the thing interpreted. So it was among the Tannaim: R. Ishmael did not accept the plain sense of R. Akiva, nor the reverse, and so among the other Tannaim. From this were born disputes in the interpretation of the verses, until they sometimes said to one another, “How long will you heap up [words] or twist the text?” And one of the later sages (Rava) said: Although in the whole Torah a verse does not depart from its plain sense, nevertheless we find that a gezerah shavah comes and takes the verse out of its plain sense entirely (Yevamot 24a). According to this view, the whole Torah that is expounded does not depart from its plain sense by means of midrash, and if one of the middot takes the verse out of its plain sense, then the matter is noted specially because of the novelty in it. Already at the end of the days of the sages of the Talmud they had a slight feeling that the midrashim of the sages, as they had developed in time, were no longer truly the plain sense of Scripture. Of Rav Kahana (the second) it is told that he said of himself that already when he was eighteen years old he was versed in the whole Talmud, but he did not know that a verse does not depart from its plain sense, until his companion Mar son of Ravina informed him that all the midrashim of the sages are the plain sense of Scripture (Shabbat 63a). There are indeed midrashim that are not complete, and of these the sages said: “It is a mere asmakhta.” Likewise they sometimes gave a hint for a matter of halakhah in Scripture (Kiddushin 80b, and in many places). Likewise they sometimes expounded a verse in the manner of rhetoric and hung a moral matter on it (Ketubbot 111b), or they used the language of Scripture in conversation between a man and his fellow, but with an intention other than its plain sense (Ḥullin 6a; see also there 133a), or by way of a parable on a matter of halakhah (Arakhin 8b). In all these they said explicitly that the plain sense of the verse is to be understood with another intention. Yet in all these places the plain sense of the verse is also brought beside the homiletic interpretation; and if we consider this interpretation to which they give the name “the plain sense of the verse,” we find that it does not differ from the whole mass of midrashim that exist, and it did not occur to the plain-sense interpreters after the Talmud that one of them would follow, in interpreting the plain sense, the interpretations that were called by them the plain sense of Scripture. This is what I said: that the midrash of the verses according to their ways and their middot was for them the plain sense of Scripture.

The term midrash is found in Scripture, as in "the midrash of the prophet Iddo" (2 Chronicles 13:22), "the midrash of the book of the Kings" (ibid. 24). The act of expounding is designated by the verb darash, as in "thus I expounded and thus my colleagues expounded" (Sanhedrin 9:9). ⚠3 The masters of midrash are called darshanim (Pesaḥim 70a); this was when they were marked by their particular quality of being learned in the exposition of Scripture, but their title as sages in general was zekenim, "elders" (Sifre Shelaḥ §115, and in many places in the Talmud). Hillel and Shammai in particular were called by the title zaken, as Shammai the Elder, Hillel the Elder, not because they were advanced in years, nor to distinguish them from other sages who were called by their names, but because the title zaken was a proper designation for sages. As to the standing of the midrash and its force: we have already shown that the later sages ascribed to tradition both the old midrashim and the newly made ones, because they believed that these agreed with the first intention of the Giver of the Torah. If there was a dispute over the exposition of the verses, the great court decided (Sanhedrin 9:3). ⚠4 This decision was made in this way: the members of the great court stood for a count and decided by the majority, and this was necessarily according to their own judgment (ibid., Bavli 88b). Therefore they ascribed the halakhic consequences that come out of the midrash to the sage who expounded it. And one who decided between two verses that contradict each other and made a compromise between them was called by the later sages "a builder of the Torah" (Yerushalmi Yoma 3:5). The causes that prompt exposition are the fine points of Scripture, which were called dikdukei soferim, "fine points of the Scribes," with respect to the subject, or dikdukei Torah, "fine points of the Torah," with respect to the object. ⚠5 The kinds of midrash, however, are various. There are midrashim that explain the intent of the words of the verse, as "and you shall speak of them" [Deuteronomy 6:7], which is aimed at the commandment of the recitation of the Shema, which is the acceptance of the kingdom of Heaven; "and you shall take for yourselves on the first day" [Leviticus 23:40], that the taking be by hand; and many like these. This way of midrash is the most ancient, for it comes to explain the meaning of the words according to their plain sense. But for all that, not all the midrashim that were expounded in this way are ancient, for some of them were expounded for some particular purpose, as we shall see in the example we brought. For surely the most ancient of the ancients did not expound "and you shall speak of them" as referring to the recitation of the Shema, since the verse speaks of the study of Torah. Without doubt the first Men of the Great Assembly already instituted the commandment of reciting the Shema in order to root in the nation the belief in the unity [of God] and the acceptance of the commandments, and the masters of midrash came at some time and expounded the verse for this purpose. There are midrashim that come from reasoning upon the verses, as: "but if the man has no kinsman" [Numbers 5:8] teaches about one who robs a proselyte (Bava Kamma 109a; Sifre Naso §4), for they found no man of whom it could rightly be said that he has no kinsman except the proselyte. Within this class are the midrashim expounded by the rules, and especially by the kal va-ḥomer and the two contradictory verses. And there are midrashim from hints in the verses, as "when she does any one of all the commandments of the Lord" [Leviticus 4:27]: when she does it in its entirety and not part of it (Shabbat 92a); they took the hint from the word aḥat, "one."11 All the midrashim fall under these three kinds of midrash, and all the interpretations made in these ways were called midrash,12 that is, midrash Torah (Kiddushin 49b), and it was also called "Torah" alone, without the term midrash (ibid., and Yerushalmi Pesaḥim 6:1 and Mo'ed Katan 3:7). The aim of the midrash was to find the sources of the halakhot and to show how they came out of the written Torah, as we saw with Hillel, who went up to the Land of Israel and expounded, and agreed, and received the halakhah (Yerushalmi Pesaḥim 6:1). And we find that a master of halakhot, from whom the source of some halakhah was hidden, praised ⚠6 the master of midrash who expounded and agreed with his tradition (Sifra Vayikra ch. 4 and in many places; Sifre Beha'alotekha §79; Tosefta Zevaḥim ch. 1). And just as they made the midrash agree with the existing halakhah, so too they created new halakhot by a midrash that they expounded in the Torah. The effort to find the sources of the halakhot in Scripture was the cause that they widened the ways of midrash and went to excess with the rules of midrash in great exaggeration. Once the ways of midrash had been widened so much, it could not escape producing new things, for they could not set limits to their words: if they used the widened ways of midrash to found the old halakhot on them, they were necessarily bound to rely on those ways when new consequences came out of them.

הכנוי מדרש, נמצא במקרא כמו מדרש עדו הנביא (דבה"י ב' י"ג כ"ב) מדרש ספר המלכים (שם כ"ד). ופעולת הדרישה מכנים בפעל דרש. כמו כך דרשתי וכך דרשו חברי (סנהדרין פ"ט מ"ט). בעלי המדרש נקראים דרשנים (פסחים ע.) הלל הזקן, לא מהיותם באים בימים וגם לא להבדילם מחכמים אחרים אשר נקראו בשמותם אמנם יען כי תואר זקן היה כנוי עצמי לחכמים. ממשפט המדרש וכחו, כבר הראנו לדעת שבין המדרשים הישנים ובין המחודשים תלו האחרונים הרוב, וזה בהכרח לפי שקול דעתם (שם בבלי פ"ח:) ועל כן יחסו התולדות ההלכותיות היוצאות מן המדרש אל החכם הדורש. ומי שהכריע בין שני כתובים המכחישים זה את זה ועשה פשרה ביניהם היה נקרא אצל האחרונים בונה התורה (ירושלמי יומא פ"ג ה"ה). הסבות המניעות לדרוש הן דקדוקי המקרא אשר נקראו דקדוקי סופרים בבחינת הנושא או דקדוקי תורה בבחינת הנשוא. ואמנם סוגי המדרש, שונים הם. יש מדרשים יפרשו כונת דבורי הכתוב, כמו ודברת בם שהוא מכוון על מצות קריאת שמע שהיא קבלת מלכות שמים. ולקחתם לכם ביום הראשון, שתהיה הלקיחה ביד ורבים כאלה. דרך המדרש הזה הוא היותר קדום כי הוא בא לפרש משמעות הדבורים עפ"י פשוטם, אבל בעבור זה אין כל המדרשים שנדרשו על הדרך הזה קדומים, כי יש מהם נדרשו לאיזה כונה מיוחדת. כאשר נראה במשל שהבאנו. כי ודאי שקדמוני הקדמונים לא דרשו ודברת בם על קריאת שמע כי הכתוב מדבר מתלמוד תורה. ובלי ספק כבר אנשי הכנסת הגדולה הראשונים תקנו מצות ק"ש כדי להשריש באומה אמונת האחדות וקבלת המצות, ובאו בעלי המדרש, באיזה זמן ודרשו את המקרא לכונה זו. ויש מדרשים היוצאים מהסברה בכתובים. כמו: ואם אין לאיש גואל מלמד על הגוזל את The midrash of the Torah widened greatly through the efforts of Hillel the Elder from the time he reached the office of nasi, and he founded houses for the Torah. Already while still in Babylonia, and before he sat before Shemaiah and Avtalyon, he was engaged in the midrash of the Torah. His teachers, who were great darshanim, further strengthened the longing within him. Besides this, he saw that the time demanded it. For on the one hand there was a necessity to prove that the teaching of the elders was not a new teaching that they had invented out of their own hearts, as the Sadducees thought, but was founded in Scripture; and on the other hand he was compelled to strive to widen the midrash of the Torah, to the degree that even some of the Pharisees had neglected it, as we saw from the matter of the Benei Bathyra and from Hillel's rebuke of them for their lack of exposition. All these signs, together with the fact that Hillel arranged, counted and established the rules by which the Torah is expounded, are clear marks that at the time of his rise to the office of nasi he set his heart to strengthen the midrash of the Torah, and from then on the midrash too began to widen. Yet we have from him, and also from Shammai, only a few midrashim that are called by their names. But this is no proof that others did not exist, for without doubt many midrashim that we have in our hands, on which the names of their authors are not called, should be ascribed to them, and especially to Hillel. Of Hillel seven midrashim are mentioned, and of Shammai four. The character of Hillel's midrashim shows us that by reasoning he went down to the depth of the Torah's intent, and he explained according to his own judgment13 even where he was compelled to contradict the meaning of the words as written;14 and if at times he explained the words as written, he did so only when reasoning too required it,15 or when he practiced the performance of one of the commandments by doing it according to the words as written.16 But this is no proof of the character of his way in the midrash of the Torah; it is only a proof that he was scrupulous in the commandments. But in the midrashim of Shammai we find that all are expounded according to the words as written, even where reasoning does not require it.17 From this it follows for us that the new road in the midrash of the Torah that Shemaiah and Avtalyon had paved was the way that Hillel chose for himself to attain the widening of the midrash of the Torah; but for Shammai, by his way, the widening of the midrash was an impossible thing. This difference of opinions, which already in the days of the Benei Bathyra was like the letting out of water [Proverbs 17:14] among the Pharisees, broke out further and became like a spring that grows stronger; and when the disciples of Hillel and Shammai multiplied, the dispute increased, and the Torah became like two Torahs. To the same degree that their opinions differed in midrash, they differed in their halakhot. The halakhot mentioned in their own names are few. Of Hillel we have eight halakhot, and it is not told that any of the sages of his generation disputed any of them. Of Shammai we have five halakhot, and on these too we have found no one disputing. Besides these, five halakhot are mentioned on which there was a dispute between Shammai and Hillel. From the halakhot on which they disputed, and still more from the disputes of their disciples, we see that the way of Hillel in his learning was different from the way of Shammai. And although their words are only few, nevertheless we can judge their ways in learning from the fruit of their teaching, which grew and prospered with their disciples; especially since among the halakhot mentioned in the name of the House of Shammai and the House of Hillel there is no small number that date from the time of Hillel and Shammai themselves. Hillel held in one hand the tradition, and in the other the contemplation of this tradition. He inquired and sought to know the causes that were the reasons for the transmitted halakhot, and how, and when, and where those causes would or would not apply.18 So he passed over the ancient halakhot in inquiry, and sifted and refined them; and in every new matter that came to his hand, if he saw that by reasoning it was proper to depart from the ancient halakhah, he did not hesitate and departed. But Shammai did not depart from what was received in any matter; on the contrary, he strove to liken every new matter on which he judged to the ancient halakhah that resembled it, even when the case at hand was not truly like the precedent. Hillel's effort was to distinguish by reasoning between things that appear alike and are not alike; Shammai's effort was to bring near by force things that are far apart, and to liken things that are not alike except to the eye, by his casuistry and sharpness of intellect (Yevamot 13b). Accordingly, both engaged in the criticism of the halakhah, except that with Hillel criticism was the measure for keeping or annulling the halakhah, while with Shammai the halakhah was the measure for criticism and inquiry. If we want to know what the later sages thought of Shammai's character and how they judged him, we must look at their judgment of the man whom the later sages likened to Shammai. This man was R. Eliezer ben Hyrcanus, of whom they said that he was a Shammuti [a follower of Shammai]. They gave him this name not because he was a disciple of Shammai, for he was not, but because he walked in Shammai's footsteps. And from all we know of this R. Eliezer we understand that his way in learning was as we have described Shammai's way. R. Eliezer too did not depart a hair's breadth from what was received, and said nothing that he had not heard from his teachers; and on every new case about which he had not heard what the earlier sages said, he said, "I have not heard," and did not decide by his own opinion (Sukkah 27b). He also treated his rules alike and made no distinctions (Yevamot 108b), and he did not permit his disciples to give a reason for a halakhah except to uphold the ancient halakhah (Nega'im 11:6, and Sifra Tazria ch. 6 and ch. 15); and further matters of this kind are told of him, all of which agree that his ways in learning were along the path we have described. Therefore from the thing compared we can judge the thing it is compared to. Now clinging to what is received, without distinguishing between like and unlike, leads mostly to new stringencies, and the reverse to the reverse. This was borne out in Hillel and Shammai and their disciples: Shammai and his House mostly came out with stringencies, and Hillel and his House the reverse. The most trustworthy proofs, which bear witness how firmly Shammai held to tradition without departing from what was received even in a matter where the case at hand did not resemble the precedent laid down in the ancient halakhah, are revealed and seen from several deeds told of Shammai the Elder. They said of him that he did not want to feed his little son on the Day of Atonement, and the sages decreed against him and made him feed him with his own hand ⚠7 (Tosefta Yoma ch. 4).19 This was surely because he relied on an ancient halakhah that children are trained in the commandments, and he did not distinguish between one child and another, or between one matter and another. Perhaps it was because of this event that they fixed the halakhah: "Children are not made to fast on the Day of Atonement, but they are trained a year or two before" (Yoma 82a). And another time he showed his strictness in the training of children: his daughter-in-law gave birth to a son on the festival of Sukkot, and he broke away the plaster of the roof and covered it with sukkah branches over the bed for the sake of the child (Sukkah 28a). Such deeds need no explanation, but anyone who has eyes will see how much Shammai refused to depart from the very substance of what was received, even where there was no room for the reason of the received thing. The same is seen from his halakhot. Regarding the impurity of the tent, Shammai held that a quarter[-kav of bones] from a single bone defiles (Eduyot 1:6); here he relied on the halakhah that a quarter causes impurity and did not depart from it in any case, and decided that this quarter applies in all cases, even from a single bone. And so with his saying, "For all women their time suffices" (Niddah 1:1): surely there was a received halakhah that a woman is not presumed impure as a menstruant until she sees [blood], and this is indeed the law of the Torah; therefore he did not depart from it, even though reasoning suggests one should fear that, as she is now found impure, so she had already been impure before this. In this case we see that by his way a leniency came out for him.

Not so did Hillel. He sifted and refined the ancient halakhah, and distinguished by rational reasoning between one act and another and between one time and another; he examined the reason for the matter, and according to his examination he gave it validity or annulled it. So it appears from his halakhot, and still more from the halakhot that were said and taught in the name of the House of Hillel, as will be made clear. This way of criticism runs also through the enactments that he made. Of his enactments only two have been preserved for us that are ascribed to him with certainty. The first is this: when he saw that the people refrained from lending to one another and transgressed what is written in the Torah, "Beware that there be not a base thought in your heart" etc. [Deuteronomy 15:9], he instituted the prosbul (Shevi'it 10:3). The aim of the prosbul is that the Sabbatical year should not cancel the loan, and by this he uprooted the commandment of the release of debts written in the Torah. The text of the prosbul is: "I hand over to you, So-and-so and So-and-so, the judges in such-and-such a place, that any debt that I have, I may collect it whenever I wish"; and the judges sign, or the witnesses (ibid. 10:4).20 From this enactment it appears that his whole aim was to go deep into the intent of the Torah and to reach the end of its purpose. For he recognized that the aim of the statutes of the Torah is to strengthen the moral law, and he saw that the statute of the release of debts came into conflict with another statute, namely, to lend to the poor man in his time of need, and through this the virtue of morality and kindness suffered; and we cannot uphold the one unless we break the other. But according to the demands of his time the one was more needed than the other; therefore Hillel did not hesitate to uproot the one in order to give standing to the one more needed. Another enactment is mentioned in his name, concerning one who sells a house among the houses of walled cities: by the law of the Torah it may be redeemed until a full year has passed, and if the last day of the twelfth month arrived and it was not redeemed, it became the buyer's for good. They used a trick: the buyer would hide on the day the twelve months ended, so that the seller could not redeem it and it would become his for good. Against this Hillel enacted that [the seller] might throw his money into the chamber, and break [the door] and enter, and the buyer would take his money (Arakhin 9:4). Of the rest of his enactments we have not been told. One may also ascribe to Hillel and the men of his time what was enacted: that they prepare for the High Priest on the Day of Atonement another priest in his place, lest some disqualification befall him and he be unable to perform the service (Yoma 1:1). This statute was enacted because of an event that happened when Mattathias ben Theophilus ⚠8 was High Priest: a nocturnal emission befell him on the night of the Day of Atonement, and Joseph ben Ellem served in his place; and because of this event it became a statute that every year they would prepare another priest in his place (Yerushalmi Yoma ch. 1, and Tosefta ch. 1; see Graetz vol. 3, p. 30?). ⚠9 Now this priest held his office in the time of the presidency of Hillel the Elder (around the year 3756 of Creation), and without doubt the enactment came from him and from his court. There is also no doubt that it was then that they enacted that the priest should stay awake all night and expound the Torah, or that others expound before him, so that he not sleep; and they further enacted that he not eat much on the eve of the Day of Atonement, to guard against a nocturnal emission. For from what happened to Mattathias it appears that until that time it was not the custom that the priest stay awake all night (Yoma 1:4 and 1:6). In all the enactments that Hillel made we do not find that Shammai opposed him, for Shammai too did not shut his eyes to the needs of the time, and he said so explicitly. At that time the tillers of the soil treated the Sabbatical year lightly: not only did they plow their fields in the Sabbatical year, but they did the plowing in the best possible way. This was evil in the eyes of Shammai, and he said of such a field that had been improved in the Sabbatical year: "If the hour were free, I would decree that it not be sown" (Tosefta Shevi'it ch. 3). And what was there in that time, of which Shammai said that it was not fit for decreeing a decree? It seems that the reason was this. It is known how heavy the yoke of taxes was on Israel in the days of Herod, for he took all their labor. And to gain the gain of extortion, his eyes and heart were always on the labor of the people, lest the spring from which he drew his extortion be stopped. Therefore he strove to remove every statute and law that kept the people from working the land — which was their chief occupation. To be sure, he feared to break the statute of the Sabbatical year because of the zeal of the people and the sages; but without doubt there was a royal decree that at least all work for the needs of the year after the Sabbatical year be done in the Sabbatical year. Therefore Shammai's words are aimed at this: that the hour is not free to decree this decree. This matter, that the government forced them to plow in the Sabbatical year, happened at various times, and the sages of the generation always permitted it.21

In all these enactments the hand of Shammai was firm with him; and of the decree he made concerning the impurity of hands it is said explicitly that both of them agreed on it (Shabbat 14b). And without doubt they dealt with each other in peace and brotherhood, although they differed in their opinions. Yet one incident is told of them from which it appears that Shammai sometimes came against Hillel with his hardness of spirit and wanted to establish his opinion by force. Once a question was asked: is [produce] that one gathers for the wine-press made susceptible [to impurity] or not? On this Shammai said: it is made susceptible; and Hillel said: it is not made susceptible. Hillel said to Shammai: Why do they gather grapes in purity, but do not harvest olives in purity? He said to him: If you provoke me, I will decree impurity on the olive harvest too. In this incident we see before our eyes Hillel in his calmness of spirit and Shammai in his strictness: Hillel weighs his opinion on the scales of reason, and Shammai comes with a strong hand and with strictness. And on that day strictness overcame forbearance, for on that day Hillel was bowed before Shammai (Shabbat 17a).22 But for the honor of the love of truth of our forebears we will think that it was not because of Shammai's strong hand that Hillel yielded and his opinion was set aside. Rather, without doubt they acted according to the line of the law and according to the halakhah: they stood for a count, and in that assembly those who agreed with Shammai's opinion were the majority; and this is the reason why Hillel bowed his head on that day before Shammai. More than Shammai's strictness, the zeal of Shammai's disciples caused Hillel grief of soul. His great humility and the love of peace that was a crown to his head, these worked against him. For there are people who do not know how to value the worth of such virtues and qualities in others, and they think that a man who deals with them in humility and in love of peace does not do so because he is truly humble and a lover of peace, but because he knows his own shortcomings and knows the weakness of his soul. So it was with the disciples of Shammai: when they saw that Hillel was gentle and meek and set aside his opinion before the opinion of others, they did not ascribe this to his humility and his love of peace, but thought that Hillel's forbearance came from his knowledge of the weakness of his soul and from lack of power to maintain his system against the system of his opponents. For this reason they sometimes banded together against him, and when they saw his wish and effort to appease them, they said in their hearts: We have defeated him! Once Hillel brought his burnt-offering to the Temple court on a festival and laid his hands on it. The disciples of Shammai banded together against him.... and he said to them: It is a female, and I brought it as a peace-offering; and they went away (Betzah 20b; ⚠10 Yerushalmi Hagigah 2:3; Tosefta there, ch. 2). Here he changed the truth for the sake of peace, for in truth it was not a female and he did not bring it as a peace-offering. But they, seeing that he appeased them and almost submitted before them, blessed themselves in their hearts, thinking that Hillel recognized that the learning of Shammai was too strong for him, and that he had set aside his opinion before theirs in the dispute over the laying on of hands. They thought that their hand had prevailed over Hillel, until they sought to fix the halakhah according to their words; and their thought had almost gone out into action, had there not been there one of Shammai's disciples who was not hasty of mind like his companions. He recognized that what Hillel did, he did only out of his humility and his love of peace. This man was Bava ben Buti; he knew that the halakhah is according to Hillel, and he was zealous with the zeal of truth and fixed the halakhah according to Hillel.

Apart from the quality of humility, in which Hillel went to the extreme, his way in his other qualities and deeds was the way of the mean. Therefore he did not love piety that goes beyond the bound of the mean (Avot de-Rabbi Natan ch. 12). Indeed, in all his deeds he took great care not to depart from the custom of the people, and he praises this quality in a fine image, saying: "Do not appear naked and do not appear clothed, do not appear standing and do not appear sitting, do not appear laughing and do not appear weeping, as it is said: a time to laugh" [Ecclesiastes 3:4] (Tosefta Berakhot ch. 2). By this he warns people to conduct themselves at all times according to the custom of the people with whom one sits and in their company (Tractate Derekh Eretz Zuta ch. 5). And it is very likely that what Hillel said elsewhere (Avot ch. 2), "Do not separate yourself from the community," means that one should not deviate from the custom of the people among whom one lives and with whom one conducts oneself. This saying has nothing to do with their saying "one who separates from the ways of the community," which is aimed at one who separates from the ways of the community in matters of faith and religion. Now if we look at all the qualities that were like a crown of glory to his soul, it is no wonder that the heart of all Israel was with him. His grace too stood him in good stead, so that Herod was at peace with him, until not even a slight hint is found that he stirred up strife with him. He too guarded himself from defying this wicked man. Yet one deed that Herod did was without doubt displeasing to Hillel too. From the days when they came up from the exile, they had in their hands a book of genealogies. Herod commanded that this book of lineage be burned.23 This matter was evil in Hillel's eyes, for the lineage of Hillel's own family, which was traced back to David king of Israel, was burned with it. (And perhaps at that time Hillel taught (Kiddushin 75a) ⚠11 "Ten genealogical classes came up from Babylonia"; and perhaps this mishnah is a copy from the burned book of lineage.) This evil deed was the cause of a new enactment. Formerly in Israel, when the book of lineage was in the Temple, that book gave testimony for every single family, and all inquiry and investigation was superfluous; but from the time Herod destroyed it, inquiry took the place of the testimony from the book. Therefore from that time it also became the rule to seat a court behind the house of the veil, where they examined the lineages of the priesthood.24 This court was made up of priests, for they knew who belonged by descent to their tribe, and it was called: the court of priests. And without doubt they judged other matters too, especially those touching the laws of the priests and their rules.

(מס' ד"א זוטא פ"ה), וקרוב הדבר מאד כי מה שאמר הלל במקום אחר (אבות פ"ב) אל תפרוש מן הצבור כונתו על זה שלא היה נוטה ממנהג בני אדם אשר הוא חי בקרבם ומתנהג עמם. ואין לזה המאמר ענין עם מאמרם „הפורש מדרכי הצבור" שהוא מכוון על הפורש מדרכי הצבור בעניני האמונה והדת. ועתה אם נסתכל בכל המדות אשר היו כעטרת תפארת לנפשו לא יפלא אשר היה לב כל ישראל אחריו, ואף הן עמדו לו כי הירודום השלים עמו עד כי לא נמצא אף רמז קל כי היה עורר אתו מדנים. גם הוא השמר לנפשו מלמרות פני הרשע הזה. אמנם מעשה אחד אשר עשה הירודום היה בלי ספק למורת רוח גם להלל. הנה מן הימים אשר עלו מהגולה היה בידם ספר יוחסין. ספר היחש הזה צוה הירודום לשרפו23. וזה הדבר היה רע בעיני הלל כי גם יחוס משפחתו של הלל אשר התיחש עד דוד מלך ישראל נשרף עמו. (ואולי בעת ההוא שנה הלל (קידושין ע"ה.) עשרה יוחסין עלו מבבל, ואולי המשנה הזאת הוא העתק מספר היחש הנשרף) המעשה הרע הזה היה סבה לתקנה חדשה. הן לפנים בישראל בהיות ספר היחש במקדש היה הספר ההוא נותן תעודה על כל משפחה ומשפחה, וכל דרישה וחקירה אך למותר, אבל מעת שהאבידו הירודום היתה הדרישה במקום התעודה על פי הספר, ועל כן גם מהעת ההיא היה לחק להושיב בית דין אחורי בית לפרכת אשר שם בדקו יחסי כהנה24. והבית דין הזה היה מן הכהנים כי הם ידעו מי הם המתיחסים לשבטם ונקרא: בית דין של כהנים. ובלי ספק היו דנים גם על דברים אחרים וביחוד בנוגע במשפטי הכהנים ודיניהם.

11

Concerning Hillel's death, one saying has reached us from the eulogy they spoke over him: "Alas, the pious one! Alas, the humble one, disciple of Ezra!" (Sotah 48b; and there in the Yerushalmi, and in the Tosefta ch. 13). These few words tell the whole strength of his deeds and his character. In them his piety and his humility are portrayed: for he was pious in all his deeds, and humble without equal among the sages of his generation; and they compared him to Ezra because he was held to have restored the Torah to its former state, like Ezra in his generation. About sixty years before the destruction of the Temple was the year of his death. Of Shammai's death nothing has been told us. But from two matters that are told it appears that Shammai died before Hillel. The first: from the incident that befell Hillel with the disciples of Shammai, and their fixing the halakhah according to Hillel; in that whole story Shammai is no longer mentioned. The second: from what is told us, that once they wanted to appoint a certain sage as head of the court; and we have no time more fitting than the end of Hillel's days in which that sage could have lived. Therefore the conjecture is close that Shammai died in Hillel's lifetime, and they wanted to appoint another head of the court in his place (Darkhei ha-Mishnah p. 57).

בבחינת מותו של הלל הגיע אלינו מאמר אחד מתוך ההספד אשר הספידו לו: אי p. 166 חסיד, אי ענו תלמידו של עזרא! (סוטה מ"ח: ושם בירושלמי ובתוספתא פי"ג). המלים המעטים האלה יגידו את כל תקף מעשיו ואת תכונתו. בהם מתוארת חסידותו וענותו כי חסיד היה בכל מעשיו, וענו מאין משלו בין חכמי דורו, והמשילוהו לעזרא יען שהוא היה מוחזק כי החזיר את התורה לישנה כעזרא בדורו. כמו ששים שנה קודם חרבן הבית היה שנת מותו. ממות שמאי לא הוגד לנו מאומה. אך משני דברים אשר יסופרו נראה כי מת שמאי לפני הלל, האחד. מן המעשה אשר אירע להלל עם תלמידי שמאי. ומה שקבעו ההלכה כהלל, ובכל הספור הזה לא נזכר שמאי עוד. והשני, ממה שמסופר לנו שרצו פעם אחת להעמיד חכם אחד להיות אב בית דין, ואין לנו זמן כשר יותר מסוף ימי הלל אשר חי בו החכם ההוא ועל כן ההשערה קרובה כי מת שמאי בחיי הלל, ורצו להעמיד אב בית דין אחר במקומו (דרכי המשנה צד 57).

12

This sage was Akavya ben Mahalalel. He was glorious in honored virtues, and they said of him that the Temple court was not closed upon any man in Israel equal to him in wisdom and in fear of sin. His lifetime falls before the destruction of the Temple, in the generation after the death of Shemaiah and Avtalyon. Of these two sages he spoke harsh words, not as a disciple speaks of his master, which is a sign that he was not among those who came to their house of study. No more than five halakhot of his are known.25 And it is no wonder, after they so extolled his wisdom, how it happened that nothing more than these halakhot is left to us; for it is true that the sages of his generation were not pleased with him. They said of him that he was excommunicated by his colleagues because he opened his mouth to speak harsh words against Shemaiah and Avtalyon. And had they not wished to make his affair known to the generations, in order to teach them a moral lesson, these halakhot too would not have reached us. For so it is told: they sent to him, saying: Retract four things [that you say], and we will make you head of the court. He said to them: It is better for me to be called a fool all my days than to be wicked for one hour before the Omnipresent. But the reason why he did not want to retract the four things, he himself has explained to us in his testament to his son. For when his days drew near to die, he said to his son: Retract the four things that I used to say. And when his son asked him: And why did you not retract? he answered him: I heard from the many and they heard from the many; I stood by what I heard and they stood by what they heard. But you have heard from an individual and from the many; it is better to leave the words of the individual and to act according to the words of the many. From this it appears that in Akavya's days there was a dispute of many against many about these halakhot; and if it is true that he lived in the days of Shammai and Hillel, this will be proof that the division among the Pharisees had already begun then. And indeed it also appears from this that at the end of his days they tried to join the division together; for, as emerges from Akavya's words, he alone held to his tradition, and all the sages of the generation disagreed with him. Perhaps this union was the work of Bava ben Buti, who was zealous for Hillel's teaching and succeeded in fixing the halakhah according to him. But the union was not whole and complete, as is seen from the dispute that persisted between the disciples of Shammai and Hillel. Of the events of Akavya's life nothing is known but this little, but it is enough to show us the character of his soul and his qualities. Not many are like him, who would cast authority away and choose to sit low rather than reverse their opinions and thoughts. In agreement with this deed is one of the moral sayings told in his name (Avot 3:1): Consider three things and you will not come into the hands of transgression: know from where you came, and where you are going, and before whom you are destined to give judgment and reckoning. And one who teaches such a lesson, what are authority and honor worth in his eyes? Beyond his quality of humility and fear of God, the quality of love of justice and uprightness adorned him. This we recognize from the answer he gave to his son when he was about to die. At that time his son asked him to commend him to his colleagues, but Akavya did not want this, for he said that it is not of the quality of justice and uprightness that a father should try to incline the hearts of others toward his son by the father's merit; rather it is a man's own merit that will win him grace and favor from others. Therefore he said to his son: Your deeds will bring you near, and your deeds will keep you far.

זה החכם היה עקביא בן מהללאל. הוא היה מפואר במעלות נכבדות ואמרו עליו שאין עזרה ננעלת בפני אדם מישראל בחכמה וביראת חטא כמוהו. ימי חייו חלים לפני חורבן הבית, ובדור אחרי מות שמעיה ואבטליון. מאלה שני החכמים דבר בדברים קשים לא כדבר התלמיד מרבו אשר זה אות כי לא היה מבאי בית מדרשם. לא נודעו ממנו יותר מחמש הלכות25. ולא יפלא אחרי שהפליגו חכמתו איך אירע כן שלא נשאר לנו מאומה יותר מאלה ההלכות? כי אמת הוא שלא היתה דעת חכמי דורו נוחה ממנו. ואמרו עליו שנתנדה מחבריו על אשר פצה פיהו לדבר בדברים קשים נגד שמעיה ואבטליון. ולולא שהיו רוצים להודיע ענינו לדורות למען למד אותם מוסר גם אלה ההלכות לא היו מגיעים אלינו. כי כן יסופר: שהיו שולחים אליו לאמר: חזור בך בארבעה דברים ונעשך אב בית דין. אמר להם מוטב לי להקרא שוטה כל ימי ולא לעשות שעה אחת רשע לפני המקום. אך הטעם למה לא רצה לחזור מארבעה הדברים? הביננו הוא עצמו בצואתו לבנו. כי כאשר קרבו ימיו למות אמר לבנו: חזור בך בארבעה דברים שהיית אומר. ויהי כאשר שאל אותו בנו: ואתה למה לא חזרת בך? השיבו אני שמעתי מפי המרובים והם שמעו מפי המרובים אני עמדתי בשמועתי והם עמדו בשמועתם אבל אתה שמעת מפי יחיד ומפי המרובים מוטב להניח דברי היחיד ולעשות כדברי המרובים. מזה נראה כי בימי עקביא היתה מחלקת מרובים נגד מרובים על אודת אלה ההלכות ואם אמת הוא כי חי בימי שמאי והלל יהיה זה ראיה כי כבר אז החל הפרוד בין הפרושים. ואמנם נראה מזה גם כן כי בסוף ימיו השתדלו לחבר הפירוד כי כאשר יוצא מתוך דברי עקביא לא היה רק הוא אשר החזיק בשמועתו וכל חכמי הדור חלקו עליו ואולי היתה ההתאחדות הזאת פעולתו של בבא בן בוטי אשר קנא לתורתו של הלל והצליח לקבוע הלכה כמותו, אבל לא היתה התאחדות תמימה ושלמה, כאשר נראה מהמחלקת אשר התמידה בין תלמידי שמאי והלל. מקורות חיי עקביא לא נודע רק זה המעט, אבל די הוא להראות לנו תכונת נפשו ומדותיו. לא רבים כמוהו אשר ישליכו שררה מנגד ויבחרו לשבת בשפל מאשר יהפכו דעותיהם ומחשבותיהם. מסכים עם המעשה הזה, הוא אחד ממאמרי מוסר הנאמר על שמו (אבות פ"ג מ"א): הסתכל בשלשה דברים ואי אתה בא לידי עברה, דע מאין באת, ולאן אתה הולך, ולפני מי אתה עתיד ליתן דין וחשבון. ומי אשר יורה מוסר כזה במה נחשבו השררה והכבוד בעיניו? יתר על מדת ענותו ויראת אלהים ענדתהו מדת אהבת הצדק והיושר. זה נכיר מתוך התשובה אשר השיב לבנו כאשר נטה למות. בשעה ההיא בקש בנו ממנו שיפקוד עליו חבריו, אבל עקביא לא חפץ בזה כי אמר אין זה p. 167 ממדת הצדק והיושר שישתדל האב להטות לב אחרים אל בנו בזכות האב, אמנם זכות עצמו היא היא שתקנה לו חן וחסד זולתו, ועל כן אמר לבנו מעשיך יקרבוך ומעשיך ירחקוך.

13

Here before us is a span of time of about half a century in which the ways of the Oral Torah began to go on new paths. These days are no longer like the first days. The strong bond that held all the Pharisees to be of one heart and one mind slackened little by little. The effort of the leaders to uphold the teaching of the elders against the opposition of the Sadducees did not attain its aim, for the ways they chose for themselves found opponents even among the Pharisees. These ways were two. The first: the widening of the midrash of the Torah in a manner that had no precedent. The second: the clarification of the halakhot of their forebears, to find their reason and their ground. Both these ways found opponents and achieved the very opposite of what they wanted. They wanted to reconcile the opinions, and they caused dispute. The disciples of Shammai and Hillel began to multiply disputes, and these were not passing disputes but lasting ones; for from that time they have not ceased, and God knows how much longer!

הנה לפנינו משך זמן כחצי מאה אשר בו החלו דרכי התורה שבעל פה ללכת ארחות חדשות. לא עוד כימים הראשונים הימים האלה. הקשר האמיץ אשר החזיק את כל הפרושים להיות לב אחד ודעה אחת נרפה מעט מעט. השתדלות הראשים להחזיק תורת הזקנים לעמת התנגדות הצדוקים לא השיגה מבוקשה כי הדרכים אשר ברו להם מצאו מנגדיהם גם בין הפרושים אלה הדרכים היו שנים. האחד, הרחבת מדרש התורה באופן אשר לא היה כמוהו מלפנים. והשני, בירור ההלכות של קדמוניהם למצוא טעמם ונימוקם. ושני הדרכים האלה מצאו מנגדים והשיגו ההפוך ממש שרצו. הם רצו לפשר הדעות, וגרמו המחלוקת. תלמידי שמאי והלל החלו להרבות המחלוקות, ולא היו מחלוקות עוברות כי אם מתמידות כי למן העת ההיא לא פסקו ואלהים יודע עד מתי עוד!

The law of the prosbul applies both to a loan by deed and to an oral loan; except that in the view of the Talmud, for a loan by deed one does not by law need a prosbul, but only hands his deeds to the court, for so we learned: "one who hands his deeds to the court, [the Sabbatical year] does not cancel." And in Gittin (1a): ⚠23 "I hand [it] over to you, that you be the exactors and I will not exact." And in Makkot (3a) he [Rashi] explains that handing one's deeds to the court is the prosbul; but the Tosafot there dispute this and write that handing one's deeds to the court and the prosbul are two different things, and so is the view of Maimonides, ch. 9 of Hilkhot Shemittah; and Rashi stands alone in this view. But Rashi's source is from Sifre Re'eh §113: "'what is yours with your brother your hand shall release' [Deuteronomy 15:3] — and not one who hands his deeds to the court; from here they said: Hillel instituted the prosbul." At first sight this is as Rashi's view. But in Yerushalmi Shevi'it 10:3 they said: "From here they found support for the prosbul, that it is from the Torah. Is the prosbul from the Torah? When Hillel instituted it, they supported it with words of Torah." Accordingly, the intent of the Sifre is: since the handing over of deeds is a matter of Torah, Hillel too instituted the prosbul. So the Ran explained, and his proof is from the Mishnah where we learned: "One who lends on a pledge, and one who hands his deeds to the court, [the Sabbatical year] does not cancel; a prosbul, it does not cancel." As for Rashi's explanation, "that you be the exactors and I will not exact," the meaning is not that the court collects the debt, for [the lender] wrote: "whenever I wish I may collect it" — so the court does not collect. And we learned: "A creditor who produces a bond of debt and there is no prosbul with it — these are not paid" (Ketubbot 9:9); so the deed and the prosbul were in the hand of the lender. But Rashi's intent is that through the prosbul he is not counted as an exactor if he collects the debt; rather the exacting comes from the side of the court. I have written all this because I saw that others erred in these laws. Know that this midrash, "what is yours with your brother your hand shall release" etc. — "and not one who hands his deeds to the court," in my opinion was newly made after Hillel's enactment. For Hillel's enactment, to uproot the release of debts, which is a commandment written in the Torah, was hard in their eyes; therefore they strove to find it a support and expounded that for one who hands his deeds to the court [the year] does not cancel by law, and in this way they supported Hillel's enactment on the exposition of the verse. For if the law had already existed, from the meaning of the verse in the Torah, that for one who hands his deeds to the court [the year] does not cancel, it would not have been so necessary to make an enactment to uproot a law of the Torah. ↩ back to the text

In Middot 5:4 we learned: the Chamber of Hewn Stone, there the Sanhedrin sat and judged the priesthood. And in Kiddushin (76b) they said: there sat those who traced the lineage of priesthood and Levites; and Rashi explained: in the Chamber of Hewn Stone etc., and he means the mishnah of Middot. This needs study, for they did not say there explicitly "the Sanhedrin sat," as in the reading of the mishnah. Moreover, the phrase "those who traced the lineage of priesthood" shows that it speaks of a special office, and it appears from this that for the purpose of examining the lineages of the priesthood there was a special court. And indeed this is stated in Sifrei, Korah, piska 116: "And you and your sons with you shall keep your priesthood for everything of the altar and within the veil" [Numbers 18:7]: from here they said, there was a place behind the house of the veil where they examined the lineages of the priesthood. So those who traced the lineage of the priesthood sat in a special place, and that is behind the house of the veil. And who were those who kept the priesthood, that is, the lineages of the priesthood? Aaron and his sons. And although in the Tosefta too, Hagigah ch. 2, the reading is "in the Chamber of Hewn Stone, where the lineages of priesthood and the lineages of Levites sit," it appears that this too is not exact, and the main thing is as stated in the Sifrei; and in the Sifrei the intent was necessarily not the Chamber of Hewn Stone, for if so it would not say "there was a place."
