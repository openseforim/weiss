The days of the Judges up to the days of Samuel the prophet

ימי השופטים עד ימיו של שמואל הנביא

3

After the Israelites came to the rest and to the inheritance [Deuteronomy 12:9], their rest was nevertheless not complete and lasting; at times they had peace from their enemies round about, and at times they were oppressed. Their bad condition was always caused by their straying from the goal that the divine Lawgiver had set for them. All the days of the Judges they were like the degenerate shoots of a strange vine [Jeremiah 2:21] in the vineyard of the Lord of hosts. The bond of unity was slack among them; therefore the pillars of society tottered, and in all the affairs of this society there was no order. Time after time they turned from the way of their God, and when they turned away their shade too departed from them [Numbers 14:9]. The laws of their life and their customs were of mixed stock: half of them from the laws of the Torah and half from the laws of the nations round about them (1 Samuel 7:3). As this mixture grew, so in that measure the faithful traces of the Torah keep dwindling in all the accounts of the events in the time of the Judges. Only at rare times do we see a small spark of light from the traces of the Torah, which darkness takes away almost at once, and it is gone. And even in the moment when it shines and flashes it is not a bright light, but like a dim light glimmering out of the fog. Yet the weakness and the fewness of these traces will not keep us from finding in them too plain hints that teach us how some of the statutes of the Torah were understood at that time. True, not all that is told of their deeds is fit to show us the sense of the statutes; for many of these deeds were dross from a mixture of foreign laws that are not of Hebrew birth. And over many narratives a veil of poetic figure has been placed. But with a proper and measured examination we shall soon be able to tell the pure from the impure of foreign admixture. So too by reasoned inquiry we shall succeed in removing the veil, until we also succeed in knowing by which way of the spirit a given statute and law, written before us in the Torah, went to find its source in the custom of the people. ⚠1

אחרי באו בני ישראל אל המנוחה ואל הנחלה לא היתה בכל זאת מנוחתם שלימה ומתמידה, פעם היה להם שלום מאויביהם מסביב ופעם נלחצו. עמדתם הרעה היתה תמיד נסבה מהתרחקם מהמטרה אשר הציב להם המחוקק האלהי. כל ימי השופטים היו כסורי גפן נכריה בכרם ה' צבאות. קשר האחדות רפה ביניהם, על כן רופפו עמודי החברה, בכל עניני זאת החברה לא היו סדרים. פעם אחר פעם סרו מדרך אלהיהם ובסורם אף סר מעליהם צלם. משפטי חייהם ומנהגיהם היו מבני התערובות מחציתם ממשפטי התורה ומחציתם ממשפטי העמים אשר סביבותיהם (ש"א ז' ג') כאשר גדלה התערובת הזאת אף כן ולערך זה רושמי תורה נאמנים ילכו הלוך וחסור בכל ספורי הקורות אשר בזמן השופטים אך לעתים רחוקים נראה רשף אור קטן מרושמי התורה אשר כמעט רגע אופל יקחהו ואיננו. וגם ברגע אשר יאיר ויבריק איננו אור בהיר רק הוא כאור כהה הנוצץ מתוך הערפל. אמנם חולשת הרושמים האלה ומעוטם לא יעצרו אותנו מלמצוא גם בהם רמזים נכחים אשר יורונו איך הובנו בעת ההיא מקצת מחקי התורה. האמנם כי לא כל המסופר ממעשיהם יאות p. 11 להראות לנו מובן החקים; כי הרבה מן המעשים האלה היו לסגים מתערובת משפטים זרים אשר לא מילדי העברים הם. וגם בהרבה ספורים הושם מסוה מליצה שירית על פניהם. אבל בבחינה נאותה ומתונה נכיר עד מהרה להבדיל בין הטהור ובין הטמא מתערובת נכריה. וכן נצליח בחקירה מושכלת להסיר המסוה, עד כי נצליח גם לדעת איזה דרך הרוח אשר הלך איזה חק ומשפט הכתוב לפנינו בתורה למצוא לו מקור במנהג העם.

4

Some narratives from the days of the Judges will be to us like a sealed book, unless we determine that the sense of some statutes that are obscure in the Torah was made clear among them, whether through a received and known interpretation or perhaps through a proper exegesis of the words of the statute. For all the days of Joshua and the elders the Israelites followed the Lord and served Him (Joshua 24:31); therefore every act of a religious kind that they did at that time was according to the Torah that Joshua received from Moses, and it is self-evident that the nature of the act will teach us how a given statute was understood which they had before their eyes in doing that act. The earliest and most compelling proof for the acceptance of the Oral Torah in its interpretation of the phrase "the morrow of the Sabbath," written with the counting of seven weeks from Passover to Pentecost [Leviticus 23:15], as referring to the morrow of the first day of Passover, comes to us from what is written in the book of Joshua (5:11): "And they ate of the produce of the land on the morrow of the Passover, unleavened bread and parched corn, in the selfsame day." These words, "unleavened bread and parched corn, in the selfsame day," refer without doubt to the statute in the Torah, "And ye shall eat neither bread, nor parched corn, nor fresh ears, until this selfsame day" [Leviticus 23:14]; and who can doubt that "the morrow of the Passover" here interprets "the morrow of the Sabbath" said there, and that its sense is as it was understood in the Oral Torah? It is all one to us whether "the morrow of the Passover" is understood as the fifteenth of Nisan (Ibn Ezra in Tosafot, Rosh Hashanah 13a) or as the sixteenth of Nisan (Ri in Tosafot there, and in Kiddushin 36b ⚠2); in either case it is proved that this verse understood the phrase "the morrow of the Sabbath" as referring to the morrow of the Passover. So too we learn from the words of this book a new statute on the matter of the oath. When a man swears by the Lord, the God of Israel, he is bound to keep what has gone out of his lips, even if afterwards it becomes known that the reason that moved him to confirm a thing by oath was mistaken. And only for this reason did the princes (who had sworn to the Gibeonites by the Lord, the God of Israel, to make peace with them) see it as their duty to keep this oath, though it became known to them afterwards that the Gibeonites had come to them with guile. From this we see that the statute of the oath was understood among them thus: that in no case may a man transgress what has gone out of his lips in an oath, and also that in no case can the oath be annulled, and no man has the power to release it. And since there is no hint of all this in the Torah, necessarily this was the received interpretation among them. But this interpretation was abolished at a later time, and the sages of the Oral Torah turned away from it entirely. So too we are told that when Joshua made a covenant with the people and set them a statute and an ordinance in Shechem, he wrote the words in the book of the Torah of God, and took a great stone and set it up there under the oak that was by the sanctuary of the Lord (Joshua 24:26). Now when we read these words we necessarily recall the great warning with which the Torah warned to remove every asherah and to cut it down (Exodus 34:13). And in Deuteronomy this warning was extended also to planting near the altar of the Lord (Deuteronomy 15 ⚠3). Remembering this, shall we not ask how Joshua planted an oak in the sanctuary of the Lord, and acted against the plain sense of the verse that forbids all planting near the altar? And according to the sense of the words of the statute among the sages of the Oral Torah, the planting of any tree is forbidden both in the Chosen House, even on the Temple Mount, and at a high place (Sifre Shofetim, pisqa 145); necessarily, then, in the ancient days this statute was understood in another way: the asherah in its essence is the idol itself which the Canaanites worshipped, namely Ashtoreth. And since the form of this idol was a planted tree, on which perhaps the figure of the idol was itself carved, or an image was hung on it, it is proper to speak of planting and cutting down in its case. Now if they were warned against planting any tree near the altar, the intent was not to join the rite of the worship of the nations, who serve their gods, to the manner of the worship of the Lord in the sanctuary; but without intent of idolatry they saw no reason to be on guard. Therefore Joshua rightly did not hesitate to plant an oak in the sanctuary of the Lord, or, if it had been planted before, to leave it there without cutting it down. Two consequences follow for us from this. The one: the case of Joshua shows us in what way some statutes of the Torah were understood in the early time; and another: we see that the sense of these statutes changed in the course of time.

5

In particular we must consider what traces there are among the narratives from the time of the Judges that let us recognize what their view was in those times regarding the sacrificial worship. True, it cannot be denied that at every step in the course of their history they turned from the statutes of the Torah and walked in the statutes of the nations, and these became a snare to them. Therefore it is hard for us to judge from their deeds about their views in the understanding of the statutes. Yet for all that there are some narratives of the events of that time on whose root views on the understanding of legal matters can be traced, especially regarding the worship of God in general and the sacrificial worship in particular. These views of theirs are hidden in subtle hints within the narratives. And if we wish to find these hidden things, we must first examine the nature of the sacrificial worship at its beginning, and what reason the Torah had to give it a place, since it is low in relation to God. On this the ancients already said: from days of old, sacrifices among men were signs of thanksgiving and of submission to God, and of the effort to win favour and atonement from Him. Israel in Egypt too were without doubt accustomed to some worship. Though we do not find that they sacrificed in Egypt, and on the contrary it is said, "Lo, shall we sacrifice the abomination of the Egyptians before their eyes, and will they not stone us?" [Exodus 8:22], at all events it is certain that they had a false worship like the worship of Egypt. And the Torah itself testified that they went astray after the satyrs and sacrificed their sacrifices to them [Leviticus 17:7]. Therefore we see that, according to the state of understanding in religious matters in these early times, the sacrificial worship was a religious worship of all the nations. There was no need to command it, for it was a thing understood of itself; but they were warned about the conditions and the forms. And since they were accustomed to sacrifice to the satyrs, they were commanded to bring their sacrifices to the door of the tent of meeting, so that they would no more sacrifice their sacrifices to the satyrs after whom they go astray, and would bring them to the Lord, whose dwelling was in the tent of meeting. So too in Deuteronomy they were commanded not to sacrifice except in the place which the Lord will choose. And all this so that the law of sacrifices would not be in the hand of each and every one, for otherwise they would easily turn from their God to go astray after other gods and serve them. Therefore, if we find that great and good men of the nation departed from the forms, this is a sign to us that they thought the form was only a fence against the breach and not an end in itself; and therefore, when the intent is acceptable, departing from the form does not prevent the acceptance of the sacrifice. And at special times we find that they sacrificed in every place, by the consent of the Torah or of their own accord. The Torah itself commanded to build an altar on Mount Ebal and to offer burnt offerings on it and to sacrifice peace offerings (Deuteronomy 26 ⚠4), and so Joshua did after crossing the Jordan (Joshua 8); Gideon built an altar and offered a sacrifice on it (Judges 10 ⚠5); the angel who came to Manoah said to him, "and if thou wilt offer a burnt offering, thou must offer it unto the Lord" (ibid. 13); Samuel offered a whole burnt offering at Mizpah (1 Samuel 7); so too the men of Beth-shemesh offered burnt offerings and sacrificed peace offerings when the ark of God returned from the field of the Philistines (ibid. 10 ⚠6); and at the renewal of Saul's kingship in Gilgal they sacrificed there sacrifices of peace offerings before the Lord (ibid. 11), and the tabernacle was not then at Gilgal. And after this too, when Saul smote the garrison of the Philistines, Saul offered the burnt offering at Gilgal (ibid. 13), and at Michmash too he built an altar (ibid. 14), and Samuel went to Bethlehem to sacrifice to the Lord (ibid. 15 ⚠7). All these are deeds actually done, and they testify that in all the early times they were not strict about sacrificing in every place; though there is no clear proof in the verses according to their plain sense that it was permitted to sacrifice at a high place at any of the times from when they crossed the Jordan and settled in the land. True, the sages of the Talmud expounded the verses by the ways of their midrash in a manner far from the plain sense. And even with their midrash they found no just ground to show a definite permission explicit in Scripture, but they judged from the later to the earlier. That is: from what is told in Scripture, that they were accustomed to sacrifice at a high place and it was not counted to them as sin, from this they judged that they necessarily knew it was permitted by the Torah. And in the Talmud they said that at one time the high places were forbidden and at another time permitted (Zevaḥim 112b; Sifre Re'eh, pisqa 66); and this wording shows that they were forbidden and permitted by the word of a prophet or of someone else. Since we saw them sacrificing at a high place, they necessarily judged that it had been permitted to them. And if we ask what reason they saw to permit sacrifice at a high place against the Torah, they found no other reason than to say that it was a temporary ruling (Zevaḥim, ibid.). This excuse was always in their mouth when they found in Scripture that the prophets or other men of rank acted with sacrifices not according to the Torah, whether in sacrificing at a high place or in departing in some other way from the statutes regarding the form of the sacrifice and its offering (Avodah Zarah 24b). Yet if we consider the matter, this excuse does not suffice in every place to quiet the doubts. When Gideon offered a burnt offering to the Lord, he offered it with the wood of the asherah (Judges 6), and he offered the second bullock that belonged to his father, which (according to the opinion of the Talmud) his father had set apart to offer to idolatry (Temurah 28b); and all this is against the statute of the Torah as it was understood by the sages of the Oral Torah; for according to that understanding the wood of an asherah may not come on the altar, nor may a sacrifice to the Lord be brought from what was set apart for idolatry. True, the Talmud found an explanation, saying that this was an exception, because Gideon did all this by the word of the Lord. But though this may suffice as an excuse for Gideon, we still do not know why the Lord commanded him to do in this way a deed against the law. And even if we assume there was a need of the hour to sacrifice, we see no need and necessity on that account to offer specifically the second bullock that was set apart for idolatry (as they understood it in the Talmud); could he not have offered the first bullock? And likewise, why did He command him to sacrifice with the wood of the asherah: did he have no other wood? It can only be that in the ancient days even the great and the good were not so strict about the forms, but thought that the form was only a fence against the breach and not an end in itself, and therefore, when the intent is acceptable, departing from the form does not prevent the acceptance of the sacrifice. And though it cannot be denied that there was an existing and known statute that one may not sacrifice in every place, nevertheless it also cannot be denied that they were not strict about this in a worship done occasionally, for the hour, and with the intent of the sake of the Lord. Only for the fixing of the sacrificial worship was the Torah strict, that it be fixed in one place only, because fixing it in different places is dangerous to the unity of the nation, and it would also easily become to them a snare of idolatry. As we see explicitly in the story of the sons of Gad and the sons of Reuben and the half-tribe of Manasseh: all the alarm that their brethren across the Jordan, in the land of Canaan, raised against them when they built an altar was only because they thought that they had built an altar for burnt offering and for sacrifice, and they saw in this rebellion and trespass against the Lord (Joshua 22). But as regards the acceptance of the sacrifice, all depends on the intent and the thought of the worshipper; and therefore occasionally and for the hour one may sacrifice in every place. And perhaps this is how the statute of the Torah was understood which commands that they may make an altar of earth to offer on it burnt offering or sacrifice, and in every place where His name is remembered He will come to them and bless them (Exodus 21 ⚠8). For from the tenor of these words it appears that all places are fit for sacrificing there, provided only that the sacrifice be for the sake of the Lord. And what is said in other places, to beware of sacrificing in another place than the place which the Lord will choose, is surely to be explained in the way we have said.1 And from the time the kingdom was divided there is no doubt that they offered sacrifices in the land of the kingdom of Israel on altars that they built for the sake of the Lord, and all this was with the consent of the prophets; for otherwise how is it possible that Elijah the prophet should complain that the altars dedicated to the Lord were torn down, if these altars stood against the Torah and in prohibition (1 Kings 19:10, 14)?

ביחוד יש לנו להתבונן במה שיש בין הספורים אשר מזמן השופטים רושמים אשר יתנונו להכיר מה היתה דעתם במועדים ההם בבחינת עבודת הקרבנות. האמנם שאין להכחיש שעל כל צעד וצעד בתהלוכות קורותיהם סרו מחקי התורה וילכו בחקות הגוים ויהיו להם למוקש. אשר על כן קשה לנו לשפוט ממעשיהם על דעותיהם בהבנת החקים. אבל עם כל זה יש ויש ספורים מקורות הזמן ההוא אשר על שרשם התחקו דעות בהבנת ענינים חקיים וביחוד בבחינת עבודת אלהים בכלל ועבודת הקרבנות בפרט. דעותיהם אלה טמונים בסתר רמזים דקים בקרב הספורים. ואם נרצה למצוא אלה התעלומות, נשים לב ראשונה לבחון מהות עבודת הקרבנות בראשיתה, ומה היתה הסבה שהניחה לה התורה מעמד בשגם פחותה היא לערך האל ה', על זה אמרו כבר הקדמונים מימות עולם היו הקרבנות אצל בני אדם אותות על התודה ועל הכניעה לאלהים, ולהשתדלות נשיאות פנים וכפרה מלפניו. גם ישראל במצרים הורגלו בלי ספק באיזה עבודה. אף שלא מצאנו שהקריבו במצרים ולהפך נאמר: הן נזבח תועבת מצרים לעיניהם ולא יסקלונו, על כל פנים ודאי הוא שהיתה להם עבודה טפלה כעבודת מצרים. והתורה עצמה העידה, שהיו זונים אחרי השעירים וזובחים להם את זבחיהם. ועל כן ראינו כי לפי מצב ההשכלה בענינים דתיים בזמנים הקדומים האלה היתה עבודת הקרבנות עבודה דתית של כל העמים. ולא היה צורך לצוות עליה כי זה היה דבר המובן מעצמו אבל הוזהרו על התנאים והתוארים. ומהיות שהיו רגילים לזבוח לשעירים נצטוו שיביאו קרבנותיהם אל פתח אוהל מועד למען לא יזבחו עוד את זבחיהם לשעירים אשר הם זונים אחריהם והביאום לה' אשר משכנו באוהל מועד. וכן נצטוו במשנה תורה שלא יקריבו כי אם במקום אשר יבחר ה'. וכל זה שלא תהיה תורת הקרבנות ביד כל אחד ואחד שלולא כן בקל יסורו מאחרי אלהיהם לזנות אחרי אלהים אחרים ועבדום. ועל כן אם נמצא שגדולים וטובים באומה נטו מן התוארים זה לנו לאות שחשבו שאין התאר כי אם גדר מפני הפרצה ולא תכלית לעצמו ועל כן כשהכונה רצויה אין נטייה מן התאר מעכבת הרצאת הקרבן. ובעתים מיוחדים נמצא שהיו מקריבים מדעת התורה או מדעת עצמם בכל מקום. התורה עצמה צותה לבנות מזבח בהר עיבל ולהעלות עליו עולה ולזבוח שלמים (דברים כ"ו), וכן עשה יהושע אחרי עברו את הירדן (יהושע ח'), גדעון בנה מזבח ויקריב עליו קרבן (שופטים י') המלאך אשר בא אל מנוח אמר אליו: ואם תעשה עולה לה' תעלנה (שם י"ג) שמואל הביא עולת כליל במצפה (ש"א ז') וכן אנשי בית שמש העלו עולות ויזבחו שלמים בשוב ארון אלהים משדה פלשתים (שם י') ובהתחדש מלוכת שאול בגלגל זבחו שם זבחים שלמים לפני ה' (שם י"א) ואז לא היה המשכן בגלגל. וגם אחרי זה כהכות שאול את נציב הפלשתים העלה שאול את העולה בגלגל (שם י"ג) ובמכמש בנה גם כן מזבח (שם י"ד) ושמואל הלך בית הלחמי לזבוח לה' (שם ט"ו). כל אלה הם מעשים הנעשים בפועל והם מעידים שבכל הזמנים הקדומים לא היו מקפידים מלהקריב בכל מקום, עם שאין הוכחה ברורה בכתובים לפי פשוטם, שיהיה היתר להקריב בבמה באחד מן הזמנים שמן העת אשר עברו את הירדן וישבו בארץ. האמנם שחכמי התלמוד דרשו לפי דרכי מדרשם את הכתובים באפן רחוק מן הפשט. וגם עם מדרשם לא מצאו משפט צודק להראות רשיון החלטי המפורש בכתוב אלא שפטו מן המאוחר אל הקודם. וזה מתוך מה שמסופר בכתובים שהיו נוהגים להקריב בבמה ולא נחשב להם לחטאת מזה שפטו שבהכרח ידעו שהוא מותר מן התורה. ובתלמוד אמרו שפעם נאסרו הבמות ופעם הותרו (זבחים קיב: ספרי ראה פי' ס"ו) ולשון זה מורה שנאסרו והותרו על פי נביא או זולתו. כי ראינו אותם מקריבים בבמה על כן p. 13 בהכרח שפטו כי הותר להם. ואם נשאל מה ראו על ככה להתיר ההקרבה בבמה שלא כתורה? לא מצאו סבה אחרת כי אם באמרם שהיתה הוראת שעה (זבחים שם). זאת ההתנצלות היתה תמיד בפיה במצאם במקרא שהנביאים או זולתם מאנשי המעלה עשו בקרבנות שלא כתורה, כן בהקריבם בבמה, כן בנטותם באפן אחר מן החקים בבחינת תואר הקרבן והקרבתו (ע"ז כד:). אמנם אם נתבונן בדבר אין ההתנצלות הזאת מספקת בכל מקום לשכך הספקות. הנה כשהקריב גדעון עולה לה' העלהו בעצי אשרה (שופטים ו') והקריב את הפר השני אשר לאביו, והוא הפר אשר — לפי דעת התלמוד — הקצהו אביו להעלותו לע"ז (תמורה כח:) וכל זה נגד חק התורה לפי מה שהובן מחכמי התורה שבעל פה; כי לפי המובן הזה לא יבואו עצי אשרה על המזבח וגם לא יובא קרבן לה' מן המוקצה לע"ז. והן אמנם כי התלמוד מצא פשר דבר באמרו שזה יוצא מן הכלל, יען כי עשה גדעון כל זאת על פי ה'. אבל זה יספיק להתנצלות לגדעון, אולם עוד לא ידענו למה צוה ה' ככה לעשות מעשה אשר לא כדת? ואף אם נניח שהיה צורך שעה להקריב, לא ראינו בעבור זה צורך והכרח להעלות ביחוד הפר השני המוקצה לע"ז — כמו שהבינו בתלמוד — הלא היה יכול להקריב את הפר הראשון? וכן למה צוהו להקריב בעצי אשרה וכי לא היו לו עצים אחרים? אמנם אין זאת אלא שבימים הקדמונים גם הגדולים והטובים לא הקפידו כל כך על התוארים אבל חשבו שאין התואר כי אם גדר מפני הפרצה ולא תכלית לעצמו ועל כן כשהכונה רצויה אין הנטייה מן התואר מעכבת הרצאת הקרבן. ואף שאין להכחיש שהיה חק קיים ונודע שאין להקריב בכל מקום בכל זאת אין גם להכחיש כי לא הקפידו על זה בעבודה שעשו דרך עראי לשעה ובכונה לשם ה'. ורק לקביעות עבודת הקרבנות קפדה התורה שלא תוקבע אלא במקום אחד כי קביעותה במקומות שונים מסוכנת לאחדות האומה וגם בקל תהיה להם למוקש עבודה זרה. כאשר נראה מפורש במעשה בני גד ובני ראובן וחצי שבט המנשה. שכל החרדה שהחרידו אליהם אחיהם אשר בעבר הירדן בארץ כנען בבנותם מזבח לא היתה רק מפני שחשבו שבנו מזבח לעולה ולזבח וראו בזה מרד ומעל בה' (יהושע כ"ב) אבל מצד הרצאת הקרבן הכל תלוי בכונת העובד ומחשבתו ועל כן בדרך עראי ולשעה יכול להקריב בכל מקום. ואולי הובן כן חק התורה אשר יצוה שיוכלו לעשות מזבח אדמה להעלות עליו עולה או זבח. ובכל מקום אשר יזכר שמו יבוא אליהם ויברכם (שמות כ"א). כי משפט הדברים האלה נראה שכל המקומות ראוים להקריב שם רק תהיה ההקרבה לשם ה'. ומה שנאמר במקומות אחרים להזהר מלהקריב במקום אחר חוץ מבמקום אשר יבחר ה' ודאי יתפשר באופן שאמרנו1. ומעת שנפלגה הממלכה אין ספק כי הקריבו קרבנות בארץ ממלכת ישראל על מזבחות אשר בנו לשם ה' וכל זה היה ברצון הנביאים, כי לולא כן איך אפשר שאליהו הנביא יתאונן על אשר נהרסו המזבחות אשר הוקדשו לה' אם אלה המזבחות שלא כתורה ובאיסור היו עומדים (מ"א י"ט, י'. י"ד).

Another part of the worship of the Lord was the vow. And we find a wonderful example of a vow in an early time, and of the manner of its fulfilment, in the vow of Jephthah. This judge, on going out to war against the Ammonites, vowed a vow: "If the Lord deliver the children of Ammon into my hand, then whatsoever cometh forth of the doors of my house to meet me shall surely be the Lord's, and I will offer it up for a burnt offering" (Judges 11). And as he prospered in his way and returned, behold, his only daughter came to meet him. And when he saw this evil occurrence he rent his clothes, for he said, "I have opened my mouth unto the Lord, and I cannot go back"; and at the end of two months he did with her according to his vow which he had vowed. Here we see that in these early times it was the custom to offer a human soul as a burnt offering to the Lord, the God of Israel; for if this had been against the law, the words put in Jephthah's mouth would be words without sense. How could he say, "I have opened my mouth unto the Lord, and I cannot go back," if the fulfilment of his vow was counted an abomination in the eyes of the Lord and a sin of bloodshed? And it is no wonder that such a false and cruel belief was not cut off at once from among the nation, for since they became a people they grew up and lived among the nations who held this belief. True, the divine statutes strove with all their might to cut it off, in warning against the worship of Molech; and perhaps the sanctity of the human firstborn too was meant to stand in place of the sacrifice of their sons, which all the nations of the East practised.2 But in spite of all this the false belief still kept root and branch among Israel. The fixed worship of human sacrifice was decreed against and cut off, but it still continued occasionally among individuals, and it was not counted among them as opposed to the religion of the Torah of Moses to offer a human sacrifice in thanksgiving for a great deliverance by which they were saved, or to quiet the anger of the Lord and to appease Him; for they found no definite statute against human sacrifice, and though it is said, "of the herd and of the flock shall ye bring your offering" [Leviticus 1:2], they erred in thinking that this does not exclude human sacrifice in great and special events. Only by this can we understand that Jephthah fulfilled his vow against his will, and that there was no man who set his mind to say that there was no duty to fulfil his vow, and that on the contrary it was his duty not to fulfil it, for human sacrifice is an abomination in the eyes of God, and the Lord did not accept it. And perhaps this corrupt view came to them from a terrible error by which they erred in the sense of the pure words of the Torah, and they twisted and corrupted its pleasant words: either the story of Abraham's binding of Isaac confused their mind, or perhaps they likewise understood, in this corrupt sense, the words of the Torah in the affair of Baal Peor, where the Lord commanded Moses, at the time when the people sinned and went astray with the daughters of Moab and joined themselves to Baal Peor, to take all the heads of the people and hang them up unto the Lord before the sun, that the fierce anger of the Lord may turn away from Israel (Numbers 25:4). And seeing that by the command of the Lord they were hanged up to the Lord, not for their own sin (for they had not sinned) but only to turn away the fierce anger of the Lord, they judged from this that human sacrifice for atonement was not abhorred in the eyes of the Lord. But the prophets, they taught the view that the Lord does not delight in many sacrifices, and does not desire that a man give his firstborn for his transgression, the fruit of his body for the sin of his soul, to make atonement (Micah 6:7). And with a zeal like fire Jeremiah was zealous against those who thought that God's will lay in human sacrifices, and he said that the Lord did not command so, and it did not come into His heart.3 From all this we see that in different times the words of the Torah too were understood in different ways. And beyond what we have seen so far from the case of Jephthah, we see further that in those days it was not possible to annul a vow in any way, even if the one who vowed regretted it; for otherwise, did not Jephthah regret his vow, and could they not have annulled it? Why then did they not annul it or release it (Genesis Rabbah 60)? Therefore it is a clear thing that the power to annul a vow that a man vows, by means of release, which has no foundation except in the oral tradition, was not yet known in this early time; and the bearers of the Torah of that time too did not know it, did not receive it and did not hand it down (see Ḥagigah 10a and Rashi's commentary), as we have already seen in the oath of the princes to the Gibeonites.

מקרבנות אדם נגזרה ונכרתה אך עוד התמידה דרך עראי אצל הפרטים ולא היה נחשב אצלם כמתנגד לדת תורת משה להקריב קרבן אדם לתודה על ישועה גדולה שנושעו, או לשכך חרון אף ה' ולכפר פניו כי לא מצאו חק החלטי נגד קרבן הם הורו דעה כי לא ירצה ה' ברבוי קרבנות ולא יחפוץ שיתן האדם בכורו פשעו ופרי בטנו חטאת נפשו למען התכפר (מיכה ו, ז). ובקנאה כאש קנא ירמיהו נגד החושבים כי רצון אלהים בקרבנות אדם ואמר כי לא ה' צוה ככה ולא עלתה על לבו3. והנה מכל זה אנו רואים כי בזמנים שונים הובנו גם כן דברי התורה באופנים שונים. ויותר ממה שראינו עד הנה מענינו של יפתח, הנה נראה עוד, כי בימים ההם לא היה אפשר לבטל נדר בשום ענין אף אלו הנודר מתחרט, שאם לא כן הלא יפתח התחרט על נדרו והיו יכולים לבטלו ולמה לא בטלוהו או התירוהו (ב"ר פ"ס) ועל כן דבר ברור הוא כי הכח לבטל נדר אשר ידור האדם על ידי התרה אשר אין לו יסוד כי אם בקבלה שבעל פה, עוד בעת הקדומה הזאת לא ידעוהו, וגם נושאי התורה של הזמן ההוא לא הכירוהו לא קבלוהו ולא מסרוהו (עי' חגיגה י'. ופרש"י) וכאשר ראינו כבר בשבועת הנשיאים אל הגבעונים.

7

Another kind of vow was the Naziriteship. This too appears to us in this ancient time in a wondrous light. According to the statute of the Torah, the Nazirite vows a vow with his mouth to separate himself as a Nazirite to the Lord (Numbers 6). Yet we are told that Samson, who judged Israel twenty years, was a Nazirite by the word of an angel who appeared to his mother and said to her: "Behold, you shall conceive and bear a son, and no razor shall come upon his head, for the child shall be a Nazirite to God from the womb" (Judges 13). It is true that this Naziriteship is unique of its kind, for according to the narrative it came about by the word of the Lord (Nazir 4b); and we do not wish to draw conclusions from miraculous events, for in such things lies the boundary of the judgment of reason, and with them the domain of faith begins. But one thing we can draw from what is described in this story, namely, that in ancient times they made it a custom to dedicate a newborn child as a Nazirite to the Lord. We find a parallel to it in Samuel, who was likewise consecrated from the womb to be a Nazirite. This was not by a special command from the mouth of the Lord; only his mother consecrated him of her own free will, and he was a Nazirite all the days of his life (Nazir 66a). And although it is not stated explicitly that Samuel was a Nazirite, yet since there was put in his mother's mouth "and no razor shall come upon his head" [1 Samuel 1:11], we understand the intent of her other words, which she said, "and I will give him to the Lord all the days of his life" [1 Samuel 1:11]: that the meaning is the Naziriteship, for letting the hair of the head grow long is the sign of the Naziriteship. Now both of them, Samson and Samuel alike, defiled themselves with the dead (Judges 15:15; ⚠9 1 Samuel 15:33), and they could not guard themselves from impurity although they were Nazirites all the days of their lives (Nazir 4b). Further, Samuel was a Nazirite by his mother's vow, and without doubt there were other such Nazirites in ancient times. This is also proved from the saying "for the child shall be a Nazirite to God from the womb": thus the notion "a Nazirite to God from the womb" was not new, but was known and current in their mouths. And now we have seen that there is a kind of Naziriteship that a person does not take upon himself, and that even a woman may vow her son as a Nazirite (see Nazir 28b), and that there is a Naziriteship in which they did not guard themselves from impurity; and of all this there is nothing explicit in the statute of the written Torah before us. Here we see old traditions and understandings of the Torah, some of which agree with, and some of which differ from, the traditions that were handed down in the Oral Torah.

מין אחר מן הנדר היה הנזירות. אכן גם הוא יגלה לנו בזמן הקדום הזה במראה נפלאה. הנה לפי חקת התורה ידור הנזיר נדר בפיו להזיר נזיר לה' (במדבר ו'). ואמנם יסופר לנו כי שמשון אשר שפט את ישראל עשרים שנה היה נזיר על פי מלאך אשר נגלה לאמו, ואמר לה הנך הרה וילדת בן ומורה לא יעלה על ראשו כי נזיר אלהים יהיה הנער מן הבטן (שופטים י"ג) והן אמנם שזאת הנזירות יחידה במינה כי לפי המסופר נהיה כן על פי ה' (נזיר ד':) וגם אין רצוננו להוציא תולדות ממעשה נסים כי בכאלה מונח הגבול p. 15 למשפט השכל ובהם מתחילה מדת האמונה. אבל אחת נוכל להוציא מן המתואר בספור הזה, והוא, שהנהיגו בימי קדם להזיר ילד היולד נזיר לה'. ונמצא דוגמתו אצל שמואל שהוקדש גם הוא מבטן להיות נזיר. וזה לא היה במצוה מיוחדת מפי ה', רק אמו הקדישתהו מרצון נפשה, ויהי נזיר כל ימי חייו (נזיר ס"ו.). ואם גם לא פורש כי היה שמואל נזיר, אך מאשר שומה בפי אמו „ומורה לא יעלה על ראשו" נבין את המכוון בדבריה האחרים אשר אמרה „ונתתיו לה' כל ימי חייו" שהכונה על הנזירות כי גדול פרע שער הראש הוא אות הנזירות. והנה שניהם בין שמשון ובין שמואל היו מטמאים למתים (שופטים ט"ו, ט"ו. ש"א ט"ו ל"ג) ולא יכלו להשמר מן הטומאה מהיותם נזירים כל ימי חייהם (נזיר ד':) ועתה הן שמואל היה נזיר על פי נדר אמו. ובלי ספק היו נזירים אחרים כאלה בימי קדם, וכן מוכח מן המאמר „כי נזיר אלהים יהיה הנער מן הבטן" הרי שמושג: נזיר אלהים מן הבטן, לא היה מחודש אבל היה נודע ושגור בפיהם. ועתה ראינו שיש מין נזירות אשר לא יזיר האדם על עצמו ואף האשה מדרת את בנה בנזיר (עי' נזיר כ"ח:) ושיש נזירות אשר לא נשמרו בה מן הטומאה ומכל זה אין דבר מפורש בחקת התורה הכתובה לפנינו. והננו רואים קבלות ישנות ומובנים בתורה אשר קצתם מסכימים וקצתם נבדלים מן הקבלות אשר נמסרו בתורה שבעל פה.

8

Around the days of the judgeship of the judge Ibzan — and in the opinion of some, Ibzan is Boaz — took place the event told to us of the history of Ruth the Moabite. According to what emerges from the scroll that tells this event, it appears that Israel in those days walked after its God, and the Israelite moral virtues stood in strength and might. This scroll presents to us a lovely picture of purity of virtues, of wholeness of hearts and of fitness in deeds of charity, which were widespread in that generation. From it we learn that there was at that time quiet and peace in the land, and proper order and arrangement in political affairs. This being so, we may firmly hold that whatever traces of the Torah are mentioned in this scroll did not turn to dross, and there is no admixture of foreign opinions in it; therefore it will be of great use for our purpose.4 It is told there that Ruth the Moabite, who returned with Naomi her mother-in-law from the field of Moab to the land of Judah, went down — on her mother-in-law's advice — to the threshing floor, to Boaz, who was a kinsman of the family of her dead husband, and asked him to spread his skirt over her, for he was a redeemer. Boaz consented to her request and said, "All that you say I will do" [Ruth 3:11], but since there is a redeemer nearer than I, I cannot redeem. And when the redeemer refused to redeem, then Boaz redeemed; and together with the redemption by which he redeemed the inheritance of the dead man, he also acquired Ruth the Moabite as his wife, to raise up the name of the dead upon his inheritance, that his name be not cut off from Israel. If we consider this story, we find that Ruth's request was founded on nothing but the duty of levirate marriage. For so she said: "Spread therefore your skirt over your handmaid, for you are a redeemer" [Ruth 3:9]; that is, since he was a redeemer, it was for this reason his duty to spread his skirt over her and take her to himself as a wife. And this also emerges explicitly from the words of Boaz, who said: "Moreover Ruth the Moabite have I acquired to be my wife, to raise up the name of the dead upon his inheritance, that the name of the dead be not cut off from among his brethren" [Ruth 4:10]. Close to these are the words said in the Torah in the law of levirate marriage, where a reason is given for this law: to raise up the name of the dead, so that the name of the dead be not blotted out of Israel [Deuteronomy 25:6–7]. Now there is no doubt that in the acquisition of Ruth, whom Boaz acquired as his wife, we have before us a description of the commandment of levirate marriage, which was a statute in Israel. But how was this commandment understood in its reason and in its purpose? This too becomes clear from the words of the scroll. There we see that this statute was brought in connection with the law of redemption, which was also a statute: that the nearest kinsman should redeem the inheritance of his kinsman. Therefore the law of levirate marriage was extended to all the kinsmen of the dead man, so that even if the dead man had no brothers, his widow would be taken in levirate marriage by one of his other kinsmen. Necessarily, then, the statute that speaks of the brother was not understood strictly, but as speaking of the usual case. Nor can we refute this understanding from the plain sense of the words of the statute. On the contrary, if we understand the end of the words of the statute, we may judge that there is no need to insist on the brother. For there a further saying is added: "the wife of the dead shall not be married abroad to a stranger" [Deuteronomy 25:5]; and this proves that the Torah warned only that the wife of the dead not be married to a stranger who is not of her dead husband's family; but in truth the nearest kinsman redeems, and he too raises up the name of the dead; and that the brother is mentioned is because he is the head and the first in the chain of kinship. And from this too we will not turn our eyes: it is clear that Ruth was bound in the bond of marriage while she was still a Moabite, and in the days of her widowhood she entered the religion of Israel. From this it appears that at that time a marriage bond between an Israelite man and a woman who was not of the daughters of Israel was valid. For if it had not been valid in their opinion, there would be no duty of levirate marriage upon the levir either. Necessarily such a marriage, once it had actually been carried out, counted with them as a marriage contracted according to religion. This scroll has further preserved for us one of the modes of acquisition in ancient times, called the drawing off of the shoe. For so it is told: "Now this was the custom in former time in Israel concerning redeeming and concerning exchanging, to confirm all things: a man drew off his shoe and gave it to his neighbour" [Ruth 4:7]. It appears from this that in the days of the writer of the scroll the matter had already sunk into oblivion, and they no longer did so.5 Now from these particulars too we have learned that the laws of the Torah were carried out in different ways and were also understood in different ways, sometimes in agreement and sometimes in contradiction with their understanding in the Oral Torah; but however it may be, we have seen in this clear traces of traditions and interpretations concerning the statutes of the Torah.

סביב לימי שפוט השופט אבצן — ולדעת קצת הוא אבצן הוא בועז — אירע המעשה המסופר לנו מקורות רות המואביה. לפי היוצא מן המגלה המספרת זה המעשה נראה כי הלך ישראל בימים ההם אחרי אלהיו ומדות המוסר הישראליות עמדו בתוקף ועז. המגלה הזאת תציג לפנינו מראה נחמדה מטוהר המדות, מתומת הלבבות ומכשרון מעשה הצדקה אשר התפשטו בדור ההוא. ממנה נתבונן כי היה בעת ההיא השקט ושלוה בארץ וערך וסדר נכון בענינים המדיניים. ובהיות כן נוכל להחזיק בבירור כי מה שנזכר במגלה הזאת מרושמי התורה לא היה לסיגים ואין בו תערובת דעות זרות, ועל כן מאד יועיל לתכליתנו4. הנה יסופר שם כי רות המואביה השבה עם נעמי חמותה משדה מואב אל ארץ יהודה, ירדה — בעצת חמותה — הגורן אל בועז אשר היה קרוב למשפחת בעלה המת, ותבקש ממנו כי יפרוש כנפיו עליה כי גואל הוא. בועז התרצה לבקשתה, ויאמר כל אשר תאמרי אעשה, אך בהיות גואל קרוב ממני לא אוכל לגאל. ויהי כי מאן הגואל לגאל אז גאל בועז, ועם הגאלה אשר גאל את נחלת המת קנה לו גם את רות המואביה לו לאשה, להקים שם המת על נחלתו ולא יכרת שמו מישראל. ואם נתבונן בספור הזה נמצא כי בקשת רות לא p. 16 נוסדה כי אם על חובת הייבום. כי כן אמרה: ופרשת כנפיך על אמתך כי גואל אתה, ועל כן בהיותו גואל בעבור זה חובתו לפרוש כנפיו עליה ולקחת אותה לו לאשה, ואף כן מפורש יוצא מדברי בועז, שאמר: וגם את רות המואביה קניתי לי לאשה להקים שם המת על נחלתו ולא יכרת שמו מעם אחיו. וקרובים לאלה הם הדברים האמורים בתורה בחק הייבום, שניתן טעם לחק הזה להקים שם המת ולא ימחה שם המת מישראל. ועתה לא יסופק כי בקנין רות אשר קנה בועז אותה לו לאשה יש לפנינו המתואר מענין מצות היבום אשר היתה לחק בישראל. אבל איך הובנה זאת המצוה בטעמה ובתכליתה? גם זה מתברר מתוך דברי המגילה. שם אנו רואים שזה החק הובא בחיבור עם משפט הגאולה אשר גם היה לחק שהקרוב הקרוב יגאל נחלת קרובו, ועל כן נתפשט חק היבום על כל קרובי המת, באופן שאף אם לא היו אחים למת תתיבם אלמנתו לאחד משאר קרוביו ובהכרח שהחק המדבר מן האח לא יובן בדקדוק אלא דבר בהווה. ואף לא נוכל לסתור המובן מפשט דברי החק. ויהי להפך אם נבין את סיום דברי החק נוכל לשפוט כי אין חובה להקפיד על האח. כי שם נוסף מאמר: לא תהיה אשת המת החוצה לאיש זר וזה מוכיח שלא הזהירה התורה רק שלא תהיה אשת המת לאיש זר אשר איננו ממשפחת בעלה המת אבל אמת שהקרוב הקרוב יגאל וגם הוא יקים את שם המת ומה שנזכר האח, זה הוא על היותו בשלשלת הקורבה הראש והראשון. וגם מזה לא נסב עינינו; הנה מבורר שרות נקשרה בקשרה אישות בעוד היתה מואביה, ובימי אלמנותה נכנסה לדת ישראל. ומזה נראה כי בעת ההיא היה קיום לקשר האישות בין איש ישראל ובין אשה אשר לא מבנות ישראל היא. שאילו לא היה לו קיום לפי דעתם אין גם על היבם חובת היבם. ובהכרח שהיתה אצלם אישות כזאת אחרי שנתקיימה בפועל כאישות שהתקשרה על פי הדת. עוד קיימה לנו המגילה הזאת אחד מדרכי הקנין בזמן קדום הנקרא שליפת המנעל. שכן יסופר: וזאת לפנים בישראל על הגאולה ועל התמורה לקיים כל דבר שלף איש נעלו ונתן לרעהו. ונראה מזה כי בימי כותב המגילה כבר נשתקע הדבר ולא נהגו עוד כן5. והנה גם מן הפרטים האלה למדנו שבאופנים שונים היו מקיימים דיני התורה והובנו גם בדרכים שונים פעם בהסכם ופעם בסתירה עם מובנם בתורה שבעל פה אבל איך שיהיה ראינו בזה רושמים ברורים לקבלות ולפירושים בענין חקי התורה.

But this time, the time of the nation's childhood, was like a cloudy morning. The sun of religion hid itself, and only at rare times did it come out for moments from the thickness of the cloud. Then, in the last period of the time of the Judges, suddenly, all at once, the clouds burst open and a new light spreads its wings over the full breadth of the land of Israel. A man in whom is the spirit of God, mighty in wisdom, glorious in understanding, and lofty and very high in moral teaching, he grew up as a tender plant out of a dry ground [Isaiah 53:2] and became a wonder among his people. From the womb and from birth God consecrated him and made him a prophet in Israel. And the man was — Samuel the seer. It was a time of stumbling for Jacob, and no spirit was left any longer in the deeds they did to fulfil the statutes and teachings of God; and their service of the Lord was a commandment of men learned by rote [Isaiah 29:13]. In the midst of this time Samuel arose and opened the springs of the Torah, which had been stopped up, at times by folly, at times by greed, ⚠10 and at times by the weakness of the heads of the people and its leaders. The word of the Lord was rare in those days; there was no frequent vision (1 Samuel 3:1). The priests were the leaders of the people and led them astray, for they too did not walk uprightly. Moral teaching was despised; their heart was set on gain, and they did not know the Lord. Even when the people served the Lord, they did not serve Him alone, but set their heart on other gods as well (1 Samuel 7:3), and their faith turned to dross and their fear of the Lord became corrupt. To straighten such crooked things was the charge of Samuel; and his wisdom, the strength of his spirit, and the spirit of the Lord that stirred in him stood by him, so that he succeeded in what he desired. How wonderful was the longing that was in him to revive the heart of the people with the spirit of knowledge and the fear of the Lord, with the spirit of the Torah and of enlightenment! As a bright light this is revealed to our eyes when we see that he did new things in the land, such as had not been before him. He made houses for the Torah, to teach the children of Israel knowledge and the Torah of the Lord. In these houses the sons of the prophets grew up, and not many days passed before a band of prophets was already met with, with Samuel standing as head over them [1 Samuel 19:20]. The place in which he founded a faithful house to educate the sons of the prophets according to his way was Ramah, for there was his home. The place of assembly was called Naioth, and this is a name for a house of study of the Torah, for in it the Torah was expounded. This work became a blessing among his people. From these Naioth the Torah went forth, and they were an opened source for a new spirit and for enlightenment. From it springs of wisdom spread forth, and historical literature grew and bore fruit; it was this that was the first foundation of the book of Samuel that we have (1 Chronicles 29:29). And without doubt poetic and prophetic literature also began to flourish then; the pleasant songs and the sublime poems that David sang are surely children of this time. We also have plain traces that Samuel did not cling to the material side of the statutes of the Torah, but for him the main thing was the purpose of the statute and its moral spirit. This is shown to us by the words said in his name concerning the service of the sacrifices. He was the first to speak against the outward act in that service, and he said that the outward act is not the main thing, and the Lord has no delight in burnt offerings and sacrifices as in obeying the voice of the Lord: "Behold, to obey is better than sacrifice, and to hearken than the fat of rams" (1 Samuel 15). How sublime are these words against the error of the mass of men, who think that the outward act in the performance of the sacrifices suffices to obtain favour from the Lord! But Samuel taught by this that it is not so, and God has no delight except in obeying His voice. For a man who holds these views it will not be hard to think that the Torah too does not insist on the form of some statute. Hence it is not surprising if we find that in his time they sometimes offered a female burnt offering, which is not according to the law of the Torah (1 Samuel 6:14). And he himself offered such a burnt offering (ibid. 7:9). True, the Talmud (Avodah Zarah 24b) wants to find an explanation, and says that Samuel did so on a private high place, and that what the men of Beth-shemesh did was nothing but a ruling for the hour. But where is the permission to depart from the statute of the Torah altogether, even for an hour? And on what did Samuel rely to permit a female burnt offering on a private high place? It can only be that with them the main thing was the purpose of the sacrifice, whatever its form might be. Therefore Samuel departed only from the form of the statute. He thought that although the Torah gave a statute that every burnt offering be of the males, it was not said on that account that a female burnt offering would not be accepted; rather, in truth, if one brought a female burnt offering with a pure intention, it too was acceptable before the Lord. And this ancient opinion left a remnant, in part, also in the later generations, who likewise decided that several matters concerning the form were said only as a commandment, and departing from them does not prevent the acceptance of the sacrifice (Zevaḥim 47a and in many places; and see Pesaḥim 63a and the Tosafot).

אך כבקר עבות היתה העת הזאת היא עת ילדות האומה. שמש הדת הסתתרה ואך לעתים רחוקים יצאה לרגעים ממעבה הענן. אז בפרק האחרון מזמן השופטים הנה פתאום לפתע יבקעו העננים ואור חדש יפרוש כנפיו במלא רוחב ארץ ישראל. איש אשר רוח אלהים בו, אמיץ בחכמה, הדור בתבונה, ונשא וגבה מאד במוסר השכל הוא עלה כיונק מארץ ציה ויהי למופת בקרב עמו. מבטן ומלידה הקדישו אלהים ויתנהו נביא בישראל. והאיש היה היה — שמואל הרואה. עת כשלון היתה ליעקב ולא נשאר עוד רוח במעשיהם אשר עשו למלאות חקי אלהים ותורותיו. ותהי עבודתם את ה' מצות אנשים מלומדה. ובקרב העת הזאת קם שמואל ויפתח מעינות התורה אשר נסתמו פעם מסכלות פעם מבצין אף פעם מרפיון כח ראשי העם ומנהיגיו. דבר ה' היה יקר בימים ההם ואין חזון נפרץ (ש"א ג' א') הכהנים היו מאשרי העם והתעום כי גם הם לא הלכו נכחה. המוסר בזו, אל בצע היה לבם ואת ה' לא ידעו. אם גם עבדו העם את ה' לא לו לבדו עבדו אבל גם לאלהים אחרים שמו לב (ש"א ז' ג') ותהי אמונתם לסיגים ויראתם את ה' נאלחה. לישר מעוקלות p. 17 כאלה היתה פקידת שמואל; וחכמתו, ועצמת רוחו, ורוח ה' אשר נוססה בו עמדו לו כי הצליח את אשר חפץ. מה נפלאה התשוקה אשר הייתהו להחיות את לב העם ברוח דעת ויראת ה' ברוח התורה וההשכלה. כאור בהיר יגלה זה לעינינו בראותנו אשר עשה חדשות בארץ אשר לפניו לא היה כן. הוא עשה בתים לתורה ללמד את בני ישראל דעת ותורת ה'. באלה הבתים גדלו בני נביאים ולא ארכו הימים וכבר נפגש חבל נביאים אשר שמואל עומד נצב עליהם. המקום אשר בו יסד בית נאמן לחנך את בני הנביאים על פי דרכו, הוא היה רמה, כי שם ביתו. מקום הועד היה נקרא נויות. והוא כנוי לבית מדרש התורה כי בו היתה התורה נדרשת. זאת הפעולה היתה לברכה בעמו. מאת הנויות האלה יצאה התורה והן היו מקור נפתח לרוח חדשה ולהשכלה. ממנו יפוצו מעינות חכמה וספרות דברי הימים גדלה ועשתה פרי אשר היא היתה, המסד הראשון לספר שמואל אשר בידינו (דה"י א'. כ"ט, כ"ט) ובלי ספק גם ספרות שירית ונבואית התחילה אז לפרוח, הזמירות הנעימות והמליצות הנשגבות אשר שר דוד הלא הן ילדי זה הזמן. גם יש לנו רושמים נכוחים כי לא דבק שמואל בחומריותם של חקי התורה, אך היה אצלו העיקר תכלית החק ורוח המוסרי. זה הראנו לדעת מן הדברים אשר נאמרו בשמו בבחינת עבודת הקרבנות. הוא היה הראשון אשר ידבר נגד הפעולה החצונית בעבודה ההיא. ואמר כי לא הפעולה החצונית עיקר ואין חפץ ה' בעולות וזבחים כשמוע בקול ה'. הנה שמוע מזבח טוב להקשיב מחלב אלים (ש"א ט"ו). מה נשגבו הדברים האלה נגד משגה ההמון מבני אדם אשר יחשבו כי הפעולה החצונית במעשה הקרבנות מספקת להפיק רצון מאת ה'. אבל שמואל הורה בזה כי אינו כן ואין חפץ אלהים כי אם לשמוע בקולו. לאיש אשר אלה לו לא יקשה בעיניו לחשוב כי גם התורה לא תקפיד על תואר איזה חק. ולזה לא יפלא אם נמצא כי בזמנו הקריבו לפעמים עולת נקבה אשר לא כדת התורה (ש"א ו', י"ד). ואף הוא עצמו הקריב עולה כזאת (שם ז', ט'). האמנם שהתלמוד (ע"ז כ"ד:) רוצה למצוא פשר דבר ואמר ששמואל עשה כן בבמת יחיד ואף אנשי בית שמש שעשו כן לא היה כי אם הוראת שעה. אבל איה הרשיון לנטות מחק התורה מכל וכל אף לשעה? ועל מה סמך שמואל להתיר עולת נקבה בבמת יחיד? אין זאת כי אם שעיקר אצלם תכלית הקרבן יהיה תוארו איזה שיהיה. ועל כן לא נטה שמואל רק מתואר החק. אמנם חשב שאף שנתנה תורה חק שיהיה כל עולה מן הזכרים לא נאמר בעבור זה שעולת נקבה לא תהיה לרצון, אלא באמת אם הביא עולת נקבה במחשבה טהורה גם היא לרצון לפני ה'. וזאת הדעה הקדומה היה לה שארית במקצת גם בדורות האחרונים, אשר החליטו גם כן שכמה דברים בבחינת התואר לא נאמרו כי אם למצוה, ואין הנטייה מהם מעכבת הרצאת הקרבן (זבחים מ"ז. ובכ"מ ועי' פסחים ס"ג. ובתוס').

A great thing was brought about in his time, which had great force down to the end of all the generations of the kingdom of Israel: the change in the manner of governing the people and the land. Behold, since the house of Israel was founded to be a nation and a kingdom, it was not destined to stand under the rule of a king. Rather, from the very beginning its kingdom was called a kingdom of priests, with the Lord at their head as their king. The priests, who were set apart to be holy, were not appointed to be rulers but to be ministers, and they were called holy only because they offer the bread of their God (Leviticus 21). And above all, it lay in the will of the divine lawgiver that there be one law for all, the people as the priest, the small as the great, and not that the people be divided into separate groups, as was the case in Egypt and among other peoples besides them. And who knows whether this was not also the cause of the complaint that Korah made — according to what is told in the Torah — against Moses and against Aaron? For Korah and his congregation thought that they wished to raise themselves above the people of the Lord after the manner of the land of Egypt, where the king stands in the place of God on earth and the people serve him with divine worship (Diodorus I, 90), and the priests are holy to God; and they thought that Moses and Aaron too sought to raise themselves to this rank. Now according to this national character it was fitting that there be no royal dominion over them, but that their king be the Lord God. But that the setting up of a king in Israel would be a breach of the statute of the Torah, this is not written explicitly; yet it emerges explicitly from Samuel's refusal. For when they asked for a king, he refused with all the efforts of his strength to give them a king. And why did he refuse so? Because he thought that the setting up of a king was against the statute of the Torah and against the will and intention of the lawgiver. And without doubt the reason for this was like the reason for several statutes that are aimed against idolatry and at striving to keep it far away: so too the divine lawgiver saw that the setting up of a king could bring them to such corrupt opinions, that they would serve their king with divine worship after the manner of the land of Egypt. And Samuel says explicitly in the name of the Lord: "They have not rejected you, but they have rejected Me, that I should not reign over them" (1 Samuel 9:7). ⚠11 Now since this is not a statute written and known explicitly from the days of Moses, it must have been a known tradition, living among the people. And have we not seen that already in the days of Gideon, when he prevailed over Midian and all the men of Israel said to him, "Rule over us, both you and your son and your son's son," he answered them and said, "I will not rule over you, neither shall my son rule over you; the Lord shall rule over you" (Judges 8:22–23)? Already then this idea was known and widespread in the nation. True, Samuel, for all that he refused to set a king over them, nevertheless set aside his opinion, passed over the statute and did as they wished; but this event is certainly a fitting interpretation of the statute in Deuteronomy concerning the king. For there it says: "When you come to the land ... and shall say, I will set a king over me ... you shall surely set him king over you whom the Lord your God shall choose; from among your brethren shall you set a king over you" (Deuteronomy 17). The matter of Samuel proves that this statute is neither a commandment nor a right to set a king over them, but a permission without the will of the Torah (see Sifre, piska 156). However, this time too we are forced to mention here the system of those who deny the unity of the Torah, according to which this statute in Deuteronomy is later than the time of Samuel, or was written in his time and is none other than "the manner of the kingdom" which Samuel "wrote in a book and laid up before the Lord" (1 Samuel 10:25). Now how will our words be believed according to this system, when we say that the matter of Samuel is the interpretation of the statute, seeing that the statute is an outgrowth of the event? To this we say: besides the fact that this is not our position, and that we think this statute is truly ancient, and that "the manner of the kingdom" does not refer to the statute; and we also see no necessity to give such a statute at a time when the kingdom in Israel was already under the hand of a king through human action; and Samuel's refusal is not surprising either, even though there was an explicit statute, "you shall surely set a king over you," because this statute is only a permission not from the will of the Torah — besides all these, surely every intelligent person will in any case agree with us that there is in the words of Samuel and in the words of Gideon the trace of an old tradition. And now, whatever his opinion on the unity of the Torah may be, this will not harm our purpose, and it is enough for us that we have seen that even the deniers agree with us on this. On the contrary: just as they reduce and diminish the written Torah, so they are forced to increase and enlarge the tradition, for every single part of the Torah whose writing they place later was, in their view, surely received in the nation and alive within it.6 Therefore in any case it is clear that this principle, that the setting up of a king is against the intention of the Torah, was received in the nation even though it is not written, and it serves to interpret the written statute.

דבר גדול התפעל בזמנו אשר היתה לו תעצומה גדולה עד סוף כל הדורות בממלכת ישראל. והוא חלוף אופן ההנהגה את העם והארץ. הן מאז הוסד בית ישראל להיות גוי וממלכה לא היה נועד להיות עומד תחת ממשלת מלך. אך מאז ומראש נקראה ממלכתו ממלכת כהנים, ומלכם בראשם ה'. הכהנים אשר נתיחדו להיות קדושים לא נפקדו להיות מושלים אבל להיות משרתים ולא נקראו קדושים רק מפני שהם מקריבים את לחם אלהיהם (ויקרא כ"א). וביחוד היה מונח כן ברצון המחוקק האלהי שמשפט אחד יהיה לכל כעם ככהן כקטן כגדול, ולא שיחלק העם לחבורות חבורות כאשר היה במצרים ובשאר עמים זולתיהם. ומי יודע אם לא גם זה סבת התרעומת אשר התרעם קרח — לפי המסופר בתורה — על משה ועל אהרן? מפני שקרח ועדתו חשבו שהם רוצים להתנשא על עם ה' כמעשה ארץ מצרים שהמלך הוא תחת אלהים בארץ והעם יעבדוהו בעבודת אלהות (Diodor I, 90) והכהנים הם קדושים לאלהים, וחשבו שגם משה ואהרן בקשו להתנשא p. 18 למדרגה הזאת, ועתה לפי התכונה הלאומית הזאת ראוי שלא תהיה שררת מלכות עליהם כי אם מלכם הוא ה' אלהים. אבל אשר יהיה שימת מלך בישראל הפרת חק התורה זה לא נכתב מפורש אבל יוצא כן מפורש ממאונו של שמואל. שבעת אשר שאלו להם מלך מאן בכל מאמצי כח לתת להם מלך. ולמה מאן בכה? יען כי חשב ששימת המלך היא נגד חק התורה ונגד רצון המחוקק וכונתו. ובלי ספק היתה סבת דבר זה כסבת כמה חקים שהם מכוונים נגד עבודה זרה ולהשתדל הרחקתה, אף כן ראה המחוקק האלהי ששימת מלך יכול להביאם אל דעות נפסדות כאלה שיעבדו את מלכם בעבודת אלהות כמעשה ארץ מצרים. ובפירוש יאמר שמואל בשם ה': לא אותך מאסו כי אותי מאסו ממלוך עליהם (ש"א ט', ז'). והנה אחרי כי אין זה חק נכתב ונודע בפירוש מימי משה בהכרח שהיה זה קבלה נודעת וחיה בקרב העם. והלא ראינו שכבר בימי גדעון בהתגברו על מדין ויאמרו אליו כל איש ישראל: משל בנו גם אתה גם בנך גם בן בנך, השיב להם ויאמר לא אמשול אני בכם ולא ימשול בני בכם ה' הוא ימשול בכם (שופטים ח', כ"ב. כ"ג), כבר אז היה הרעיון הזה נודע ונתפשט באומה. ואמנם שמואל בכל מה שמאן לשים להם מלך בכל זאת בטל דעתו והעביר החק ועשה כרצונם, אבל המאורע הזה הוא ודאי פירוש נאות להחק אשר במשנה תורה בבחינת המלך. ששם נאמר: כי תבא אל הארץ ואמרת אשימה עלי מלך שום תשים עליך מלך אשר יבחר ה' אלהיך מקרב אחיך תשים עליך מלך (דברים י"ז). וענינו של שמואל יוכיח כי אין החק הזה לא מצוה ולא זכות לשים עליהם מלך אבל הוא רשיון בלא רצון התורה (עי' ספרי פיסקא קנ"ו). ואמנם גם הפעם מוכרחים אנו להזכיר פה שיטת מכחישי אחדות התורה אשר לפיה החק הזה שבמשנה תורה מאוחר מזמנו של שמואל או נכתב בזמנו והוא הוא משפט המלכה אשר כתב שמואל בספר וינח לפני ה' (ש"א י' כ"ה). ועתה איך יאמנו דברינו לשיטה הזאת לאמר שענינו של שמואל יהיה פירוש החק אחרי שהחק הוא תולדה מן המאורע? על זה נאמר מלבד מה שאין זה מצבנו ושאנו חושבים שזה החק באמת קדום, ומשפט המלוכה אינו מכוון על החק, וגם אין אנו רואים הכרח לתת חק כזה בזמן אשר היתה הממלכה בישראל תחת יד מלך כבר לפעולת אדם, וגם לא יפלא מאונו של שמואל בשגם היה חק מפורש: שום תשים עליך מלך יען כי זה החק אינו רק רשיון שלא מרצון התורה. הנה מלבד כל אלה הלא על כל פנים יודה לנו כל משכיל כי יש בדברי שמואל ובדברי גדעון רושם קבלה ישנה ועתה יהיה דעתו בענין אחדות התורה איזה שתהיה הלא לתכליתנו לא יזיקנו זה ודי לנו במה שראינו שגם המכחישים יודו לנו על זאת. ותהי להפך כי כאשר הם ממעטים ומקטינים התורה הכתובה כמו כן הוכרחו להרבות ולהגדיל הקבלה כי כל חלק וחלק מן התורה אשר הם יאחרו כתיבתו הלא לדעתם היה מקובל באומה וחי בקרבה6. ועל כן בכל ענין ברור הוא שזה היסוד ששימת המלך הוא נגד כונת התורה הוא היה מקובל באומה אף שאינו כתוב, ויאות לפרש חק הכתוב.

The moral results that came out of Samuel's activity had already grown and prospered in his time, and more after his time. From the day that Saul came to the kingship he strove with all his might to remove from the land every vain and foolish custom and every corrupt belief (1 Samuel 23:3). ⚠12 Therefore from the narratives of history too we sometimes perceive how a statute in the Torah was understood. A plain example for this matter comes to us from the story of the war that Saul fought with the Philistines at Michmash. For then, when they prevailed over their enemies, the people flew upon the spoil and slew sheep and oxen on the ground and ate them with the blood. And when they told Saul, "Behold, the people sin against the Lord, in that they eat with the blood," he said: "You have dealt treacherously; roll a great stone to me this day ... and slay them here and eat, and do not sin against the Lord in eating with the blood" (ibid. 14). And Saul's warning in this is without doubt aimed at the statute of the Torah, "You shall not eat with the blood" (Leviticus 19). Now for us today this statute is like a sealed thing, and its matter is hidden from us; but at the time it was given they knew what the sense of this saying was, and what was meant by the expression "eating with the blood." We can only judge, from the fact that this statute comes together with "you shall not use enchantment nor observe times" [Leviticus 19:26], that like them, eating with the blood was a kind of worship among the nations (Ibn Ezra and Ralbag). And if the sages, the bearers of the Oral Torah, handed down to us other and different interpretations of the notion of eating with the blood, this is only because already in their time eating with the blood was no longer among the rites of idolatry, as it had been in ancient days. But in truth the intent of the statute, first and foremost, was nothing other than what we said: that it was a custom of the nations to eat the flesh of the slaughtered animal in the place where its blood was poured out, and they sat around the blood, and this was their manner of worship to their idols. Now if we consider the story mentioned, this true interpretation emerges for us. For on what does Saul place the main weight of his complaint against the people? Only on their slaughtering on the ground and eating with the blood. Therefore what was evil in his eyes and counted as a sin was nothing other than that they slaughtered on the earth, and the blood was on the earth, and there they ate the flesh beside the blood poured out on the ground. And against this practice he commanded them to slaughter upon the great stone that they rolled to him, and then they could eat in any place, but at a distance from the blood, and they would not sin against the statute in the Torah. This custom persisted for many more days, that they were wont to eat with the blood as a form of worship of idols, and even in the days of the prophet Ezekiel we find a trace of it (Ezekiel 33:25), where he tells his people their transgressions, and one particular among them is that they ate with the blood and lifted up their eyes to their idols.7

One example of the understanding of the statutes in those days will show us clearly that they were not then strict about the meaning of the words as written, so that at times they departed from the sense of the statute and applied it in a particular way, against its plain meaning in the Torah; and this is a sign of a reasoned understanding. This example will appear to us in what is told of David when he came to Ahimelech the priest at Nob, the city of the priests. David asked him for bread for his men, and the priest did not withhold from him the holy bread that he had at hand, since he had no other bread besides it. He gave him the showbread that had been removed from before the Lord, but on condition that they had kept themselves from women (1 Samuel 21). Now if we ask what the sense of the Torah's statute is in this matter, we must say that from the statute in the Torah there is no proof in any way that this bread may be eaten by non-priests, even if they are clean. For it says explicitly: "And it shall be for Aaron and his sons, and they shall eat it in a holy place" [Leviticus 24:9]. How then shall we reconcile this with the story before us? We are forced to say that in those days they decided the matter thus: once the showbread had already been removed from before the Lord, its holiness was lessened, so that it was permitted to clean non-priests. And this is surely a departure from the plain sense of the statute in the Torah (see Menaḥot 95).

דוגמא אחת ממובן החקים בימים ההם תראנו ברור כי לא הקפידו אז על מובן הדברים ככתבם עד שלפעמים נטו ממשמעות החק ועשוהו באפן מיוחד נגד פשוטו שבתורה וזה אות על הבנה מושכלת. הדוגמא הזאת תגלה לנו במה שיסופר מדוד בבואו אל אחימלך הכהן לנוב עיר הכהנים. הן דוד שאל ממנו לחם לאנשיו. והכהן לא מנע מאתו לחם הקדש אשר היה בידו כי לא היה לו לחם אחר בלעדו. ויתן לו את לחם הפנים המוסרים מלפני ה', אך בתנאי אם נשמרו אך מאשה (ש"א כ"א). ואמנם אם נשאל מה משמעות חק התורה בענין זה? הלא נאמר כי מן החק שבתורה אין הוכחה בשום ענין שיהיה הלחם הזה מותר להאכל לזרים אף אם הם טהורים. כי בפירוש נאמר והיה לאהרן ולבניו ואכלוהו במקום קדוש. ועתה איך נפשר דבר זה עם ענין המעשה אשר לפנינו? הלא הוכרחנו לאמר שבימים ההם הכריעו הדבר כן: שאם הלחם הפנים כבר מוסרים מלפני ה' נגרעה קדושתם לענין שיהיו מותרים לזרים טהורים. וזה ודאי נטייה ממשמעות פשוטו של החק בתורה (ע' מנחות צ"ה):

The new thing that Samuel created in the land, founding the Naioth in Ramah to educate the youths of Israel in the Torah of the Lord, was the foundation on which the house of Israel would establish its happiness and its survival in the times to come. ⚠13 These Naioth were a blessed source from which the springs of faith and of the knowledge of the Lord would spread in every generation, for from them came the sons of the prophets. They were at all times among the men faithful to the covenant of the Lord, without partiality. They served faithfully on the holy soil, and they were called to proclaim the word of the Lord. Both the priests and the kings turned aside time after time from the way of the Torah, but the prophets did not turn aside. This is the course of the spirit that moves through all the events of the days of the kings up to the exile of the land. Had the prophets not stood up for their people, the Torah would then have perished. And if through all the days of the First Temple we find a slackness in its observance, so that in the days of King Josiah's reign it was actually forgotten, to whom shall this iniquity be reckoned? Only to the priests, who were called to keep the charge of the Torah and profaned their office. They were appointed to teach the people its statutes, but they did the work of the Lord falsely and deceitfully. From the very beginning the Torah of God was given into their hands. The book of the Torah was placed in the Tabernacle of the Testimony beside the ark of the covenant of the Lord (Deuteronomy 31:26); and when Joshua set a statute and an ordinance in Shechem and wrote these words in the book of the Torah of the Lord,8 he placed them likewise in the sanctuary (Joshua 24). For it was an ancient custom among most nations in ancient times to deposit their holy writings in the temples of their gods.9 So it was also in the Temple that Solomon built, which called one part of the house "the devir of the house," because the word [davar] of the Lord was placed in it.10 Of the book of the Torah there was a copy in the hand of the king and also in the hand of the priests. The copy in the priests' hand was kept in the Temple court and was called "the book of the Court," and from it they would correct all the copies (Mo'ed Katan 18b; Yerushalmi Shekalim 4:5 and Sanhedrin 2:4). Now it is self-evident that the priests in charge of the House were also charged with keeping the book (Deuteronomy 31); the king's copy too was made from the one before the Levitical priests (ibid. 17:18), and they were called to teach the statutes of the Lord (ibid. 16:9) ⚠14; and this office continued until a very late time. The prophet Jeremiah describes the office of the priests as the teaching of the Torah, and that of the prophets as having the word of the Lord in their mouth (Jeremiah 18:18). And so it is described also in the book of Malachi (2:7). But how did they keep their charge? On this the prophets tell us that they transgressed their covenant and did violence to the Torah, caused many to stumble in the Torah, and taught for hire. And they said: "those who handle the Torah did not know" the Lord [Jeremiah 2:8]; and those who handle the Torah are none other than the priests. And the prophet Jeremiah suspected them of falsifying the Torah, when he said: "How do you say, We are wise, and the Torah of the Lord is with us? Behold, the false pen of the scribes has made it into a lie" (Jeremiah 5:8) ⚠15. Yet for the most part they did not corrupt their way out of wickedness of heart, but out of a weakness of moral strength to stand against kings; and at every time and in every place where the kings corrupted their way, the priests too could not hold back, and went with the kings after vanity and became vain. And when the king set his heart to his God, the hand of the priests too was with him. In the time of King Jehoshaphat, who walked in the commandments of his God, he appointed some of the priests and the Levites to teach the people knowledge and Torah (2 Chronicles 17); and Hezekiah strengthened the hand of the priests so that they too would hold fast to the Torah of the Lord (ibid. 31:4). But this was a crooked way, and it led only to want; for when the priests did evil in turning aside, they did not do good in returning, since they did not return from the heart. And they still went in this way in the days of the exile. For instead of keeping the Torah of the Lord in their mouths and turning many from iniquity, as they were charged, they turned from the way and caused many to stumble in the Torah, and so corrupted the covenant of Levi (Malachi 2:6–5) ⚠16. Even the high priest Joshua, who was set up at the beginning of the Second Temple, was not free of every blemish, as appears from the prophet's visions directed at him (Zechariah 3); and he was not held to be clean of guilt as befits a high priest (Sanhedrin 93a, and Song of Songs Rabbah on the verse "I rose"). All the more so the priests who lived in the days of the exile in the land of Judah, who did not separate themselves from the nations of the land but mixed with them and were profaned (Ezra 9:11). But the remnant whom the Lord calls were the prophets. Even in the land of the Ten Tribes the prophets stood guard over the religion, from the time the kingdom was divided in the days of Jeroboam until the exile of the land in the days of Hoshea son of Elah. And they drew some of the people after them, so that even among the servants of Ahab's wicked kingdom there were those who feared the Lord (1 Kings 18). And many thousands of the people had their heart whole with the Lord (ibid. 19), and many in Israel were found who went to the prophet on the Sabbath and on the new moon to inquire of the Lord (2 Kings 4:23); and in the days of Elisha the elders sat before him, and their hand was steady with him, and they did their deeds according to the Torah. So in every generation the prophets stood at their post and kept the religion and the Torah. And in truth we owe thanks for the survival of the Torah to them alone. And for that reason this remained firm in the belief of the people: that the chain of transmission of the Torah went from Moses to Joshua, from Joshua to the elders, from the elders to the prophets, and the prophets handed it to the Men of the Great Assembly (Avot 1:1), and there is no mention of the priests; although it cannot be denied in any way that the priests were the first recipients, as it is said: "And Moses wrote this Torah and gave it to the priests the Levites" [Deuteronomy 31:9]. This can only be because they knew clearly that the priests broke the statute and changed the teachings and did not hold the chain of tradition; only the prophets held it. Now we have seen from all this how great was the effect of the prophet Samuel on all the generations to come. For in founding the Naioth to educate the sons of his people in the ways of the Torah, he was the cause of the strengthening of the religion in all the times to come; these Naioth were an open source from which prophecy went forth, and the men who were its bearers were also the bearers of the Torah in every generation. For this reason the sages after him recognized the greatness of his worth and rank, so that they set him on the level of Moses (Jeremiah 15:1); and in later generations too they said that Samuel was equal to Moses and Aaron (Numbers Rabbah ch. 18 and Exodus Rabbah ch. 15). And for that reason we find that from his time on the traces of the Torah multiply in the books meant to tell the events of his time and after it.

In the Gemara, Bava Batra (14b), the scroll of Ruth is ascribed to the prophet Samuel. Without doubt they were moved to hold this opinion by seeing that the whole purpose of the book was to trace the lineage of David to the tribe of Judah, which was destined to be the one from which the rulers in Israel would come forth; and since Samuel sought to found the kingship in the house of David, he saw fit to set forth his lineage first, to show that the kingship befits him by his descent. But to this argument there is an answer. First, we do not find that it was made public in Samuel's lifetime that he had anointed David king; for he did all this in secret. A second thing is proved from the phrase "And it came to pass in the days when the judges judged" [Ruth 1:1]: that between the writer and the Judges a long time lies. It is more fitting to ascribe the composition of this scroll to the time of the division of the kingdom, that is, the time of Rehoboam, when the author wished to show the lineage of the house of David. Yet this too is forced, for then there would be only five generations between the time of the writer and the time of the event; and from his saying there, "Now this was the custom in former time in Israel concerning redeeming and concerning exchanging" [Ruth 4:7], it appears that there was a great distance between the time of the writer and the time of the event. This too is a proof against the view of the Gemara that Samuel wrote it. Some place it as late as the time of the exile (see the commentary of the Christian scholar Bertheau on Judges and Ruth, p. 237), and his proofs are from the character of the language, in which Aramaic traces are already found, such as timtse'in, tidbakin, teda'in, ta'asin, kayyem, Mara; and Shaddai is found in place of El Shaddai, and the whole phrase has a parallel in the book of Job: "and Shaddai has dealt very bitterly with me." We also find that this composition has a reflective character: it is not enough for it to tell only the events that happened before it, ⚠18 but it adds to them also its reflection on them, as when it explains the ancient custom and its difference from the custom of its own time concerning the manner of acquisition. Of this kind too is what it adds at the end of the composition, the lineage of David; and this custom was not found among the writers of chronicles of ancient times. But for all this we have a clear proof that the scroll was written at an early time, from what is said there: "to raise up the name of the dead upon his inheritance." By the word "his inheritance" the speaker without doubt intended the purpose of the statute under discussion, which is that an inheritance not pass from tribe to tribe; and certainly at the time of its writing they still guarded the permanence of the inheritance according to the division of the land among the tribes. And it is a wonder that in Deuteronomy nothing is mentioned of raising up the name of the dead upon the inheritance. ↩ back to the text

The Masoretic reading in Samuel is: once "al ha-dam" [upon the blood] and once "el ha-dam" [to the blood], and both are one in intent. But according to the Septuagint translation, it is rendered in the Torah as if it were written "al he-harim" [upon the mountains], and in Samuel they translated as if it were written "et ha-dam" [the blood]. But we do not know the origin of this reading. For in saying "and they slaughtered on the ground" [1 Samuel 14:32], he indicates that they did not receive the blood in order to eat it, but that it was spilled on the ground. Therefore, if we emend to "et ha-dam," the word "on the ground" has no explanation; for he wanted to tell us on purpose that they slaughtered on the ground, and in this matter, that is, in slaughtering on the ground joined to eating the slaughtered animal in that place, lies the sin. ⚠19 Whereas if they had eaten the blood, the slaughtering on the ground would neither add nor take away. Therefore the correct reading in Samuel is as we have it. And now it is self-evident that in the Torah too the true reading is that of the Masoretic text. For the words "Behold, the people are sinning by eating upon the blood" must refer to some statute, and it is no other statute than the one in the Torah; whereas if the reading were "upon the mountains," we would not know what the sin is in eating upon the blood. ↩ back to the text
