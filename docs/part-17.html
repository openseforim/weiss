Hillel the Babylonian, Menaḥem, Shammai.

הלל הבבלי, מנחם, שמאי.

5

The good forces were weakened by the rod of Herod. The upheaval that he brought about in all the affairs of the nation worked very much for the worse also on the exposition of the Torah. For from the first day that he came to the throne, terror came with him; the peace of the sages passed away and agitation came in its place. Shemaiah and Avtalyon, in the strength of their spirit, still stood in the breach; but when they died, quiet and security died with them, and the light of the exposition of the Torah no longer shone as before, and darkness could be felt [Exodus 10:21]. This darkness was doubled further on account of the division of opinions that broke out among the sages of the Pharisees. And the end of all this was that the time came of the lack of exposition and of the forgetting of the Oral Torah. At this time there came up, like a root out of a dry ground [Isaiah 53:2], a man wonderful in his wisdom and in honorable qualities, and he reached the seat of the patriarchate. This man was Hillel the Babylonian, who is also called Hillel the Elder. His descent was from a high family in Israel. His father was of the tribe of Benjamin and his mother of the root of Jesse the Bethlehemite, of the descendants of Shephatiah son of King David and his wife Abital (Yerushalmi Kil'ayim ch. 9). His native land was Babylonia, and therefore he was called Hillel the Babylonian (Pesaḥim 66a; Sukkah 20a). The year of his birth was around the end of the seventh century of the fourth millennium.1 He had a brother named Shebna, who engaged in trade, and Hillel did not want to be his partner in his trade, for his soul desired the Torah, and he parted from him (Sotah 21a) and went to Jerusalem to hear Torah from the mouth of Shemaiah and Avtalyon. From this it appears that at that time there were no houses of Torah in Babylonia. True, in the days of the exile the Torah was planted in Babylonia; also the men who brought it back to the Land of Israel and planted it there were Babylonians who returned “when the Lord turned again the captivity of Zion” [Psalms 126:1]. But these days were not like the former days, for the times had changed, and the exposition of the Torah had now come to a new stage, and this stage depended on the Land of Israel, where most of the halakhot and midrashim were born. Likewise the ordinances and decrees arose from events that happened there and from the needs of the time in the land of their dwelling. In addition to this, all the days of the Temple there was no man of Israel who did not hold with complete faith to the great principle “for out of Zion shall go forth the Torah” [Isaiah 2:3], and any matter of halakhah, midrash or fence that had not been voted on and concluded in the Great Court in the Chamber of Hewn Stone did not have binding force for them. Therefore it is self-evident that in Babylonia there was no place for the exposition of the Torah, nor could there be. Nor has anything great or small reached us of the exposition of the Torah among the Babylonian Jews; we are only told that they followed the customs of the Jews and were very strict in keeping the Sabbath, and sent their shekels and their offerings and gifts to Jerusalem; but of a spread of Torah study among them there is no hint at all. A hint to the matter, that the exposition of the Torah in Babylonia at that time was only small, is given us by the story told of Izates (Zotos in the Midrash Rabbah ⚠1), king of Adiabene, who entered the Jewish religion with his mother and sent his sons to Jerusalem to study Torah (Josephus, Antiquities 20), although Babylonia was near to him. And from the continuation of that story another difference appears between the views of the Jerusalemites and of the Babylonians. For it is further told there that the teacher of Izates, who taught him the law of the Jews, was a Jew from his own country named Ḥananiah; and when the teacher and his pupil took counsel together about the necessity of circumcision, Ḥananiah permitted the king to accept the Jewish religion without circumcising the flesh of his foreskin, for he said that it is possible to worship God even while uncircumcised, if only he cleaves to the law of the Jews with a whole heart. Not so thought another Jewish sage from the land of Galilee, called by the name Eleazar, for he told the king that it is not enough to know the ways of God and His laws, but he must keep His commandments, and circumcision is a duty from which there is no exemption in any way. From this it appears that the Babylonian did not make the deed the main thing, and certainly the Babylonians were not strict in the details of the practical commandments as the people of the Land of Israel were; and this too is some proof that in Babylonia they were not strict in the details of the Torah and of the Scribes as the Jerusalemites were. And who knows whether it was not this laxity that caused the Babylonians to be hated by the people of the Land of Israel, so that every frivolous thing (Yoma 66b), and likewise every improper deed by the measure of proper conduct, they ascribed to the Babylonians (Menaḥot 100a). And it is a clear fact that at that time the Babylonians were regarded in the eyes of the people of the Land of Israel as not knowing the Torah. For only in this way can we understand the saying of the sons of Betera, who said — when Hillel expounded before them to bring proof that the Passover overrides the Sabbath — “We have already said: is there any hope from a Babylonian?” (Yerushalmi Pesaḥim 6:1). And it is no empty thing that the Babylonian Gemara omitted this saying from the baraita, and always strives to show the strength of the Babylonians in the Torah, and that at all times they were the men who brought the Torah to the Land of Israel; this is only because the reverse was well known among the people of the Land of Israel. But the effort of the later sages to show that it was not so did not help them, and their testimony is contradicted by reality. For what does it help when they say that in the days of Ezra the Torah was forgotten and he brought it up with him from Babylonia? This is still no decisive proof that the exposition, as it developed from the days of Ezra to the days of Hillel, found a place for itself in Babylonia. And what more does it help if they boast that Hillel was a Babylonian and that he founded the Torah anew in the Land of Israel at the time when it had been forgotten, since it is a clear fact that Hillel did not bring all his Torah and his wisdom from his country, but rather he went to the Land of Israel to expound the Torah? Therefore Hillel did not bring the Torah from Babylonia; rather it was the reverse: he went up to the Land of Israel and studied there, and returned to Babylonia and brought the Torah with him, and perhaps he was the first who began to rouse the heart of his Babylonian countrymen to the exposition of the Torah. And in the days of the last war that the Jews had with the Romans, some of the sages escaped for their lives and went down to Babylonia or to Aram (Mesopotamia) near it, such as R. Judah ben Betera, who settled in Nisibis in Aram; and without doubt other sages went down there too; and likewise after the destruction sages of the Land of Israel settled there, such as Ḥananiah the nephew of R. Joshua. Therefore from the time of Hillel onward the exposition of the Torah was brought to Babylonia by various sages from the Land of Israel.

הכחות הטובים נחלשו משבט הירודוס. המהפכה אשר הפך בכל עניני האומה עצמה מאד לרעה גם על דרישת התורה. כי למן היום הראשון אשר בא למלוכה באה בהלה אתו, מנוחת החכמים חלפה ויבא רגז תחתיה. שמעיה ואבטליון בתעצומת רוחם עוד עמדו בפרץ, אך כאשר מתו השקט ובטחה אתם מתו ואור דרישת התורה לא נגה עוד כמלפנים וימש חשך. החשך הזה עוד נכפל לרגלי פרוד הדעות אשר פרץ בין חכמי הפרושים. ויהי אחרית כל אלה כי הגיע זמן חסרון הדרישה ושכחת התורה שבעל פה. בעת הזאת עלה כיונק מארץ ציה איש נפלא בחכמתו ובתכונות נכבדות ויגיע עד כסא הנשיאות. האיש הזה היה הלל הבבלי אשר נקרא גם הלל הזקן. תולדתו ממשפחה רמה בישראל. אביו היה משבט בנימין ואמו בתים לתורה. הן אמת כי בימי הגולה היתה התורה נטועה בבבל. גם האנשים אשר החזירוה לארץ ישראל ונטעוה שם היו בבליים אשר שבו בשוב ה' את שבות ציון. אבל לא כימים הראשונים היו הימים האלה כי העתים השתנו ודרישת התורה באה עתה למדרגה חדשה, וזאת המדרגה היתה תלויה בארץ ישראל אשר שם נולדו רובי ההלכות והמדרשים. וכמו כן התקנות והגזירות נסבו ממאורעות אשר אירעו שם ומצרכי הזמן בארץ מושבותם. ונוסף על זה הלא כל ימי הבית לא היה אדם מישראל אשר לא החזיק באמונה שלמה בהעיקר הגדול "כי מציון תצא תורה" וכל דבר הלכה או מדרש או סייג אשר לא נמנו עליו וגמרוהו בבית דין הגדול שבלשכת הגזית לא היה להם כח המאסר; ועל כן מובן מעצמו כי בבבל לא היה מקום דרישת התורה ולא היה יכול להיות. וגם לא הגיע אלינו דבר גדול או קטן מדרישת התורה ליהודים הבבליים רק הודע לנו שנהגו במנהגי היהודים ומדקדקים מאד בשמירת השבת ושולחים את שקליהם ואת קרבנותיהם ומתת ידם לירושלים, אבל מהתפשטות תלמוד תורה ביניהם אין רמז מאומה. רמז לדבר, כי דרישת התורה בבבל בעת ההיא אך מעטה היתה, יורה לנו הענין המסופר מאיזטי (זוטוס במ"ר) מלך האדיאביני שנכנס עם אמו לדת היהודים ושלח את בניו לירושלים ללמוד תורה (יוסיפון קדמוניות ס"כ), אף שבבל קרובה אליו. ומהמשך הספור ההוא נראה עוד הבדל אחר בין דעות הירושלמיים והבבליים. כי יסופר עוד שם שרבו של איזאטי אשר למדהו את תורת בתורת היהודים בלב שלם. לא כן חשב חכם יהודי אחר מבני ארץ הגליל הנקרא בשמו אלעזר כי הוא אמר למלך כי לא יספיק לדעת את דרכי אלהים ואת תורותיו אבל יצטרך לשמור את מצותיו והמילה היא חובה אשר בשום ענין אין להפטר ממנה. ומזה נראה כי הבבלי לא שם את המעשה עיקר וודאי שהבבליים לא היו מדקדקים בדקדוקי מצות המעשיים כבני ארץ ישראל, וגם זה מופת קצת כי בבבל לא דקדקו בדקדוקי התורה והסופרים כהירושלמיים. ומי יודע אם The days of Hillel's stay in the Land of Israel, while he was serving his masters, were bad; there he lived a life of distress and sustained his soul by the labor of his hands. He was a day laborer and the wage of his work was small; but since he was modest in all his wants, this little sufficed him for his needs and more. From the surplus he paid the doorkeeper of the house of study of Shemaiah and Avtalyon half the tropaic [coin] that was due to him according to the custom in force. One day Hillel earned nothing and could not pay his due; but this did not keep him from hearing Torah, for instead of going inside into the house of study, he went and lay down at the opening of the skylight to hear from there the teaching of his masters. That night much snow fell to the ground, until all his body was covered to a height of three cubits. In the morning the day did not grow light as before, and all who sat in the house of study wondered; the matter was sought and found, and behold, the body of a man lay at the opening of the skylight, covered with snow. They quickly removed him from there, and behold, it was Hillel. They washed him, anointed him and sat him opposite the fire, and said: this one is worthy that the Sabbath be profaned for him (Yoma 35b). So strong was the love of the Torah within him. But as he loved the wisdom of the Torah, so he loved all the other sciences and inquired into them. They said of him that he left no matter of the words of wisdom that he did not learn: he studied foreign languages and all kinds of sciences; all of it he learned in order to magnify the Torah and make it glorious [Isaiah 42:21] (Soferim ch. 16). In this way too he guided his disciples, and taught them to hold fast to the Torah and not to let their hands go from the other sciences (Sukkah 28a; Bava Batra 139a ⚠2). His love for the exposition of the Torah is seen especially from his leaving the land of his birth and going to a land that at that time was full of strife and horror. But he saw that in Babylonia he could not fulfill his longing; and even if he expounded by himself or in the company of sages in his country, he knew well that, according to the state of the wisdom of the Torah at that time in the Land of Israel, from which instruction goes forth, he could not attain what he sought. For if he expounded and drew halakhic consequences from his expositions, he still did not know whether his exposition agreed with the halakhah that was the practice of the people and had been agreed upon in the Great Court in Jerusalem. And this is the thing that moved him to go up to the Land of Israel. Indeed it is told that Hillel went up from Babylonia on account of three matters. The first was the matter of the purity and impurity of the leper (Sifra Tazria ch. 10). The second was the eating of matzah from the new grain. And the third was the festival offering (Yerushalmi Pesaḥim 6:1). In these three matters the halakhah was in doubt for him, and there was no man in Babylonia who could resolve his doubts, for they did not know how these had been decided by the court in the Chamber of Hewn Stone and how the matters were practiced in the custom of the people in Jerusalem. And if they said that he went up on account of three matters, without doubt this is not exact; rather he had doubts already before this, but he still delayed leaving his country and his birthplace until doubts came to him in these three matters; then his soul was strengthened to go up to Jerusalem, and he went up. Nor was his hope disappointed, for in truth he expounded, reached agreement, went up and received the halakhah (Yerushalmi there). The same happened to other sages who lived in Babylonia: they expounded according to the ways of analogy and thought that their midrash was founded on foundations of truth which all the winds in the world could not move from their place, and nonetheless they were forced to set aside their opinion and to overturn their analogy and their midrash, because it had not been so decided in the Great Court in Jerusalem, and the halakhah was the opposite of what they had derived by their midrash. So it happened at a later time, close to the destruction of the Temple or right after it, to one of the sages who went down to Nisibis, namely R. Judah ben Betera. This sage too derived a midrashic consequence and set up the halakhah that a betrothed daughter of Israel eats terumah, and he proved this by the rule of kal va-ḥomer. Then a sage, one of his friends, named Yoḥanan ben Bag Bag, sent to him as if in wonder, saying: “They said in your name that a betrothed daughter of Israel eats terumah!” He sent back and said to him: “I held you to be expert in the secrets of the Torah; you do not know even how to expound a kal va-ḥomer!” And he expounded a kal va-ḥomer before him, but he added, saying: “And what shall I do, when they said: a woman never eats terumah until she enters the bridal canopy, and they supported it with the verse ‘every one that is clean in thy house shall eat of it’ [Numbers 18:11]” (Yerushalmi and Tosefta Ketubbot ch. 5; and see Kiddushin 10b; Sifrei Koraḥ sec. 117). Thus we see that what happened to Hillel happened to him, except that Hillel expounded, reached agreement and received the halakhah, while he expounded and derived a consequence from his midrash, and although he had no reason to overturn his midrash on its scholarly side, he was forced to overturn it because the custom in the Land of Israel in his time was the opposite of the consequence of his midrash. For in truth the ancient halakhah agreed with his midrash, for at first they said that a betrothed daughter of Israel eats terumah, and they derived this from Scripture, and a later court decided the opposite and derived the opposite also from Scripture (Yerushalmi there). Therefore it is self-evident that the midrash of R. Judah ben Betera too could not stand against the decision of the court in the Land of Israel.2 And now we understand how hard it was for the expounders in Babylonia to decide a matter as halakhah and for practice, which has no standing if there is no agreement on it from the court in Jerusalem; therefore it is not surprising that Hillel chose to live in Jerusalem to learn Torah from Shemaiah and Avtalyon. We cannot fix with certainty the days of his service to his masters, but it appears that he returned once more to his country: the first time he went up to Jerusalem to satisfy his longing to learn Torah from Shemaiah and Avtalyon, and then he did not go up at the request of the sages of the Land of Israel, by their wish to ask halakhah of him (commentary of Rabad to the Sifra, at its beginning); rather he received halakhah from them, not they from him. After a time, and this without doubt at the time when Herod persecuted the sages, he returned to his country; but when the land was quiet he came back once more to Jerusalem, and this return was before the hallowing of the Feast of Unleavened Bread [Isaiah 30:29]. When all Israel gathered in Jerusalem to sacrifice the Passover sacrifice, Hillel too was among those who came.

ימי שבתו של הלל בארץ ישראל בעודו משמש את רבותיו היו רעים, שם חי חיי צר והחיה את נפשו מיגיע כפיו. הוא היה שכיר יום ומשכרת עבודתו מצערה, אך בהיותו ענו בכל משאליו על כן הספיק לו זה המעט די מחסורו והותר. מן המותר שלם לשומר הפתח אשר בבית מדרשם של שמעיה ואבטליון את חצי הטרפעיק המגיע לו לפי החק הנהוג. ויהי היום והלל לא השכיר מאומה ולא יכול לשלם חקו אבל זה לא עצרהו משמוע תורה כי תחת אשר נכנס פנימה לבית המדרש הלך וישכב לו על פי הארובה לשמוע משם את תלמודם של רבותיו; ויהי בלילה והנה נפל שלג רב ארצה עד כי נכסה כל גופו ברום שלש אמות ויהי בבקר והנה לא האיר היום כתמול שלשום, ויתמהו כל יושבי בית המדרש ויבוקש הדבר וימצא והנה גופת איש שוכב על פי הארובה ומכוסה בשלג. מהר פרקוהו משם והנה הוא הלל. רחצוהו סכוהו והושיבוהו כנגד המדורה, ואמרו ראוי הוא זה לחלל עליו את השבת (יומא ל"ה:). כן עצמה בקרבו האהבה אל התורה, אך כאשר אהב את חכמת התורה כמו כן אהב את שאר כל החכמות ודרש בהן. ואמרו עליו שלא הניח מדברי החכמה דבר אשר לא למד. דרש בלשונות זרות, וכל מיני חכמות את הכל למד להגדיל תורה ולהאדיר (סופרים פט"ז). גם בדרך הזה נהג את תלמידיו ולמד אותם לאחוז בתורה ולבלתי הנח ידם משאר חכמות (סוכה כ"ח. ב"ב קל"ט.). אהבתו לדרישת התורה ניכרת ביחוד ממה שעזב את ארץ מולדתו וילך לו אל ארץ אשר בעת ההיא היתה מלאה מריבה ושערורה. אבל ראה כי בבבל לא יכול למלאות את תשוקתו, ואם גם כי היה דורש לעצמו או בחברת חכמים אשר בארצו, בכל זאת ידע מאד כי לפי מצב חכמת התורה בעת ההיא בארץ ישראל אשר משם ההוראה יוצאת לא יתכן שישיג מבוקשו. כי אם היה דורש והוציא תולדות הלכותיות מדרישותיו עוד לא ידע אם דרישתו מתאימה עם ההלכה אשר היתה לפעולת אדם בעם והוסכמה בבית דין הגדול שבירושלים. ועתה זה הדבר אשר הניעהו לעלות לארץ ישראל. ואף יסופר כן שעל שלשה דברים עלה הלל מבבל. האחד היה בענין טהרת וטומאת המצורע (ת"כ תזריע פ"י). השני בענין אכילת מצה מן החדש. והשלישי בענין קרבן חגיגה (ירושלמי פסחים פ"ו ה"א) ובשלשת הדברים האלה היתה ההלכה מסופקת בידו ולא היה איש בבבל אשר היה יכול להתיר ספקותיו כי לא ידעו איך הוכרעו מפי הבית דין אשר בלשכת הגזית ואיך נוהגים הדברים במנהג העם בירושלים. ואם אמרו שעל שלשה דברים עלה, בלי ספק אין זה בדקדוק אמנם כבר לפני זה היו לו ספקות אבל התרשל עוד לעזוב ארצו ומולדתו עד שאירעו לו ספקות בשלשת הדברים p. 149 האלה אז היתה נפשו מתאמצת לעלות ירושלימה ויעל. וגם לא היתה תוחלתו נכזבה כי באמת דרש והסכים ועלה וקבל הלכה (ירושלמי שם). כענין הזה אירע גם לחכמים אחרים אשר ישבו בבבל כי דרשו לפי דרכי ההיקש וחשבו כי מדרשם נוסד על אדני האמת אשר כל הרוחות שבעולם לא יזיזוהו ממקומו ובכל זאת הוכרחו לבטל דעתם ולסתור היקשם ומדרשם מפני שלא הוכרע כן בבית דין הגדול שבירושלים וההלכה כהיפוך ממה שהוציאו הם על פי מדרשם. כן קרה בזמן מאוחר קרוב לחורבן הבית או מיד לאחריו לאחד מן החכמים אשר ירד לנציבין, והוא רבי יהודה בן בתירה. זה החכם הוציא גם כן תולדה מדרשית והעמיד הלכה שארוסה בת ישראל אוכלת בתרומה והוכיח כן על פי מדת קל וחומר. והנה חכם אחד מאוהביו ויוחנן בן בג בג שמו שלח אליו כמתמיה לאמר: אמרו משמך שארוסה בת ישראל אוכלת בתרומה! והחזירו ואמר לו: מוחזק הייתי בך שאתה בקי בסתרי תורה אפילו לדרוש בק"ו אין אתה יודע! ודרש לפניו ק"ו, אבל הוסיף לאמר: ומה אעשה והם אמרו לעולם אין האשה אוכלת בתרומה עד שתכנס לחופה וסמכו להם מקרא כל טהור בביתך יאכלנו (ירושלמי ותוספתא כתובות פ"ה ועי' קידושין י': ספרי קרח פי' קי"ז). והנה אנו רואים שאירע לו מה שאירע להלל אלא שהלל דרש והסכים וקבל הלכה, והוא דרש והוציא תולדה ממדרש ואף שלא היה לו סבה לסתור מדרשו מצדו הלימודי הוכרח לסתור אותו מפני שהמנהג בארץ ישראל היה בזמנו בהפך מתולדת מדרשו. כי באמת היתה ההלכה הקדומה מתאימה עם מדרשו כי בראשונה אמרו ארוסה בת ישראל אוכלת בתרומה והוציאו כן מן המקרא ובית דין של אחרון החליטו בהפך והוציאו ההפוך גם כן מן המקרא (ירושלמי שם) ועל כן מובן מעצמו כי גם מדרשו של רבי יהודה ב"ב לא היה יכול לעמוד נגד החלטת בית דין שבא"י2. ועתה נבין כמה קשה היה לדורשים שבבבל להחליט דבר להלכה ולמעשה אשר אין לו מעמד אם אין הסכמה עליו מבית דין שבירושלים, ועל כן לא יפלא אשר הלל בחר לשבת בירושלים ללמוד תורה משמעיה ואבטליון. לא נוכל לצמצם בבירור ימי שמושו את רבותיו, אבל נראה כי שב עוד הפעם אל ארצו, שבראשונה עלה לירושלים להשקיט תשוקתו ללמוד תורה משמעיה ואבטליון, ואז לא לבקשת חכמי ארץ ישראל מרצותם לשאול ממנו הלכה עלה (פי' הראב"ד לת"כ בתחלתו) אמנם הוא קבל הלכה מהם לא הם ממנו, ואחר זמן, וזה בלי ספק בעת שרדף הירודוס את החכמים, שב אל ארצו, אך כאשר שקטה הארץ חזר עוד הפעם לירושלים, וזאת החזרה היתה לפני התקדש חג המצות. מדי התאספו כל ישראל ירושלימה לזבוח את זבח חג הפסח, אז גם הלל היה בתוך הבאים.

In that year the fourteenth of the first month fell on the Sabbath. And the Benei Bathyra, who were the heads of the Sanhedrin, did not know whether the Passover offering overrides the Sabbath or not. One of the disciples rose and said: There is a man who came up from Babylonia, Hillel the Babylonian is his name; he served Shemaiah and Avtalyon, and he knows whether the Passover offering overrides the Sabbath. But the Benei Bathyra shook their heads, saying: Is it possible that there is any hope from a Babylonian? Nevertheless they sent to call him. When he came they said to him: Have you ever heard, when the fourteenth falls on the Sabbath, whether it overrides the Sabbath or not? He said to them: Have we then only one Passover offering that overrides the Sabbath? Do we not have many offerings like it, and they are offered every single Sabbath? And he expounded to them from a hekesh, from a kal va-ḥomer and from a gezerah shavah. But against all his proofs they had only one answer, saying: We have already said, is there any hope from a Babylonian? And although he sat and expounded before them the whole day, they did not accept it from him until he said: May [evil] come upon me — thus I heard from Shemaiah and Avtalyon. As soon as they heard this from him their mind was at rest, and they appointed him Nasi over them (Yerushalmi Pesaḥim 6:1, and see the Bavli there). Now it appears from this that the Benei Bathyra did not go in the ways of Shemaiah and Avtalyon in the exposition of the Torah; they did not want to rely on the decision of reason, and their mind was not at rest with midrashic proofs according to the middot; only then did they listen to his words, when he told them that it was so received from the mouth of Shemaiah and Avtalyon. And this is proof that even among the Pharisees not all opinions were the same, and the new paths that Hillel's teachers had paved in the exposition of the Torah found their opponents. But this too emerges for us from this event: many recognized that the turning away of the opponents from the teaching of Shemaiah and Avtalyon had caused the Torah to be forgotten, and without doubt they were anxious for the future, that the lack of knowledge would grow still stronger; for only if matters were so can we understand how they at once voted and resolved to remove the Benei Bathyra from the office of Nasi and set up Hillel in their place. And perhaps the Benei Bathyra themselves recognized that their opposition had no standing, and made room for a man better than they. It is very likely that the Benei Bathyra were not removed from the office of Nasi, but gave up the place to Hillel willingly and with a desiring soul; at any rate such was the tradition among the people, and so it follows from the words of Rabbi, who said of himself: Whatever anyone tells me, I will do for him, except what the Benei Bathyra did for the elder Hillel, who removed themselves from the office of Nasi and seated Hillel in their place. However that may be, it is clear that Hillel's elevation to the office of Nasi was the cause that the exposition of the Torah broadened and reached a new level. On that occasion they asked him one more question, which depended on the first. They said to him: What is to be done for the people who did not bring their knives? He said to them: This halakhah I heard and forgot; but leave Israel alone — if they are not prophets, they are the sons of prophets. On the morrow they brought their offerings, and the knives were stuck in the wool of the lambs or tied to the horns of the kids. When he saw the deed he remembered the halakhah and said: Thus I heard from Shemaiah and Avtalyon. From this we learn a great foundation among the principles of the Oral Torah. For this thing that Hillel did on that occasion, that he kept silent to know what the people would do and relied on their custom, is the thing that from of old was before the eyes of the sages of the Torah. How many statutes, laws and halakhot whose root we do not know — and already the ancients did not know it — became known to them from the custom of the people! And this was a rule for the sages: Every halakhah that is weak in the hand of the court, and you do not know its nature, go out and see how the community conducts itself, and conduct yourself so (Yerushalmi 7:6) ⚠3.3 Yet even more than we recognize today from the story of that event that the state of exposition was very bad, Hillel himself recognized it, for he saw the matters with his own eyes. Therefore he rebuked them to their faces, saying: Who caused you to need this Babylonian? Was it not that you did not serve the two great men of the world, Shemaiah and Avtalyon, who sat among you? And it is told explicitly that he rebuked them with this open reproof after they had already appointed him Nasi over them; for after he had been raised on high he saw in this a duty upon himself to explain to them the way in which he would go, and to reveal to them their blemish and their failings. For the man who is called to heal the ills of a congregation must also reveal what the disease is, so that its signs may be recognized. Therefore, although by the character of his soul he was a humble and lowly man, on that occasion he could not restrain himself, and he rebuked them with hard words for their lack of knowledge and for the causes that brought about this lack. And who knows whether at that time, when he spoke judgments with them and took the stumbling-block under his hand [cf. Isaiah 3:6], he did not call to mind the saying that was habitually on his tongue: In a place where there is no man, strive to be a man (Avot 2:3).4 For in truth those days were a time that lacked the right man to make the Torah great and glorious [cf. Isaiah 42:21].

בשנה ההיא חל ארבעה עשר לחדש הראשון להיות בשבת. ובני בתירה אשר היו ראשי הסנהדרין לא היו יודעים אם הפסח דוחה את השבת אם לא. ויקם אחד מן התלמידים ויאמר אדם אחד עלה מבבל והלל הבבלי שמו והוא שמש את שמעיה ואבטליון ויודע אם p. 150 הפסח דוחה את השבת. אך בני בתירה הניעו ראש לאמר אפשר שיש תוחלת מבבלי? אך בכל זאת שלחו לקרא לו. ויהי כבאו אמרו לו שמעת מימיך כשחל ארבעה עשר להיות בשבת אם דוחה את השבת אם לא? אמר להם: וכי אין לנו אלא פסח אחד בלבד דוחה את השבת והלא הרבה קרבנות כיוצא בו יש לנו והם קרבים בכל שבת ושבת, ודרש להם מהיקש מק"ו ומגזירה שוה, אמנם נגד כל ראיותיו לא היה להם רק תשובה אחת באמרם: כבר אמרנו אם יש תוחלת מבבלי, ואעפ"י שהיה יושב ודורש לפניהם כל היום כלו לא קבלו ממנו עד שאמר יבוא עלי כך שמעתי משמעיה ואבטליון, כיון ששמעו ממנו כן נחה דעתם ומנו אותו נשיא עליהם (ירושלמי פסחים פ"ו ה"א ועי' בבלי שם). והנה נראה מזה כי בני בתירה לא הלכו בדרכי שמעיה ואבטליון במדרש התורה, ולא רצו לסמוך על הכרעת השכל, ולא נחה דעתם בראיות מדרשיות על פי המדות ורק אז שמעו לדבריו כשאמר להם כי כן מקובל מפי שמעיה ואבטליון, וזה הוכחה כי גם בקרב הפרושים לא היו כל הדעות שוות, והמסילות החדשות אשר סללו רבותיו של הלל במדרש התורה מצאו מנגדיהם. אבל גם זה יוצא לנו מתוך המאורע הזה כי רבים הכירו כי נטיית המתנגדים מתורתם של שמעיה ואבטליון גרמה שכחת התורה ובלי ספק היו דואגים לעתיד שחסרון הדעת עוד יתגבר, כי רק אם היו הדברים כן נבין איך מיד נמנו וגמרו להעביר את בני בתירה מנשיאות והעמידו את הלל במקומם, ואולי בני בתירה עצמם הכירו כי אין להתנגדותם מעמד ופנו מקום לאיש הטוב מהם, וקרוב הדבר מאד כי בני בתירה לא הועברו מן הנשיאות כי אם ברצון ובנפש חפצה פנו המקום מפני הלל, ועל כל פנים היתה כן ההגדה בעם, וכן יוצא מדברי רבי אשר אמר על עצמו כל מה שיאמר לי אדם אני עושה לו חוץ ממה שעשו בני בתירה לזקני הלל שסלקו עצמם מן הנשיאות והושיבו את הלל במקומם. איך שיהיה ברור הוא כי התנשאותו של הלל לנשיאות היתה סבה כי התרחבה דרישת התורה ובאה למדרגה חדשה. במעמד ההוא שאלוהו עוד שאלה אחרת התלויה בראשונה. אמרו לו: מה לעשות לעם שלא הביאו סכיניהם? אמר להם הלכה זו שמעתי ושכחתי אלא הניחו לישראל אם אינן נביאים בני נביאים הם. למחר הביאו קרבנותיהם והסכינים היו תחובים בגיות הכבשים, או קשורים בקרני הגדיים כיון שראה המעשה נזכר ההלכה ואמר כך שמעתי משמעיה ואבטליון. ומזה למדנו יסוד גדול בעיקרי התורה שבעל פה. כי זה הדבר אשר עשה הלל במעמד ההוא אשר החריש לדעת מה יעשה העם ויסמוך על מנהגם, הוא הדבר אשר היה מאז ומקדם, לחכמי התורה לעינים. וכמה חקים ודינים והלכות אשר לא נדע שרשם וכבר הקדמונים לא ידעוהו הודע להם ממנהג העם. והיה זה חק לחכמים שכל הלכה שהיא רופפת ביד בית דין ואין אתה יודע מה טיבה צא וראה איך הצבור נוהגים ונהוג (ירושלמי פ"ז ה"ו)3. אמנם יותר מאשר אנו מכירים היום מתוך סיפור המאורע ההוא כי מצב הדרישה היה ברע מאד, הלא הכיר הלל עצמו אשר ראה הדברים בעיניו. אשר על כן הוכיחם על פניהם לאמר: מי גרם לכם לצרך לבבלי הזה לא על שלא שמשתם לשני גדולי עולם לשמעיה ואבטליון שהיו יושבים אצליכם? ויסופר בפירוש כי התוכחה המגולה הזאת הוכיח אותם אחרי אשר כבר מנוהו נשיא עליהם כי אחרי אשר הוקם על ראה בזה חובה לעצמו לפרש להם את דרכו אשר ילך בה, ולגלות להם מומם וחסרוניהם. כי האיש אשר נקרא לרפאות תחלואי עדה אף עליו לגלות המחלה מה היא? למען ינוכרו אותותיה. ולכן אם גם מצד תכונת נפשו היה איש ענו ושפל רוח בכל זה בפעם ההוא לא יכול להתאפק, והוכיחם בדברים קשים על חסרון ידיעתם ועל הסבות אשר גרמו החסרון הזה. ומי יודע אם לא בעת ההיא כאשר דבר אתם משפטים ולקח המכשלה תחת ידו, אם לא העלה על זכרונו את המאמר p. 151 אשר היה רגיל על לשונו: במקום שאין איש השתדל להיות איש (אבות פ"ב מ"ג)4. כי באמת הימים ההם היו זמן אשר חסר להם האיש הנכון להגדיל תורה ולהאדיר.

All the later generations extolled his eminence. They said of him that he in his generation was like Ezra in his generation; both of them were founders of the wisdom of the Torah (Sukkah 20a). As he was wondrous in his wisdom, so was he in his virtues. He is the first of the sages of the Mishnah who said outright that the chief purpose of religion is the keeping of the duties between a man and his fellow, and that the keeping of the practical commandments is the means to attain this purpose. Therefore he set up the love of man for man as the chief principle in the religion of the Torah. Once a gentile came before him and asked to become a Jew. But this gentile knew that the statutes of the Jewish religion were very many, and it would be hard for him to learn the law of this religion in all its matters; therefore he asked to learn the foundation of the teaching of the Jews, and he asked Hillel to teach him this foundation within a short time. Hillel received him with a friendly face and willingly, and said: Let it be as you say! The essential foundation of the religion of the Jews is: What is hateful to you, do not do to your fellow; and the rest is its commentary — go and learn (Shabbat 31a). This spirit of love shone forth in all his teachings. If he speaks of a man's judgment on the deeds of another, the love of man was his measure, when he warned that a man should not judge his fellow until he reaches his place (Avot ch. 1) ⚠4. In his dealings with people his speech was gentle, and he bore and endured their trouble and knew no anger (Shabbat ibid.); for this love was a lamp to his feet in all his relations with others. And especially he kept the duty of love toward the needy among men. In supporting the hand of the poor he was not content to give him only his necessary wants, but gave him also luxuries if he had been accustomed to them from his youth (Ketubot 67b; Yerushalmi Pe'ah ch. 1). A sister to this virtue was his humility. In this virtue he was a model among his people, until it became a proverb: A man should always be humble like Hillel (Shabbat 30b). Likewise he was glorious in the virtue of patience. Various aggadot about his patience spread among the people. In all that people troubled him, out of folly or out of malice, they could not make him lose his calm of spirit and his quiet (ibid. 31a). On a level above all this, he kept his humility before God, through which his trust in God was always strengthened. He carried one saying on his lips, and it testifies to the strength of his trust; he used to say: Blessed be the Lord day by day! [Psalms 68:20] If he had today enough for his needs, he did not worry for the morrow, for he trusted in God (Beitzah 16a). Once he was coming on the road and heard a sound of outcry in the city, and he said: I am sure that this is not in my house; for he was not afraid of evil tidings, but his heart was steadfast, trusting in the Lord [Psalms 112:7] (Berakhot 60a). As his trust in God, so his fear of Him was great and strong, and borne on the wings of perfect love. Therefore it was despised in his eyes, loathsome, to make the rejoicing before the Lord like the rejoicing of a man when he is merry in the company of friends. But he held it as a rule that in our rejoicing before God no recklessness or levity should mix in, but the joy should be with trembling [cf. Psalms 2:11] and with holy feeling (Yerushalmi Sukkah 5:4). In the moral parables by which he taught the virtues of goodness and righteousness, he always used fine and wondrous images that engrave themselves in the soul of the hearer. When he speaks of the covenant of love between the Lord and Israel, he pictures it in a lovely image, saying: To the place that my heart loves, my feet lead me; if you come to my house, I will come to your house; if you do not come to my house, I will not come to your house (Tosefta Sukkah ch. 4 and the Bavli there). And so he pictures beautifully the divine justice. Once he saw a skull floating on the face of the water, and said: Because you drowned others, they drowned you; and in the end those who drowned you will be drowned (Avot ch. 1) ⚠5. And perhaps in saying so he remembered the events that happened day by day in his time under the rule of Herod, in which bloodshed was a daily deed. In almost all the moral sayings we have from him we find that he chose a short and puzzling figure of speech, really close to a riddle. Once, when he was taking leave of his disciples, they asked him where he was going. He answered and said: To do kindness with the guest that I have inside my house. And when he spoke to them so day after day, they wondered, saying: Do you then have a guest in your house every single day? He answered: Indeed it is so! Is not the poor soul like a guest inside my body? Today it is here and tomorrow it has passed away from here (Leviticus Rabbah ch. 34). And what was this kindness that he did with his soul? It was the effort to perfect it with the greatest possible perfection. The idea that lies in these words of his he described elsewhere in a pleasant figure, in which he tells a man his duty to care for his soul, to do kindness with it and perfect it; for if he does not do it for himself, who will do it for him? For even a man's own effort is slack, and all the more the effort of others. And if he himself is slothful to act, time will pass, and who knows whether his strength tomorrow will be as his strength today. But he describes this in a short figure like a riddle, saying: If I am not for myself, who is for me? And when I am for myself, what am I? And if not now, when? (Avot ibid.) This last saying finds its counterpart in another saying ascribed to Hillel, namely: Do not say, When I have leisure I will study — perhaps you will not have leisure (ibid. ch. 2). And as he figured the duty of a man toward his soul, so too he pictures his duty toward his body, saying that it is a man's duty not to neglect the welfare of his body, and to strive to keep it whole and strong. For the Creator of man, who gave him the body, wants us to be its guardians, to attend to it at all times and to keep it. This idea too he clothed in a short answer to his disciples. When he took leave of them, his disciples said to him: Where are you going? He answered and said: To do a commandment. They asked him: And what commandment is this? He said to them: To bathe in the bathhouse. They said to him: Is this then a commandment? And he said to them: Yes! Man, who was created in the image of God — how shall he not keep this image in cleanliness and purity? (Leviticus Rabbah ibid.) But because of this let us not think that he loved the pleasures of men; have we not already seen that he accustomed himself from his youth to the virtue of contentment, and warned very greatly against the lust of the flesh and against striving to multiply possessions, wives, menservants and maidservants (Avot ch. 2).

בכל הדורות האחרונים הפליגו מעלתו. אמרו עליו כי הוא בדורו כעזרא בדורו שניהם היו מיסדי חכמת התורה (סוכה כ'.). כאשר היה נפלא בחכמתו כן היה במדותיו. הוא הראשון מחכמי המשנה אשר אמר בפה מלא, כי עיקר תכלית הדת הוא שמירת החובות בין אדם לחבירו, ושמירת המצוות המעשיות היא האמצעי להשגת זאת התכלית. ועל כן העמיד את אהבת אדם לאדם לעיקר הראשי בדת התורה. פעם אחת בא לפניו נכרי ובקש להתיהד. אך הנכרי הזה ידע כי רבו מאד החקים אשר לדת היהודית ויקשה ממנו ללמוד את משפט הדת הזאת בכל עניניה, על כן בקש ללמוד את יסוד תורת היהודים, וישאל מאת הלל שיורהו את היסוד הזה במשך זמן קצר, הלל קבלהו בסבר פנים יפות וברצון ויאמר: לו יהי כדבריך! היסוד העקרי לדת היהודים הוא: מה דסני לך לחברך לא תעביד, ואידך פירושא הוא זיל גמור (שבת ל"א.). רוח האהבה הזאת נוססה בכל לימודיו, אם ידבר ממשפט האדם על מעשה זולתו היתה אהבת האדם מדתו בהזהירו שלא ידין האדם את חברו עד שיגיע למקומו (אבות פ"א). במשאו ומתנו עם בני אדם היה דבורו בנחת ונשא וסבל טרחם ולא ידע רגז (שבת שם) כי זאת האהבה היתה נר לרגלו בכל ייחוסיו לזולתו, וביחוד שמר חובת האהבה נגד אביוני אדם. בתמכו את יד העני לא הספיק לנפשו לתת לו רק מחסוריו ההכרחיים אך נתן לו גם מותרות אם הורגל בהן מנעוריו. (כתובות ס"ז: ירושלמי פאה פ"א). אחות למדה הזאת היתה מדת ענותו. במדה הזאת היה למופת בעמו עד שהיה למשל לעולם יהא אדם ענותן כהלל (שבת ל':). וכמו כן היה נאדר במדת הסבלנות. הגדות שונות נתפשטו בעם מסבלנותו. בכל מה שהטריחוהו בני אדם מסכלות או מרוע לב לא יכלו להאביד ממנו שאננות רוחו והשקטתו (שם ל"א.). במדרגה למעלה מכל זה שמר ענותו לפני אלהים אשר בה התחזק בטחונו באל תמיד. מאמר אחד נשא על שפתיו והוא יעיד על עז בטחונו, הוא היה אומר: ברוך ה' יום יום! ואם היה לו היום די מחסורו לא דאג ליום מחר כי באל בטוח (ביצה ט"ז.). ופעם אחת היה בא בדרך ושמע קול צוחה בעיר ויאמר מובטח אני כי אין זה בתוך ביתי כי לא ירא משמועה רעה אך נכון לבו ובטוח בה' (ברכות ס'.). כבטחונו באלהים כן היתה יראתו אותו גדולה וחזקה, ונשואה על כנפי אהבה תמימה. ועל כן נבזה בעיניו נמאס לעשות השמחה לפני ה' כשמחת האדם בשמחו בחברת רעים. אך היה לו לחק כי בשמחתנו לפני אלהים לא תתערב פחזות או קלות ראש אך השמחה תהיה ברעדה וברגש קדש (ירושלמי סוכה פ"ה מ"ד). במשלי מוסר אשר הורה לדעת מדות הטוב והצדק, השתמש תמיד בציורים יפים ונפלאים המתחקים בנפש השומע. בדברו מברית האהבה בין ה' ובין ישראל יצייר זה בציור נחמד באמרו: במקום שלבי אוהב רגלי מוליכות אותי אם אתה תבא לביתי אני אבא לביתך אם אתה לא תבא לביתי אני לא אבא לביתך (תוספתא סוכה פ"ד ובבלי שם). וכן יצייר יפה הצדק האלוה. פעם אחת ראה גלגלת צפה על פני המים ואמר: על דאטפת אטפוך וסוף מטיפיך יטופון (אבות פ"א). ואולי בדברו כן זכר את המאורעות אשר אירעו יום יום בזמנו תחת ממשלת p. 152 הירודוס אשר היה שפיכות דם אדם מעשה בכל יום. כמעט בכל דבורי מוסר השכל אשר יש לנו ממנו נמצא כי בחר במליצה קצרה ומתמיהה וקרובה ממש לחדה. פעם אחת בהפטרו מתלמידיו שאלוהו לאן הוא הולך? ויען ויאמר: לגמול חסד עם האכסניא אשר יש לי בתוך ביתי. ויהי כדברו אליהם כן יום יום, היו מתמיהים לאמר: וכי יש לך בכל יום ויום אכסניא בתוך ביתך? ויען: אמנם כן הוא! וכי הנפש העלובה איננה דומה לאכסניא בתוך גופי, היום היא פה ולמחר חלפה לה מזה (ויקרא רבה פל"ד). ומה היה גמילות חסדו זאת אשר גמל עם נפשו? זה היה ההשתדלות להשלים אותה בשלמות האפשרית, אך הרעיון המונח בדבריו האלה תאר במקום אחר במליצה נעימה אשר בה יגיד לאדם חובתו להיות דואג לנפשו לעשות עמה חסד להשלימנה שאם הוא לא יעשה לעצמו מי יעשה בעבורו? כי אף השתדלות עצמו רפויה וכל שכן השתדלות אחרים. ואם יתרשל הוא עצמו לעשות יעבור הזמן ומי יודע אם יהיה כחו מחר ככחו היום. אבל יתאר זה במליצה קצרה דומה לחדה. באמרו: אם אין אני לי מי לי? וכשאני לעצמי מה אני? ואם לא עכשו אימתי? (אבות שם) והמאמר האחרון הזה ימצא דוגמתו במאמר אחר מיוחס להלל. והוא: אל תאמר כשאפנה אשנה שמא לא תפנה? (שם פ"ב). וכאשר המליץ חובת האדם לנפשו אף כן יצייר את חובתו נגד גופו. באמרו כי חובה לאדם שלא יטוש את טובת גופו וישתדל להשלים אותו ולהחזיקו. כי יוצר האדם אשר נתן לו את הגויה ירצה כי נהיה אומנים אותה לפקדה בכל עת ולשמרה. גם הרעיון הזה הלביש בתשובה קצרה לתלמידיו. בהפטרו מהם אמרו לו תלמידיו לאן אתה הולך? ויען ויאמר לעשות מצוה. וישאלוהו, וכי מה מצוה היא זו? אמר להם לרחוץ בבית המרחץ. אמרו לו: וכי זו מצוה היא? ואמר להם: הן! האדם אשר נברא בצלם אלהים איך לא ישמור את הצלם הזה בנקיות ובטהרה? (ויקרא רבה שם). אבל בעבור זה לא נחשוב כי אהב תענוגות בני אדם, הלא כבר ראינו כי הרגיל נפשו מנעוריו במדת ההסתפקות, ומאד מאד הזהיר מתשוקת בשרים ומההשתדלות להרבות נכסים ונשים ועבדים ושפחות (אבות פ"ב).

But the chief principle that he set before himself as a goal was the spreading of the Torah. Therefore he said: He who does not learn Torah is not worthy to live — he who does not increase, decreases, and he who does not learn deserves death (ibid.). In this he teaches that the face of the one who studies the Torah should be turned toward two aims. The first: that he should not say, I have learned much and it is enough for me; rather he should not weary of meditating in it, for its measure is longer than the earth [Job 11:9]; for one who stops from the Torah at any time is like one who stops from life, since it is our life and the length of our days. The second: that one who occupies himself with the Torah has not yet fulfilled his duty if he studies continually; rather he is obliged to teach Torah to others as well, and one who does not do so has transgressed a statute and missed the aim of his life. For the Torah and knowledge lead to fear [of Heaven], and without knowledge there is no fear of Heaven and no piety: for an ignorant man does not fear sin, and an am ha-aretz is not pious (ibid.). But he warned against doing all these not for the sake of Heaven, and said: He who makes use of the crown passes away; he who spreads abroad his name loses his name (ibid. ch. 1). Out of his desire that knowledge increase and the Torah grow great, he roused those who knew to spread it abroad and scatter it, and his word to them was always: At the time of the gatherers, scatter; and at the time of the scatterers, gather (Berakhot 63a, and Yerushalmi and Tosefta there). And who will not find in this a counterpart to his saying: In a place where there is no man, strive to be a man (Avot)? Yet he thought that one must also pay attention to the state of the times, for not all times are equal for spreading the Torah. Therefore he said: If you see that the Torah is dear to the generation, scatter; and if you see a generation to which the Torah is not dear, gather (ibid.). One who considers this saying attentively will understand how many are the consequences that follow from it; but in general it becomes clear from it that the will of the generation, its customs and its aims were honored in his eyes. For this reason too he warned elsewhere, saying: Do not separate yourself from the community (Avot ch. 3) ⚠6. In teaching Torah to others he kept the virtue of patience very much. He was not one of those people who stand by their opinion and cannot bear another opinion, and come with hard words to overturn the opinion of others; but he loved peace and pursued peace, and when he wanted to prove the truth of his opinion against others his speech was gentle. In a special saying he speaks of this virtue, saying: Be of the disciples of Aaron, loving peace and pursuing peace, loving people and bringing them near to the Torah (ibid. ch. 1). This virtue is revealed and seen in several aggadot that tell of his affairs. Let us mention especially what is told of his dealings with a certain gentile. Once a gentile came before him to become a proselyte on condition that he would accept only the Written Torah and not the Oral Torah. Hillel did not rebuke him, but accepted him at once and converted him. He began to teach him the teaching of the Jews, and in his wisdom he contrived to show him little by little that there was no place for his request. For he showed him that it is impossible to learn anything from others unless the learner relies on the words of the teacher with full faith; and since he must believe the teacher, he must of necessity believe everything that he teaches him. From this the gentile understood by himself that he was obliged to believe Hillel, who was his teacher, in this too: that the Written Torah is explained only with the help of the Oral Torah (Shabbat 30b) ⚠7. Now the idea that lies in this aggadah is the very idea that speaks from his saying: Loving people and bringing them near to the Torah. There is no doubt that this aggadah is rooted in his patience, and that he was known as a man who did not come in storm and tempest against those who cast doubt on the Oral Torah, but his words were gentle and reasoned; and in this his way was distinct from the way of his predecessors toward the Sadducees. This is the truth in this aggadah; but the aggadah, after its manner, put the matter in figurative images and clothed it in blossoms and flowers, and so this story came out. And now we learn from this that Hillel's conduct with the Sadducees was not like the conduct of the earlier [sages]. The earlier ones fought them with a bitter soul, and therefore they achieved nothing to bring them back, but only stirred up strife by their opposition. And if Simeon ben Shetaḥ succeeded in defeating them, he did not succeed in turning them from their way. But Hillel did not come with force upon the people who refused to accept the Oral Torah; only with soft words did he draw their heart to it and bring them near to the Torah. Therefore it is no wonder that Hillel did not pay attention to who the man was who came to learn and what his nature was, but brought every person near to the study of Torah; for he said: Many sinners were in Israel, and they were brought near to the study of Torah, and from them came forth righteous, pious and worthy men (Avot de-Rabbi Natan ch. 2). And now, since his way was a way of understanding, and his staff with which to guide the people was a staff of pleasantness [cf. Zechariah 11:7], therefore in his days the Sadducees are not remembered or mentioned [cf. Jeremiah 3:16], and who knows how many of them he brought back to the teaching of the Pharisees; at any rate they did not make strife, because Hillel did not pursue them as his predecessors had; until in another generation, in the time of his disciples and his disciples' disciples, when the division grew even among the Pharisees and they became two camps, then the Sadducees too were roused to stand up as in the former days. Hillel's refusal to force others to accept his opinion speaks explicitly from one of his sayings. He used to say: Do not say a thing that cannot be heard, [thinking] that in the end it will be heard (Avot ch. 2). By this he says that one should not say a thing that by its nature will meet with opposition in the hearer, and we should not think that even if he does not listen now, in the end it will be heard, and that there is no harm if its beginning is by compulsion, since its end will be willing. Against such a practice Hillel warns, and such was also his rule all his days: not to compel the acceptance of his opinion; for a faithful teacher like him strives to prove the truth of his opinion with reason and demonstrations. Therefore calm of spirit toward the learners and gentle speech with them were in his view the most necessary things for a teacher, and conversely, in his eyes the greatest harm for a teacher was anger and irritability. Calm of spirit, together with his patience, he kept in all their aspects. Through these virtues he won for himself the heart of his disciples, and they removed from them the shame of asking, which is a bad trait in many students who are ashamed to ask lest they be despised. This shame grows stronger and stronger in proportion to the irritability of the teacher; therefore Hillel's patience and good sense removed this shame from his disciples, and he warned very strongly against the opposite, saying: The bashful does not learn, and the irritable does not teach (Avot ch. 2). And perhaps this saying was aimed especially against Shammai the Elder. For he was very irritable, as all who have told the story of his life relate of him, and as will yet come below in our narrative.

אבל העיקר הראשי אשר שם לו למטרה היה התפשטות התורה. על כן אמר: מי שאינו לומד תורה איננו כדאי שיחיה דלא מוסיף יסיף ודלא יליף קטלא חייב (שם). הנהו יורה בזה כי לשני תכליות יהיו פני הדורש בתורה מועדות האחת שלא יאמר למדתי הרבה ודי לי כי אם לא ילאה מהגות בה כי ארוכה מארץ מדה, כי הפוסק מן התורה בזמן מן הזמנים דומה כפוסק מן החיים שהיא חיינו וארך ימינו. והשנית שהעוסק בתורה עוד לא השלים חקו אם מתמיד ללמוד, כי אם חייב ללמד תורה גם לאחרים, ומי שאינו עושה כן עבר חק והחטיא מטרת חייו. כי התורה והדעת מביאות לידי יראה, ובלא דעת אין יראת שמים ואין חסידות. שאין בור ירא חטא ולא עם הארץ חסיד (שם). אבל הזהיר מעשות כל After Hillel rose to the office of Nasi, a certain sage named Menaḥem was raised up, and he became Av Beit Din. Josephus tells of a man called by the name Menaḥem, who was of the society of the Essenes; he foretold to Herod, while he was still a boy, the things to come [cf. Isaiah 41:23]: that he would reach the kingship. And when his word was fulfilled, Herod remembered Menaḥem, called him before him and asked him how many would be the days of the years of his reign. And when Menaḥem hinted to him good and comforting words [cf. Zechariah 1:13], the king honored him with great honor, stretched out his hand to him and sent him away with kindness and honor (Antiquities 15). Perhaps this Menaḥem was the man Menaḥem who was Av Beit Din, and perhaps not. For of the history of the life of this one and of that one nothing has reached us; we know only this, that the days were not long before Menaḥem left his office and went his way with eighty pairs of his disciples, and they received portions from the king. Where did he turn? And what was his deed and his conduct from then on? This has not been told to us. Indeed, the sages of a later generation said that he went to the king's service, and some say that he went out from one measure to another, and some say that he went out against his own face ⚠8 (Ḥagigah 16b, and Yerushalmi there). From all these conjectures we cannot understand where he went and what his deeds were; but this follows from the words of all of them: that they reversed the custom they had before and separated from the sages. And now it is possible that Menaḥem and his disciples too were among the opponents who refused to accept the broadening of the exposition of the Torah that had newly arisen in the house of study of Shemaiah and Avtalyon. After Menaḥem went out, Shammai entered in his place. He was irritable and quick to anger. Calm of spirit and patience were not his portion (Shabbat 31a), and his soul was impatient to bear the burden and load of the multitude [cf. Deuteronomy 1:12]. But his quickness to anger was not a sign of wickedness or hardness of heart; for even though it was his nature to grow angry quickly, his heart was nevertheless good toward people, and he conducted himself with humility even toward disciples5 (Bava Batra 134b) ⚠9. But this cannot be denied: that from a small cause he came to anger. Once a gentile came before him and asked of him something that in his opinion could not be fulfilled, and he pushed him away with the builder's cubit. And likewise he used to rebuke people with a scolding if they asked him something improper (Shabbat 31a). It is no wonder that his own conduct is found to be opposed to what he taught others, when he warned them to beware of coming with a sour face toward people, and said explicitly: Receive every man with a friendly face (Avot ch. 1); for in truth his irascibility did not spring from wickedness of heart but from the natural character of his soul. Without doubt he himself recognized that this fault clung to him, but he had no strength to overcome his nature, and therefore he warned others to beware of it; for he, the possessor of the bad trait, knew in his own soul how great its harm was. In his piety he did not keep the middle way, and he loaded on himself, as on others, a great burden of stringencies and decrees, until once it came into his mind to afflict the soul of his son with fasting while he was still a small child (Yoma 77b); and when his daughter-in-law bore a male child on the festival of Sukkot, he broke open the plaster of the roof and spread a sukkah covering over the bed for the sake of the infant (Sukkah 28a). In general it appears from his customs that he was very exacting in the keeping of the commandments (Beitzah 16a). And indeed this trait of his, to multiply stringencies and to give precept upon precept and line upon line [Isaiah 28:10], is revealed and seen before our eyes even more in the conduct of his disciples, those trained in his house of study, almost all of whose halakhot incline to the side of stringency; and we can know the tree by the fruit that it bore. Of the rest of the history of his life nothing has reached us, and this is a sign that he was not beloved in the eyes of the men of his generation; and the later generations, too, did not attach aggadot to him as they did to Hillel, every one of whose traits of soul was beautified in aggadic images. But from his relation to Hillel and the disputes and words that were between them, and also from the sayings that we have from him in halakhah, we recognize his way in his studies, and for the most part also his character; for a different spirit hovers over them. It is clear that these two sages broadened the boundaries of the Oral Torah, and Hillel especially brought a new spirit into its exposition. Midrashim and halakhot are mentioned in the names of both of them, but they are few; yet without doubt their words are included in the words of their disciples, and from these we have a large number of halakhot and also some midrashim. But before we consider the details of their sayings, midrashim and halakhot, to understand from them the difference between their ways, let us turn our eyes once more to the consequences of the event that happened to Hillel with the Benei Bathyra; for it was a great cause for Hillel to broaden the exposition of the Torah, and it was also the beginning of the controversy of the sages in the generation of Shammai and Hillel and after them.

There a story is told of a man whose sons did not behave properly toward him, and he wrote his property over to Jonathan ben Uzziel. What did Jonathan ben Uzziel do? He sold a third, consecrated a third, and returned a third to the man's sons. Shammai came upon him with his staff and his bag. He said to him: Shammai, you cannot take back what I have sold, etc. And Shammai said: Ben Uzziel has rebuffed me. Rashbam explains “with his staff and his bag” as: to dispute with him. From this the scholar Graetz concluded that Shammai went out against him with a staff to compel him, and that this is a sign of his irascibility. But the intention of the baraita is not so. In my opinion one should judge the reverse: from the fact that Shammai went to him and did not summon him to come before him, as is the way of a master with a disciple, it is shown that he dealt with him in humility. And as for his coming with his staff and his bag, one should not explain that he came with a staff to chastise him and compel him, for if so, why his bag? Rather, the phrase “came with his staff and his bag” is a standing expression everywhere for describing setting out on a journey, as in Shabbat (31a): “This proselyte came with his staff and his bag”; and in Rosh Hashanah chapter 2, in the story of Rabban Gamaliel, we find “he took his staff and his money in his hand.” And as for the author of the statement using the word “upon him,” which implies that he came upon him with a staff to chastise him, as in “that you come to me with staves” [1 Samuel 17:43], said in the story of Goliath, such a fine point is not enough to take the phrase “came with his staff and his bag” out of its plain sense. But know that the whole story is told in the Yerushalmi, Nedarim end of chapter 4, in another manner and the very reverse of what is told in the Bavli; therefore it is hard to draw conclusions from it for the benefit of history. ↩ back to the text
