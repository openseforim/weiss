Midrash aggadah; the external books.

מדרש אגדה, ספרים חצונים.

3

Behold, we have seen that from the day they came up from the exile the heads of the generations and their sages brought a new spirit into the midst of the people. The study of the Torah was their aim and their occupation. The goal of their study was the practical part of the Torah, but the narrative part too was dear to them, to occupy their spirit with it. The ways of study for the two parts were also the same. Just as in the legal part they expounded every single word, and even single letters, to found the halakhot on the words of the Torah, until this way of midrash became a method in the interpretation of the Torah for drawing out new things by it, so they also did in the narrative and speculative part. For every idea born of the intellect they sought a source and a foundation in the Torah. The new opinions that became known to them in the exile and were right in their eyes, and those that became known to them from the Greeks and that they accepted and favored, they strove to find their foundation in the Torah. And conversely, opinions became known to them that they strove to keep away, and for these they sought proofs to refute them. To reach these two aims they used the ways of midrash of the legal part. The view was also spreading that all the narratives of the Torah have a moral purpose; and the first elders proved this from the words of the Torah, "Write this for a memorial in a book" [Exodus 17:14], the meaning in their opinion being that it was to be written in a book so that it might be a memorial for a later generation, to know the measure of the Holy One, blessed be He (above, ch. 12). Philo the Jew goes one step further and wrote that all the words of the Torah and its statutes have a meaning in two aspects, the revealed and the hidden. And without doubt this was also the opinion of the sages of the Land of Israel, as is seen from the way Josephus went in his inquiries into the inner meaning of the Torah and its reasons; he said explicitly that the Torah hinted at several things in wise hints, and in short sayings in the garment of mystery and parable (introduction to the Antiquities). From then on they believed that the Torah is the foundation of all wisdom (Ben Sira ch. 24), and that only one who studies it and the writings of the wisdom of Israel and the Prophets will become wise (ch. 39); and this idea filled the hearts of the sages of the Oral Torah. Therefore it is self-evident that for every matter of wisdom and every opinion in beliefs that they held in the course of the ages they were compelled to give a foundation in the Scriptures; and this caused them to act in this part of the midrash of Scripture as in the midrash of halakhah. Therefore in this part too only a minority of the midrashim lean to the plain meaning of Scripture, for most of them strive to show a source for ideas, virtues, beliefs and opinions: whether what they received from their predecessors, or what became known to them from other nations and was right in their eyes, or what they invented from their own minds; to all these they gave a source in Scripture. Many of these midrashim are strange, and in truth some of them have only the value of rhetoric. This is so with midrashim whose basis is a passing cause, and when the cause passes the effect passes too (Zunz, G[ottesdienstliche] V[orträge], p. 58); and likewise, when they wished to rouse the heart of the multitude to some matter, or to speak in praise of certain people or a matter or one of the nations, or on the contrary when they spoke in disparagement and warning, they used the Scriptures by way of rhetoric (Jellinek, Nofet Tsufim). But it is not so with the general midrashim, in which the idea placed in them was true for them in itself: in those midrashim most of them had for them the true meaning of Scripture, especially since they held that Scripture has a hidden and a revealed interpretation. All the expositions of this part of the Torah were called "midrashei aggadah." If at times the exposition is strange in our eyes, this is no proof that in their opinion it was not the plain interpretation; for what the ancients called peshat, as against derash, is also strange (Kiddushin 80b; Ketubot 111b; Ḥullin 6a, 133b; Arakhin 8b). Therefore most of the general aggadic midrashim go by the way of peshat, that is, what was called by them the plain meaning of Scripture. These general aggadic midrashim were called "praiseworthy aggadot that are heard in the ears of every man" (Mekhilta, Va-yassa ch. 1). Only very few of the aggadic midrashim and of aggadic sayings in general have reached us in the names of the sages who lived in the early time; but this is no proof that they did not exist. We have general aggadic sayings without the names of those who said them, and sometimes transmitted by sages after the destruction, of which it is recognizable from their content that they are early; and especially we find sayings and ideas in other books from an early time that bear witness to several aggadic sayings in the books of the Oral Torah, proving that they are old.

הנה ראינו שמיום עלותם מן הגולה הביאו ראשי הדורות וחכמיהם רוח חדשה בקרב העם. דרישת התורה היתה מגמתם ועסקם. מטרת דרישתם היה חלק המעשי בתורה. אך גם חלק הספורי יקר אצלם לענות בו את רוחם. גם דרכי הדרישה לשני החלקים היו שוים. כאשר דרשו בחלק החקיי כל מלה ומלה וגם אותיות פרטיות ליסד ההלכות בדברי התורה. עד שהיה דרך המדרש הזה למדה בפירוש התורה להוציא על פיהו גם דברים חדשים, אף כן נהגו בחלק הספורי והעיוני. לכל רעיון יליד השכל חפשו לו מקור ומסד בתורה. הדעות החדשות אשר נודעו להם בגולה וישרו בעיניהם, ואשר נודעו להם מן היונים, וקבלו אותן ורצו בהן השתדלו למצוא יסודן בתורה. וגם להפך נודעו להם דעות אשר השתדלו הרחקתן ובאלה בקשו ראיות על סתירתן. ולמען השיג שתי התכליות האלה השתמשו בדרכי המדרש של החלק p. 194 החקי. גם היתה הדעה מתפשטת שכל ספורי התורה יש להם תעודה מוסרית, והזקנים הראשונים הוכיחו כן ממאמר התורה: כתוב זאת זכרון בספר. שהכונה לדעתם שיכתב בספר למען יהיה זכרון לדור אחרון לדעת מדת הקב"ה (למעלה פי"ב). ופילון היהודי הולך עוד צעד אחד הלאה וכתב שכל דברי התורה וחקיה יש להם מובן לשני פנים: הנגלה והנסתר. ובלי ספק גם דעת חכמי ארץ ישראל כן, כאשר נראה מן הדרך אשר הלך בו יוסיפון בחקירותיו על פנימיות התורה וטעמיה. ואמר בפירוש שכמה דברים רמזה התורה ברמזים מחוכמים, ובמאמרים קצרים בלבוש הסוד והמשל (הקדמה לקדמוניות). ומאז האמינו כי התורה היא יסוד כל החכמות (בן סירא פכ"ד) ורק הדורש בה ובכתובי חכמת ישראל ובנביאים יחכם (פל"ט) וזה הרעיון מלא את לב חכמי התורה שבעל פה; וע"כ מובן מעצמו שכל דבר חכמה וכל דעה באמונות אשר החזיקו בהן במשך הזמנים הוכרחו לתת להם יסוד בכתובים וזה גרם שגם בחלק הזה ממדרש הכתובים נהגו כמו במדרש ההלכה. לכן גם במין המדרשים בחלק הזה רק מיעוטם נוטים לכונה הפשוטית שבמקרא. כי רובם משתדלים להראות מקור לרעיונים ומדות ואמונות ודעות, בין מה שקבלו מקדמוניהם ובין מה שנודע להם משאר אומות וישר בעיניהם ובין מה שהמציאו מדעתם, אל כל אלה נתנו מקור במקרא. רבים מן המדרשים האלה הם זרים, ובאמת אין לקצתם כי אם ערך מליצה, וזה במדרשים אשר יסודם סבה עוברת וכשתעבור הסבה יעבור גם המסובב (צונץ ג. פ. 58), וכן ברצותם לעורר את לב ההמון לאיזה ענין או לדבר בשבח איזה אנשים או ענין או אומה מן האומות, או להפך בדברם לגנאי ולאזהרה השתמשו בכתובים דרך מליצה (יעללינעק נפת צופים). אבל אין כן במדרשים כוללים אשר הרעיון המונח בהם היה מתאמת אצלם מצד עצמו, המדרשים ההם רובם היו להם מובן המקרא באמת, ובפרט בחשבם שיש למקרא פירוש נסתר ונגלה, כל הדרושים מחלק הזה מן התורה נקראו: מדרשי אגדה. אם לפעמים הדרשה זרה בעינינו אין זה ראיה שלא היה זה לפי דעתם הפירוש הפשוטי, כי מה שקראו הקדמונים פשט בחלוף אל הדרש גם הוא זר (קידושין פ': כתובות קי"א: חולין ו'. קל"ג: ערכין ח':), וע"כ רוב מדרשי האגדה הכוללים הולכים על דרך הפשט כלומר מה שנקרא אצלם פשט המקרא. אלה מדרשי האגדה הכוללים נקראו: אגדות משובחות הנשמעות באזני כל אדם (מכילתא דויסע פ"א). אך מתי מעט ממדרשי אגדה ומדברי אגדה בכלל הגיעו אלינו על שמות החכמים אשר היו בזמן הקדום, אך אין זה ראיה על חסרון מציאותם. ונמצאו אצלנו דברים אגדיים כוללים בלי שם אומריהם ולפעמים נמסרים מאת החכמים שלאחר החרבן אשר ניכר מתוכם שהם קדומים, וביחוד נמצא דברים ורעיונים בספרים אחרים מזמן קדום אשר יתנו תעודה על כמה מאמרי אגדה בספרי תורה שבעל פה להוכיח על ישנם.

4

The books to which we allude are all the books written by Jews from this ancient period that are not included among our Holy Writings. The later sages gave them a special name and called them "outside books," and they forbade reading them. All these books were sealed with the seal of the opinions of their time, to a greater or lesser degree. Of the traces of beliefs and opinions that are in them we find many examples in all the books of the Oral Torah, and this shows that the spirit ruling in both the one and the other is the spirit of the ancient time. The book of Ben Sira was written some time after the death of Simeon the Just, and was translated into Greek by the author's grandson. The book of the Proverbs of Solomon was before the author's eyes. In countless places he uses the Proverbs of Solomon and expands them, and by his expansion we sometimes understand the sense of the proverbs that were before him. He also took as the basis of his inquiry ideas and figures of speech from the books of Psalms and Job. And whoever examines his words and proverbs with proper examination will find that the book of Ecclesiastes too was before his eyes; for it is no accident that in several chapters of his book (11:20–22; 14:3–17) he puts forward ideas whose like we find in Ecclesiastes, only Ben Sira expands them and puts them in more ornate language. But the expansion is a sign that in Ben Sira they are derivative and in Ecclesiastes they are primary, and not the reverse. And perhaps his words against inquiring into what is hidden, when he says: "Seek not what is too wonderful for you, and search not what is hidden from you," etc. (3:21), are aimed against all the doubts of Ecclesiastes. And when the author describes, at the end of his book, the excellence of King Solomon (47:14–20), he also mentions his songs, his figures of speech, his proverbs and his expositions. Now the words "songs, figures of speech, proverbs and expositions" we cannot understand unless we say that Ben Sira attributed three kinds of books to Solomon. And what are they? The Song of Songs, Proverbs, Ecclesiastes. From all this it appears that the foundations of the book were the opinions of the Torah, the Prophets and the other Holy Writings as they were understood in his time, and all in agreement with the opinions of the Pharisees. And therefore they copied the words of the book as though it were one of the Holy Writings, and according to the account of the Talmud, Simeon ben Shetaḥ already alluded to the words of Ben Sira. Indeed, in many places we find opinions and principles of religion in the mouths of the sages of the Torah whose exact like we find in Ben Sira, and this is a sign that those opinions and principles were the opinions of the ancient time. And it is fitting to list a few examples here in particular. Speaking of wisdom he says (1:1, 4) that it is from the Lord, and that it was before His works of old. And the source of wisdom is the Torah of the Lord (ibid. 5; 24:9, 25). And this is the opinion of the Pharisees at all times, that wisdom is the Torah (Mekhilta Beshallaḥ end of ch. 9, Ba-Ḥodesh ch. 10, and in many places, and in the Talmud and Midrashim). And he says that wisdom sought a dwelling place among all the nations, and at the Lord's command it dwelt among the people of Israel (44:6–9) ⚠1; this agrees with the aggadah of our sages in the Talmuds, that all the nations were asked to accept the Torah (Avodah Zarah 2b; Mekhilta Ba-Ḥodesh ch. 5; and Sifre Deuteronomy pisqa 343). He who occupies himself with the Torah, said Ben Sira, is fit to stand guard over the affairs of state and religion, to instruct the people, to bring counsel to princes and to be appointed to every kind of office; and therefore such men shall not toil at labor and the work of the hands (38:23; 39:1–10). This agrees with the opinion of the sages of the Oral Torah: "Whoever takes upon himself the yoke of Torah, the yoke of government and the yoke of worldly occupation are removed from him." Against the ancient view that the sick man should trust in the Lord ⚠2 and not consult a physician (II Chronicles 16:12), the sages laid down that the physician has been given leave by the Lord to heal, and said: "'And he shall surely heal' [Exodus 21:19]: this teaches that permission was given to the physician to heal" (Bava Kamma 85a). Ben Sira expanded this principle and decided that first of all one should entreat the Lord to send healing, and then entrust oneself into the hand of the physician, who is sent by the Lord to heal the diseases of the flesh (38:1–13). On divorcing a wife, too, he said: if his wife is not according to his wish, let him send her away, even if he has not found in her any unseemly thing (6:26; 25:29); this is according to the opinion of the Pharisees from the days of Simeon ben Shetaḥ and of the House of Hillel. And of a number of moral sayings in the Talmud the like is found in the book of Ben Sira, such as: "Repent one day before your death" (Shabbat 153a; Ben Sira 5:8); "Do not despise any thing" (Avot ch. 4; Ben Sira 5:18); "Be exceedingly lowly of spirit, for the end of man is the worm" (Avot ch. 2 ⚠3; Ben Sira 7:18); that one must not say to a penitent, "Remember your former deeds" (Sifra Qedoshim ch. 8; Bava Metzia 58b; Ben Sira 8:10); and many like these. From this we judge that his opinions are the opinions of the Pharisees in the Land of Israel at the time when he wrote. And now anyone who has a heart to understand will learn from this that many things in the Talmud and the Midrashim that are called by the name of one of their sages — which, as our eyes see it, we think are new, as though the one who said them brought them out of his own heart and devised them by the spirit of his understanding — are in truth old: the early ones already carried them on their lips generations before, those who came after them in the later generations preserved them, and their master ⚠4 only collected them, to leave them as a blessing for the later generations. Besides the book of Ben Sira there are, among the outside books, writings that were composed in the Hebrew or Aramaic language and whose native land is the Land of Israel. The first is the First Book of the Hasmoneans [I Maccabees]. From its figures of speech it is recognizable that it was written in Hebrew; this book was already before the eyes of Josephus, and its writer was one of the Pharisees. The Book of Tobit too was written in Hebrew and in the Land of Israel. The writer's belief in the life of the world to come (2:18) and in angels and evil spirits, according to the opinions of the Pharisees, teaches us that he was one of the Pharisees. There are also allusions in it to statutes of the Oral Torah (6:24; 7:9; 10:16). The saying of Hillel, "What is hateful to you, do not do to your fellow," is stated in it word for word (4:16). The Book of Baruch was written in Hebrew, and from beginning to end the spirit of the opinions of the Pharisees rules in it. We cannot decide clearly when it was written, but this is clear: the writer saw the book of Daniel and copied sayings from the prayer that is placed in Daniel's mouth (9:9–19); and whoever considers his words will find that he copied from Daniel and not the reverse1; and he likewise copied a number of things from the book of Nehemiah. The Wisdom of Solomon, which the sages of Israel call "the great Wisdom of King Solomon," was attributed to King Solomon, and they thought that it was originally written in Hebrew by Solomon's own hand (Naḥmanides, introduction to the Torah; Me'or Einayim ⚠5 ch. 57); and some Gentile scholars too held it to be the work of Solomon's hands. But in our time and in the generation before us the critics have agreed and concluded that its original is Greek, since it has distinct marks of Greek wisdom. Yet they admit that it differs from the other books that Jews in Alexandria composed on religion and the Torah, in that those made the secondary thing the main thing; but for this book Greek wisdom was not the main thing, but was like buds and flowers with which to adorn the wisdom of the Jews. And if it uses expressions taken from the writings of Greek philosophy, it does so only to make the matter sweet to Greek readers; and we already know that the sages of the Land of Israel did not refrain from accepting a number of things from Greek wisdom, to make them a spice for the Torah. The author of this book too did not refrain from using Greek opinions where they agree with the opinions of the Torah. Therefore, when we find in this book Greek figures of speech, or ideas and expressions whose content is usual in the books of the Greeks, this is no sign that its author stands in the position of the Jews in Alexandria; for the Jews in the Land of Israel too upheld and accepted a number of opinions and ideas from Greek wisdom. And he who says that its figures of speech in attributes and its abstract ideas are evidence that it followed the opinions of the Greeks and the philosophy of the Alexandrian Jews errs in two respects. First, the use of abstract attributes and ideas was not peculiar to the Greeks alone, for we already find it at times in the figures of speech of our Holy Writings, especially in the writings composed in the exile and after the return. And who would not be astonished to hear that the concept "everlasting light" could not have been current in the mouths of the Hebrews (Langen, The Jews in Palestine 28), when we see that it is already usual in the mouths of the prophets? And if the writer describes wisdom and says that it is a radiance of everlasting light, a pure mirror of God's working, and the image of His goodness (7:26), we shall not take from this a proof that the writer walked in the ways of the Alexandrian Jews (Langen, ibid.), since the writers of the Jews in the Land of Israel also walked in this way. In Ben Sira (17:7) we find: "the eye of God's wisdom, which He planted in the heart of man"; and he says (21:3): "the tooth of iniquity," and "the fear of God is the produce of wisdom"; and he says (34): "the visions of the night are images of vanity; as in water, face answers to face." Here we see before us that the use of abstract attributes was usual among the Jews of the Land of Israel. And second, we say: even if we admit that the author used Greek figures of speech, as befits anyone who writes in the Greek language, this is still no proof that the opinions of the book were not the opinions of the Jews in the Land of Israel. Indeed, whoever is versed in the words of our sages knows that all its opinions are hinted at, a little or much, in the words of the sages of the Land of Israel; and since the author described his opinions in Greek figures of speech, the opinions themselves became unrecognizable and were considered by many to be Greek opinions. But the truth is this (Langen, ibid. 34): this book is an offspring that came forth from the joining of the wisdom of the Jews with the wisdom of the Greeks; its flesh is flesh of the flesh of Jewish wisdom and bone of its bone, and Greek gave it its appearance and form. Therefore this book too is a book of remembrance that has preserved for us the opinions of the Jews in the Land of Israel in the time of the Temple. The Second Book of the Hasmoneans [II Maccabees], which is an abridgment of the book of the history of the Hasmonean war that Joshua [Jason] of Cyrene wrote in five books — although its source and birthplace is Alexandria, nevertheless, since its opinions are truly Jewish, it will be of use for our purpose.

הספרים אשר אנו רומזים עליהם הם כל הספרים הנכתבים מאת יהודים אשר מזמן הקדום הזה ואינם נכללים בתוך כתבי קדשנו. החכמים המאוחרים כנו להם שם מיוחד וקראום ספרים חצונים. ואסרו הקריאה בהם. כל אלה הספרים נחתמו בחותם דעות זמנם אם רב או מעט. מרושמי אמונות ודעות אשר בהם נמצא דוגמאות רבות בכל ספרי התורה שבע"פ, וזה יורה כי הרוח השליט באלה ובאלה הוא רוח הזמן הקדום. ספר בן סירא נכתב בזמן מה אחרי מות שמעון הצדיק, ונעתק ליונית מאת נכד המחבר. ספר משלי שלמה היה למחבר לעינים. במקומות אין מספר משתמש במשלי שלמה ומרחיבם ולפי הרחבתו נבין לפעמים כונת המשלים אשר היו לנגד עיניו. אף כן לקח ליסוד חקירתו רעיונים ומליצות שבספר תהלים ואיוב. ואשר יבחון בבחינה נאותה את דבריו ומשליו ימצא שגם ספר קהלת היה לנגד עיניו כי לא מקרה הוא אשר בכמה פרקים מספרו (פי"א, כ' — כ"ב פי"ד ג' — י"ז) יציע רעיונים אשר דוגמתם נמצא בקהלת רק שבן סירא מרחיבם וממליצם יותר, אבל ההרחבה היא אות כי p. 195 בבן סירא הם בערך תולדות ובקהלת הם עיקריים ולא בהפך. ואולי דבריו נגד הדרישה במופלא באמרו: במופלא ממך בל תדרוש ובמכוסה ממך בל תחקור וגו' (פ"ג, כ"א) הם מכוונים נגד כל ספקותיו של קהלת. וכאשר יתאר המחבר בסוף ספרו את מעלת שלמה המלך (פמ"ז, י"ד — כ') מזכיר גם שיריו, מליצותיו, משליו, ודרושיו. אמנם הדבורים: שירים, מליצות, משלים ודרושים לא נבין בלתי אם נאמר שבן סירא יחס לשלמה שלשה מיני ספרים. ומה הם? שיר השירים משלי קהלת. מכל זה נראה כי יסודי הספר היו דעות התורה והנביאים ושאר כתבי הקדש לפי מה שהובנו בזמנו וכלם בהסכמה עם דעות הפרושים. ואף על כן העתיקו דברי הספר כאילו הוא מכתבי הקדש ולפי ספור התלמוד כבר רמז שמעון בן שטח על דברי בן סירא. אמנם במקומות רבים נמצא דעות ועקרי דת בפי חכמי התורה אשר נמצא דוגמתם ממש אצל בן סירא, וזה אות שהדעות והעיקרים ההם היו דעות הזמן הקדום. וראוי לפרט פה ביחוד איזה דוגמאות. בדברו מהחכמה יאמר (פ"א, א' ד') שהיא מאת ה', והיתה קדם מפעליו מאז. ומקור החכמה הוא תורת ה' (שם ה' כ"ד, ט', כ"ה) והיא דעת הפרושים בכל הזמנים שהחכמה היא התורה (מכילתא בשלח ספ"ט בחדש פ"י ובכ"מ ובתלמוד ומדרשים). ואמר: שאצל כל הגוים בקשה החכמה מכון לשבתה ובמצות השם שבתה בתוך עם ישראל (מ"ד ו' — ט') מסכים עם הגדת חכמינו בתלמודים: שנתבעו כל האומות לקבל התורה (ע"ז ב': מכילתא בחדש פ"ה וספרי דברים פי' שמ"ג). מי שעוסק בתורה, אמר בן סירא, לו יאות לעמוד על משמרת הענינים המדיניים והדתיים להורות לעם, ולהביא עצה לשרים ולהיות נפקדים בכל מיני פקידות וע"כ לא יהיו עמלים במלאכה וביגיע כפים (פל"ח כ"ג. ל"ט, א' — י'), מסכים עם דעת חכמי התורה שבע"פ: מי שקבל עליו עול תורה פורקים מעליו עול מלכות ועול דרך ארץ. נגד הדעה הקדומה שהחולה יבטח בד' ולא ישאל לרופא (דה"יב ט"ז י"ב) קבעו החכמים שהרופא הורשה מאת ה' לרפאות ואמרו: ורפא ירפא מלמד שניתן רשות לרופא לרפא (ב"ק פ"ה.) והעיקר הזה הרחיב בן סירא והכריע כי קודם כל ראוי לחלות פני ה' שישלח רפואה ואז יפקד נפשו ביד הרופא אשר שלוח מאת ה' לרפא תחלואי בשר (פל"ח א' — י"ג) גם בענין גירושי אשה אמר: אם אשתו אינה כנפשו ישלחנה אף אם לא מצא בה ערות דבר (פ"ו כ"ו. פכ"ה כ"ט) כדעת הפרושים מימי שמעון בן שטח ובית הלל. וכמה מאמרי מוסר בתלמוד, דוגמתם בס' בן סירא כמו: שוב יום אחד לפני מיתתך (שבת קנ"ג. ב"ס פ"ה ח') אל תהי בז לכל דבר (אבות פ"ד ב"ס פ"ה י"ח) מאד מאד הוי שפל רוח שאחרית אנוש רמה (אבות פ"ב ב"ס פ"ז י"ח), שאין לומר לבעל תשובה זכור מעשיך הראשונים (ת"כ קדושים פ"ח ב"מ נ"ח: ב"ס פ"ח י') וכדומה לאלה הרבה. ומזה נשפוט כי דעותיו הן דעות הפרושים בא"י בזמן כותבו. והנה כל מי אשר יש לו לב להבין ילמד מזה כי הרבה דברים בתלמוד ובמדרשים הנקראים על שם אחד מחכמיהם ולפי ראות עינינו נחשוב כי הדברים חדשים והוא האומרם מלבו הוציאם וברוח בינתו המציאם אשר באמת ישנים הם וכבר הראשונים לפני דור ודור נשאום על שפתם והבאים אחריהם בדורות האחרונים קימום ורוהם הוא קבצם להשאירם לברכה לדורות האחרונים. מלבד ספר בן סירא יש עוד בין הספרים החצונים כתובים שנתחברו בלשון עברי או ארמי וארץ מולדתם ארץ ישראל. האחד הוא: ספר החשמונאים א'. ממליצותיו ניכר כי נכתב עברי, והספר הזה כבר היה לעיני יוסיפון, וכותבו היה מן הפרושים. ספר טוביה גם הוא נכתב עברי ובארץ ישראל. אמונת הכותב בחיי העה"ב (פ"ב י"ח) ובמלאכים וברוחות רעות, לפי דעות הפרושים, תורנו כי היה מן הפרושים. גם נמצאו בו רמזים מחקי התורה שבעל פה (פ"ו כ"ד. פ"ז ט'. פ"י ט"ז). מאמרו של הלל: מה דסני לך לחברך לא תעבד מפורש בו מלה במלה (פ"ד ט"ז). ס' ברוך נכתב עברית ומתחלתו עד סופו רוח דעות הפרושים שליט בו. לא נוכל להחליט בבירור מתי נכתב אבל זה ברור כי ראה הכותב ס' דניאל והעתיק מאמרים מן התפלה אשר p. 196 שומה בפי דניאל (ט' ט' — י"ט) והמתבונן בדבריו ימצא כי הוא העתיק מדניאל ולא בהפך1 וכן העתיק כמה דברים מספר נחמיה. ס' חכמת שלמה, ונקרא בפי חכמי ישראל חכמתא רבתא דשלמה מלכא יחסוהו לשלמה המלך וחשבו שבעצם וראשונה נכתב עברי מיד שלמה (רמב"ן הקדמת התורה מ"ע פנ"ז) וגם קצת חכמי אומות החזיקוהו למעשי ידי שלמה, אבל בזמננו ובדור שלפנינו נמנו וגמרו המבקרים כי יסודו יונית מהיות בו סימנים מובהקים מחכמת יונית, אבל יודו כי נבדל משאר ספרים אשר חברו יהודים באלכסנדריא בפנת הדת והתורה, שהם עשו התפל עיקר, אבל לספר הזה לא היתה חכמת היונית עיקר כי אם היתה כציצים ופרחים לעטר בהם את חכמת היהודים, ואם משתמש בדבורים לקוחים מכתבי הפילוסופיא היונית לא עשה כן כי אם להמתיק הענין לקוראים יונים וכבר ידענו כי כמה דברים מחכמת היונית לא נמנעו חכמי א"י מלקבלם לעשותם תבלין לתורה. ומחבר זה הספר גם הוא לא נמנע מלהשתמש בדעות יוניות במקום שמתאימות עם דעות התורה. וע"כ במצאנו בספר הזה מליצות יוניות או רעיונים ודבורים תוכנים הרגילים בספרי היונים אין זה אות שמחברו עומד על מצב היהודים באלכסנדריא כי גם היהודים בא"י קיימו וקבלו כמה דעות ורעיונים מחכמת יונית. והאומר, שמליצותיו בתוארים ורעיוניו מפשטים הם לעדה על המשכו אחרי דעות היונים ואחרי הפילוסופיא של היהודים האלכסנדרים, טעה משני פנים. האחד כי השמוש בתוארים ורעיונים מפשטים לא היה מיוחד רק ליונים כי כבר במליצות כתבי קדשנו נמצאם לפעמים וביותר בכתובים שנכתבו בגלות ולאחרי שובם. ומי לא ישתומם לשמוע, כי מושג "אור עולם" אי אפשר שהיה שגור בפי העברים (לאנגען היהודים בא"י 28), ואנו רואים שכבר רגיל בפי הנביאים. ואם הכותב יתאר החכמה ואומר שהיא נוגה מאור עולם, ומראה טהור פעולת אל, ודמות טובו (פ"ז כ"ו) לא נקח מזה ראיה שהכותב הלך בדרכי היהודים האלכסנדרים (לאנגען שם) אחרי שגם סופרי היהודים בא"י הלכו בדרך הזה. בבן סירא (י"ז ז') נמצא: עין חכמת אל אשר נטע בלב האדם, ואומר (כ"א ג') שן העון, ויראת אלהים היא תבואות החכמה. ואומר (ל"ד) מראות הלילה הן תמונות הבל כמים הפנים לפנים. הנה לפנינו שהשמוש בתוארים מפשטים רגיל אצל היהודים שבא"י. ועוד שנית נאמר אף אם נודה שהמחבר השתמש במליצות יוניות כאשר יאות לכל כותב בלשון יונית אין זה עוד ראיה שדעות הספר לא היו דעות היהודים בא"י. אמנם מי שבקי בדברי חכמינו הוא יודע שכל דעותיו רמוזות מעט או הרבה בדברי החכמים שבא"י, ובהיות שהמחבר תאר דעותיו במליצות יוניות התנכרו גם הדעות עצמן ונחשבו בעיני רבים כדעות יוניות. אבל האמת כן הוא (לאנגען שם 34) זה הספר הוא תולדה אשר יצאה מהתחברות חכמת היהודים עם חכמת היונים בשרו הוא בשר מבשר חכמת היהודים ועצם מעצמה, והיונית נתנה לו התואר והצורה. וע"כ גם זה הספר הוא ספר זכרון אשר קיים לנו דעות היהודים בא"י בזמן הבית. ספר החשמונאים השני אשר הוא קצור מספר דברי הימים למלחמת החשמונאים אשר כתב יהושע הקיריני בה' ספרים, אף כי מקורו ומולדתו אלכסנדריא בכל זאת מהיות דעותיו יהודית באמת יועיל לתכליתנו.

5

Now we shall not pass on from here until we have said a few words about some books that were born among the Jews in the Land of Israel in this ancient period. They are: the Fourth Book of Ezra, the Book of Enoch, the Book of Jubilees. All three contain beliefs and opinions that grew on the soil of Israel, though as a whole they are "degenerate plants of a strange vine" [Jeremiah 2:21]. The Fourth Book of Ezra, by its spirit, is the work of a Jew of the Land of Israel. Whether it was written in Hebrew or in Greek, the opinions of the scholars are not in agreement, and to us it is all one in which language it was written. In the course of time many hands touched it for the benefit of other opinions. The book contains visions and prophecies attributed to Ezra. The content of the prophecies concerns the fate of the Israelite nation and the divine destiny appointed for it, the kingdom of the Messiah, the world of the resurrection, the last day of judgment, and the end of the kingdom of Rome. These matters are told in the book as a dialogue between Ezra and various angels. One prophecy contains a parable and its interpretation about the destruction of Rome by the Messiah, and alludes in it to twelve kings. But even with the interpretation it is like a hidden riddle. Many scholars of our time have sought to solve the riddle and did not succeed. But this is clear from the words of all of them: the book is not later than the destruction of the Temple2. Opinions of aggadic midrash run all through the book, and in this respect it will be of use to us. Like it is the Book of Enoch, which contains visions and prophecies attributed to Enoch the righteous. Its purpose is to comfort his people in their toil. The content of the prophecies is like the prophecies of Ezra. It also speaks of the Nefilim and the sons of God who came in to the daughters of men, and it attributes to these angels, who corrupted their way, the invention of sorcery and of various arts; and it speaks of evil spirits and demons. The angels lead him to the ends of the earth and reveal to him the secrets of the heavenly and earthly world, and they show him a writing of God engraved on a tablet, of the deeds of men to the end of all generations. By the prophecies and announcements that he received from God and from the angels, he understands and beholds the history of man to the last generation, and he speaks of it in parable and image. He clothes the history of Israel and its troubles in the garment of parable. He divides the time of the world to its end into ten weeks, which include the history of the nation to the author's time; and there is no trace in it alluding to the time from the Hasmonean kingdom onward, apart from a few additions that are recognizable in it, which show traces of a somewhat later time. In the last two weeks he describes the kingdom of the Messiah, the resurrection and the last day of judgment, and he seals the book with general words of reproof and moral instruction. This is the main content of the book. Most of its critics thought that it was written scroll by scroll, and they also pointed out hints in it that its author was of the sect of the Essenes. And it is true that the course of his ideas is like the way of the Essenes. His inquiry into the secrets of the world and into the names of the angels and their workings — is this not also the practice of the Essenes? And special writings of this kind were already attributed to them (Jellinek, Beit ha-Midrash vol. 3, XXXII, note 4). But what has been pointed to as particular traces is not enough to decide. And if we find in the book that Enoch prayed at sunrise and praised the Lord who splits the windows of the firmament and brings the sun out of its place, to shine on the face of the heavens and run its course on high (ch. 82:12), this is no proof that the author was of the Essenes, who prayed before sunrise; for this is not a custom peculiar to the Essenes — did not the pious men of old (vatikin) among the Pharisees also do so? And the content of the prayer is like the content of our blessing of the Shema. They further point to his Essene character in that his mouth is full of the praise of the humble and of the reward appointed for them for their bodily self-mortification, and of the reward of those who are insulted by the wicked, who love the Lord and not silver and gold and the pleasures of the world, who accept sufferings and stand the test (in the last chapter), and several virtues like these (Ewald). But only one who knows the Pharisees and their customs from nothing but the writings of their opponents and slanderers will think so: he holds the Pharisees to be seekers of dominion, honor and pleasures, and the Essenes to be marked by the virtues of humility and patience. But whoever is versed in the words of the Pharisees and their teachings knows that these virtues are the foundations of Pharisaism; and the Essenes are marked by them not because they were Essenes and differed in their virtues from the Pharisees, but because they were of the Pharisees and held to the virtues of the Pharisees. Are not all these virtues found recorded and stated explicitly in the words of the Pharisees? They said: "By the merit of a man's humility he is delivered from Gehinnom" (Yevamot 102b). And who in their eyes was of those who truly love the Lord? "Those who are insulted and do not insult, who hear their disgrace and do not answer, who act out of love and rejoice in sufferings" (Shabbat 88b). And of whom did they say, "Happy are you in this world, and it shall be well with you in the world to come"? Of one who conducts himself by the virtue of contentment: "who eats bread with salt, drinks water by measure, sleeps on the ground, lives a life of hardship and toils in the Torah" (Avot ch. 6). And now, is not the content of these words exactly the content of what is written in the Book of Enoch? However that may be, it is clear that this book contains the opinions of the Jews in the time of the Temple, and in this respect it is fitting for us to pay attention to it in this place. Likewise it is fitting to pay attention to the Book of Jubilees, which is called Bereshit Zuta. Its character is that of an aggadic midrash. Many aggadot found in our aggadic midrashim have found their place in it (Jellinek, ibid. XII). Several words of the book are explained by our midrashim (Beer, in The Relation of the Book of Jubilees to the Midrashim 25). In the manner of the masters of aggadah it gives reasons for the commandments of the Torah, and in its interpretations it follows the way of the masters of midrash. Who was the author, and what was his name? It has not been made known to us. But it is clear that he lived and wrote his book in the Land of Israel, and that he was one of the masters of aggadah, for at all times there were "rabbis of aggadah." Sometimes his words are against the halakhah, but this is no proof that he was of the sect of those who opposed the teaching of the Pharisees. Do not the masters of aggadah in general sometimes expound the verses not in accordance with the halakhic midrash, though they were of the Pharisees? And if one of the masters of aggadah says, by way of example, that one who has intercourse with a Gentile woman transgresses "You shall not sow your field with two kinds," "You shall not plow with an ox and an ass together," and "You shall not wear mingled stuff" (Yalkut Deuteronomy, remez 931), which contradicts the halakhah, we shall not say on this account that the expounder is not among the sages who follow the halakhah. And moreover, who will give us the measure of which of the halakhot was in practice in the days of the writer of this book, so that we could know that if he denied some halakhah found among us, this is a sign that he is opposed to the Pharisees, the masters of the halakhah? Indeed, just as one cannot judge from his agreement with the opinion of the Pharisees in some of his words and say that he was a Pharisee out of love for their teaching, so one cannot judge the reverse from his contradictions3. It is not part of our purpose to describe the history of the book here; we shall only remark that there is nothing in it indicating that the author was an Alexandrian (Frankel) or that he was a Dositheian of the sect of the Kutim [Samaritans] in Alexandria (Beer). Rather, the language of the book from beginning to end shows that it was written in Hebrew, and the spirit that rules in it is the spirit of the aggadah of Jerusalem; like all the sons of his land he glories in Jerusalem and in the Sanctuary on the site of Mount Zion (ch. 1), and in most of his words he agrees with the Pharisees on matters of the commandments and is strict about the performance of the commandments; and all this is a sign that he was neither an Alexandrian nor a Kuti. But this too is plain to see: the author meant to cast doubt on the Pharisees concerning their custom of intercalating the years and sanctifying the month by sighting; with harsh words he challenges the intercalators of the years and says of them that they corrupt the times, the months, the Sabbaths and the festivals (ch. 6). Yet this is not the way in which the Kutim and the Boethusians challenged it, who sought to mislead Israel; rather his challenge is directed against their reckoning, in that they reckoned by the days of the moon and sanctified the month by sighting in agreement with their reckoning, whereas in his opinion the years should be reckoned by the days of the sun. And who will tell us that there were not among the Pharisees themselves individuals who did not approve of the practice of the court in sanctifying the month and intercalating the year, when we see that some of the greatest of the Tannaim cast doubt on the court's reckoning and were grieved that they sanctified the month on the word of witnesses whose testimony was contradicted by their reckoning? And from such a challenge to a challenge against the reckoning by the days of the moon is only a short step. In sum, one cannot pass judgment on the author and say that he did not follow the Pharisees because he sometimes departs from their midrash, just as we do not say so of Josephus, who also sometimes departed from the midrash of the Pharisees; and Josephus himself testifies of himself that he is of the Pharisees. And indeed it is so, that the general principles of religion hinted at in this book are founded on opinions and beliefs that were alive in the people in his time. Like these three books, other outside books were born in the Land of Israel in that time, and from all of them there is profit for one who inquires into the antiquities of the nation, its opinions and its beliefs.

עתה לא נעבור מזה עד אם דברנו דבר קטן על דבר ספרים אחדים אשר נולדו בין p. 197 היהודים בא"י בזמן הקדום הזה. והם: ספר עזרא הרביעי, ס' חנוך, ס' היובלים. שלשתם כוללים אמונות ודעות אשר צמחו על אדמת ישראל, אף שבכללם הם סורי גפן נכריה. ס' עזרא הד' לפי רוחו הוא מעשה ידי יהודי שבא"י. אם נכתב עברית או יונית אין דעות המחברים שוות ולנו אחת הוא באיזה לשון נכתב. במשך הזמן חלו בו ידי רבים לתועלת דעות אחרים. הספר כולל חזיונות ונבואות המיוחסות לעזרא. תוכן הנבואות הוא על דבר גורל האומה הישראלית, והיעוד האלהי אשר נועד לה, ממלכות המשיח, ועולם התחיה, ויום הדין האחרון, ומקץ מלכות רומי. אלה הענינים מדוברים בספר בדרך שיחה בין עזרא ובין מלאכים שונים. נבואה אחת כוללת משל ופתרונו מאבדן רומי על ידי המשיח, ורומז בו על י"ב מלכים. אבל עם הפתרון הוא כחדה נעלמה. רבים מחכמי זמננו רצו לפתור החדה ולא עלתה בידם. אבל זה מבורר מדברי כלם כי לא יאוחר הספר לחרבן הבית2. דעות מדרש אגדה סובבים והולכים בכל הספר ומצד הזה יהיה לנו תועלת בו. כמוהו כן ס' חנוך כולל חזיונות ונבואות המיוחסות לחנוך הצדיק. תכליתו לנחם עמו בעמלו. תוכן הנבואות דומה לנבואות עזרא. גם מדבר מענין הנפילים ובני אלהים אשר באו אל בנות האדם. ומיחס לאלה המלאכים שהשחיתו דרכם המצאת הכשוף וחכמות שונות, ומדבר מרוחות רעות ושדים, המלאכים יוליכוהו לקצוי ארץ ויגלו לו סתרי העולם השמימי והארצי, ומראים לו מכתב אלהים חרות על לוח ממעשי בני אדם עד סוף כל הדורות. ע"פ הנבואות וההודעות אשר קבל מאלהים ומן המלאכים מבין ומביט קורות האדם עד דור אחרון. ומדבר עליהם במשל וציור. דברי הימים לישראל ותלאותיו יעטה בלבוש המשל. זמן העולם עד סופו יחלק לעשר שבועות כוללים קורות האומה עד זמן המחבר ואין בו רושם הרומז על זמן ממלכות החשמונאים והלאה, מלבד מקצת הוספות אשר ניכרות בו, שהן מראות על רושמי זמן מאוחר מעט. בשני שבועות האחרונים יתאר מלכות המשיח, והתחיה ויום הדין האחרון, וחותם הספר בדברי תוכחה ומוסר כוללים. זה עיקר תוכן הספר. רוב מבקריו חשבו כי מגלה מגלה נכתב, וכן הראו בו רמזים שמחברו היה מכת האסיים. ואמת הוא כי תהלוכת רעיוניו דומה לדרך האסיים. דרישתו בסתרי העולם ובשמות המלאכים ופעולותיהם הלא גם זה מעשה האסיים וכבר נתיחסו להם כתובים מיוחדים ממין הזה (יעללינעק בה"מ ה"ג XXXII הערה 4). אבל מה שנרמזו על רושמים מיוחדים אינו מספיק להכריע. ואם נמצא בספר שהתפלל חנוך עם הנץ החמה ומהלל את ה' כי בוקע חלוני רקיע ומוציא חמה ממקומה למען תאיר על פני השמים ותרוץ ארחה במרום (פפ"ב י"ב), אין זה ראיה על היות המחבר מן האסיים אשר התפללו קודם הנץ החמה כי אין זה מנהג מיוחד לאסיים הלא גם הותיקין מן הפרושים נהגו כן. וענין התפלה הלא הוא כענין ברכת ק"ש שלנו. עוד נרמז על תכונתו האסיית על אשר מלא פיהו מתהלת הענוים ומן השכר הנועד להם עקב סגופם הגופי, ומשכר הנעלבים מן הרשעים אוהבי ה' ולא כסף וזהב ותענוגות העולם ומקבלים יסורים ועומדים בנסיון (בפרק האחרון) וכמה מעלות הדומות לאלה (עוואלד), Some of the external books that we have mentioned, and some that we have not mentioned, were creations of thoughts that issued from the fancies of the soul. And although their authors were Jews, and they were written in Hebrew and on holy soil, they were nevertheless not to the liking of the sages. Therefore the sages strove to put a check on their spread. But one must complain that as it went with the bad, so it went with the good: as they put away the former, so they put away the latter, and one fate befell them all. Indeed, from ancient days they had laid a prohibition on writing words of Torah beyond the twenty-four holy writings. In the course of time the prohibition grew lax in their hands, and they wrote various books, as we have seen until now. Aramaic translations, too, existed a long time before the destruction of the Temple (Zunz, G. d. V. [Die gottesdienstlichen Vorträge der Juden], p. 62), and until about the last quarter of the century before the destruction we do not find that the sages took any action to uphold and maintain the first prohibition; rather, they shut their eyes to the matter. But in the time of Rabban Gamaliel the Elder the matter changed. For then, time after time, men arose who were led by the fancies of thought, who boasted of a spirit of prophecy and made themselves Messiah. In the distress of those times most of the people believed in them, for it is easy for the soul to believe a thing that its heart desires, and it neither seeks nor inquires. At that time there also came about a renewal of religious sects, and various writings were found in the hands of their leaders; these are the ones known to us by the name 'books of the minim.' These books were dangerous to the teaching of the Pharisees, and therefore they were then compelled to warn against writing words of Torah outside the twenty-four books, and to strive to remove the external books or to store them away. Rabban Gamaliel the Elder was the first who girded up his strength to restore the prohibition to its former state and to remove from the land the books that were found. Once he was standing on top of a step on the Temple Mount, and they brought before him a book of Job in translation, and he commanded the builder, and they sank it under the course of stones (Shabbat 115a; Soferim ch. 5). From this it appears that Rabban Gamaliel commanded that all the writings be gathered in order to store them away. And when it is said there: 'they brought before him a book of Job in translation,' it implies that it was brought at his command, when he wished to store away all the writings. Perhaps it was also about his time that it was said that the gilyonim and the books of the minim are to be burned, they and the divine names in them, and storing them away is not enough, for they cast enmity, rivalry and dissension between Israel and their Father in heaven (Yerushalmi Shabbat ch. 16, and the Bavli there, and Tosefta ch. 11). This enactment reached its completion in the assembly of the sages that took place in the house of Ḥananiah ben Hezekiah ben Garon, where they deliberated on the writing of books and with great difficulty permitted the writing of Megillat Ta'anit. Then they sought to store away the book of Ezekiel, and Ḥananiah ben Hezekiah saved it from their hands (Shabbat 14b) ⚠6; and according to some they sought to store away the book of Ecclesiastes (ibid. 30b), and the Song of Songs and Proverbs (Avot de-Rabbi Natan ch. 1). And as for what is said, that the Men of the Great Assembly sought to store away these two books, this is nothing but an exchange of one assembly for another: in truth the matter was disputed in a great assembly, but not in the well-known Great Assembly; rather, in the great assembly in the days of Ḥananiah. At that time they decided that Ben Sira and all the books written from then on do not defile the hands (Tosefta Yadayim ch. 2). For the decree of the defilement of the hands by the holy writings was an ancient decree (Graetz 561). And on that day they decreed — according to some — another decree concerning the holy writings: that they disqualify the terumah, and that hands that were defiled by a book also disqualify terumah (Shabbat 14a; Ḥagigah 24b). The decision that Ben Sira and all the books written from then on do not defile teaches us that all the books that defile were held by them to have been written before Ben Sira.4 But not only against the external books [did they act]; it appears that they also raised their hand to store away the translations. And as Rabban Gamaliel stored away the translation of Job, so they stored away other translations. The two translations that we have, the translation of the Torah ascribed to Onkelos and the translation of the Prophets ascribed to Jonathan ben Uzziel, were without doubt founded on ancient sources (Zunz). And if the aggadah says that the translation of the Torah was a halakhah [given] to Moses at Sinai, was forgotten, and Onkelos came back and founded it, and that Jonathan received the translation of the Prophets from the prophets Haggai, Zechariah and Malachi, from this we can judge that their root was very old, and therefore the views that shine forth from within them are the views of the Pharisees in an early time. Did not two sages among the greatest of the Pharisees, R. Eliezer and R. Joshua, approve the translation of Onkelos, and of the translation of Jonathan they said that secrets of the Torah were revealed in it? And even if we grant that the translations as they are before us were arranged at a late time (Geiger), nevertheless one cannot deny that their foundation already existed in writing before the time of Rabban Gamaliel the Elder, and Rabban Gamaliel stored them away. We should not wonder at their survival after they were stored away, just as we do not wonder at the survival of the book of Ben Sira and the other external books, which survived although they abolished and removed them. It is very likely that some copies of old translations remained in the hands of individuals and were saved from being stored away. Later, perhaps in the days of R. Eliezer and R. Joshua, the Targum Onkelos came to light, and the approval of great sages like R. Eliezer and R. Joshua was enough to argue in its favor and to give it endurance and survival, although it had been among the stored-away books. Likewise, when the masters of the Talmud bring an aggadah that exaggerates the great trembling with which the whole world trembled and shook on the day the translation of Jonathan was revealed, and a heavenly voice was heard saying, 'Who has revealed My secrets to the sons of men?', and Jonathan answers that he did not do it for his own honor but for the honor of Heaven, so that disputes would not multiply in Israel (Megillah 3a), the trembling and the heavenly voice are directed only against its coming to light. The meaning is: since the early sages wished to hide it, it is not right to reveal it. Thus we have seen a broad literature whose birthplace is the Holy Land, and it will teach us to know the value of the midrashim of aggadah in the books of the Oral Torah and their date, and to understand the state of the beliefs and opinions among the Jews until the destruction of the Temple.

The source of the beliefs and opinions in this literature is in the holy writings, which are the foundation also of the beliefs and opinions in the literature of the Oral Torah. But it cannot be denied that opinions from foreign sources also came into its midst, and in the course of time became as if they had burst forth from the womb of our mother, the Congregation of Israel. Here we mean especially the opinions of the Greek wisdom of the Jews in Alexandria. There they occupied themselves with Greek wisdom, and with its help they devised a new way in the midrash of the Torah. In general they were faithful in their covenant with the Torah on the side of faith, and with Greek wisdom on the side of scientific knowledge, and they thought that both together are the absolute truths. Therefore, out of their desire to hold on to both together, they brought them into agreement and gave Greek wisdom a support in the divine Torah of Moses, and devised a midrash of the Torah according to the foundations of that wisdom. From this two principles of midrash came forth for them, namely the way of parable and figure. By these ways they interpreted the attributes in the Torah that ascribe a body to the essence of God. Through Greek wisdom they attained spiritual concepts of His essence that do not tolerate the attributes with which God is described in Scripture according to their plain sense, and they interpreted them by way of parable and figure. Other matters of the Torah, too, they interpreted by way of parable. Already in the days of Ptolemy Philometor the Jewish philosopher Aristobulus wrote a profound commentary on the Torah according to the philosophical opinions of his time and land, and handed it to the king to show him the glory of the Torah and its wisdom; and he testified before him that Plato knew of the Torah of Moses and that it served him as eyes in his inquiries, and that many words of the Torah had been translated for the Greeks already before the time of Alexander. And although his testimonies do not accord with the truth, at any rate it follows for us that the purpose of his commentary was to bring about agreement between the Torah and Greek wisdom, and this he achieved through their way of midrash, parable and figure. For some of them this way became a stumbling block, for through it they grew slack in practice, thinking that the statutes of the Torah are only hints to intellectual matters and that actual practice is secondary; and therefore they were not careful about keeping the practice. But the enlightened ones found fault with this and said that one must be careful in both: in the understanding of the hidden and in the keeping of the revealed (Philo, On Abraham 401). Philo the Jew compared the speech of the Torah to a body, and it is in our hands to give it life in several ways (ibid.). The ways in which the Torah is interpreted are two: either according to its plain sense or according to the hidden intention; and the midrash by way of parable and figure is the hidden intention. The sages in the Land of Israel did not close their hearts to this way of midrash, since from ancient days their belief was that the Torah is interpreted in several aspects, and they too spoke of secrets of the Torah. Ben Sira already hinted (ch. 39) that there is a hidden intention in the words of the Torah, and their midrashim on the Chariot were of this kind, and Ben Sira hinted at them too (ch. 49). Likewise, the effort to remove corporeality was great among the Jews from of old, and it grew still greater from the time they became acquainted with the Greeks. Was not the Septuagint made with the approval of the sages of the Land of Israel, and it already set itself this effort as its aim? And so too the Aramaic translations, whose root is ancient, as we saw above. Now we do not say that they learned these ways of midrash only from the Jews who were drawn after Greek wisdom; but we do say that these ways gained strength in the Land of Israel from the time that the Alexandrian ways of midrash became known to them. Great proof of this is found in the words of Avtalyon, who spoke of the evil that comes to religion through sages who incur the penalty of exile to another land, because the disciples who come after them will drink there the bad waters, which are the corrupt opinions (Avot ch. 1). There is no doubt that he had in mind the exile in which many sages went into exile to Alexandria and learned there from their ways and opinions. We see from this that the Land of Israel was not shut and closed before the opinions of their brothers in Alexandria. At times there is a wonderful likeness between the ways of midrash of the Alexandrians and the midrash of the sages of the Land of Israel; in particular, there are in the books of Philo many midrashim that were current in the Land of Israel (R. Zacharias Frankel, in the book on the midrashim of the Alexandrians, §§ 9, 34). The way of parable was for the sages of the Land of Israel a rule that they used in their interpretation of the commandments of the Torah,5 and especially in the narrative part and in the words of the Prophets and the Writings;6 and likewise they were accustomed, like the Alexandrians, to give reasons for the commandments.7 Josephus too, in his Antiquities, shows reasons of the Torah; yet Josephus was one of the Pharisees, as he himself testified. And when he speaks of the statutes and ordinances (Antiquities, book 4, ch. 5) ⚠7 he says that he will not turn right or left from anything that Moses left us in his Torah, so that the members of his people will not complain against him if they read his words and find a deviation from the commandments of Moses. Now since we find that he gives reasons for the commandments, it is proved from this that he was sure that he would not be blamed for it, and certainly all the sages in the Land of Israel in his time went in this way. We find, further, a great likeness between the midrashim of Philo and the midrashim of the sages of the Land of Israel. When Philo sets out the narratives of the Torah, he invents rhetorical dialogues between the persons of the narratives and puts words in their mouths, as he imagined in his soul that the persons of the story ought to speak with one another, although Scripture has no hint of this. The sages of the Land of Israel did the same. Josephus did so in his narratives of the histories of the Patriarchs and the tribes, and especially the history of Moses. He puts words in the mouth of Moses when he stands before Pharaoh, and before the congregation on the day of assembly, and when he contends with the congregation of Koraḥ, and when the people murmur against him, and before his death — all of which are inventions, and there is no hint of them in Scripture. And such is the custom of the sages of the Oral Torah in their midrashim, who composed dialogues between the Holy One, blessed be He, and the Congregation of Israel, or between Him and a particular man, or between the angels and men, or between one man and his fellow, to present the matter in a fine and acceptable way. This custom gave birth to various books, such as the Book of Enoch, Fourth Ezra, Jubilees and the like. We cannot say today with whom this custom had its source, for all the writers of the nations from ancient days did so; but since the Greek writers clung to this custom with great strength, and the Jewish writers in Alexandria, disciples of the Greeks, planted it on holy soil in the interpretation of the Torah, it is very likely that the custom of the Alexandrians exerted force on them and on their midrashim. This way was useful on the one hand, for great benefit came from it in engraving moral lessons in the heart of one who reads the matters in the garment of a story; but harm for generations also came from it, for people who did not understand the matter thought that those stories were truly events that happened, and so aggadot spread about things that never were and never were created. But in the use of the way of parable and figure there is a difference between the Alexandrians and the sages of the Land of Israel. The Alexandrians went astray at the hand of Greek wisdom: they sought every philosophical opinion in Scripture and forced the verses into agreement, and therefore they twisted the pleasant words of Scripture and found in every simple story hints to intellectual matters, and interpreted the names of the persons of the stories as having special meanings. And events that our faith holds to have actually happened, they subtilize and interpret as some opinion in the sciences and in the influence of reason, and they say that the story is a parable and figure. Philo too did so in his midrashim. He explained (in the book On Noah 192) that the Garden of Eden is a parable for the garden of wisdom and contemplation, which teaches man knowledge of his soul and its powers. And he says (in the book On the Confusion of Tongues 302) that the story of the generation of the dispersion is a figure of the causes that move and bring about the confusion of opinions and the debasement of virtues. The names of Abraham and Sarah he interprets by way of figure: Abraham is the figure of the depth of reason, and Sarah the figure of righteousness, which is a princess [sarati] among the virtues (in the book On Abraham II, 15). Not so did the sages of the Land of Israel go astray. If they interpret Scripture by way of figure, their intention is mostly only to express words of praise and glory about the deeds of the righteous, or disgrace and reproach ⚠8 about the deeds of the wicked, in the language and style of Scripture; but they never thought that this was truly the intention of the verse. And even where their intention is to interpret by way of figure, and they found in the verse a hint to some matter, there is never a contradiction between the outer side of the story or saying that is interpreted and its inner side, which is the hint and the figure; nor is it found that they would say of an event that happened according to the plain sense of Scripture that it is only a parable. And if they said that what Moses threw into the water at Marah was not wood of a growing kind but a parable for words of Torah, by their way this is no contradiction of the plain sense, for in their view 'tree' is said also of words of Torah. From the great multitude of midrashim that were expounded by way of parable and figure, and in giving reasons for the words of the Torah, it appears that these ways were greatly honored among the sages; and already in an early time they gave special names to those who went in these ways of midrash: those who interpret by way of parable and figure were called dorshei reshumot (Mekhilta Va-Yassa ch. 1, and in many places), and those who interpret by way of giving a reason for the Torah were called dorshei ḥamurot (Ḥullin 134b; Pesaḥim 54a, and in many places). And they said of the interpreter of the first kind that he interprets 'as a kind of parable' (Mekhilta Mishpatim ch. 6), and of the interpreter of the second kind that he interprets 'as a kind of ḥomer' (Kiddushin 22b; Sotah 15a). Now, after we have understood a little of the character of the midrashic literature that grew up in the Land of Israel, and after we have further seen the relation of the midrash of the Alexandrians to the midrash of the sages of the Oral Torah, and how far the former poured of its spirit upon the latter, it will not be hard for us now to bring forth from the remnants of that literature, together with the midrashim of aggadah in the books of the Oral Torah, the opinions of the Jews until the destruction of the Temple and the foundations in the beliefs and principles of the Jewish religion.

The explanation of the words 'from then on' is: from the writing of Ben Sira on. R. Ẓevi Graetz, in his commentary on the book of Ecclesiastes, in the appendices at the end of the book, p. 166, explains 'from then on' as from the assembly in the house of Ḥananiah ben Hezekiah, or from the day on which R. Eleazar ben Azariah was appointed Nasi, when they also spoke about certain books with regard to the defilement of the hands (Yadayim 3:5). He relies on the Yerushalmi, Sanhedrin 10:1: 'R. Akiva says: Also one who reads in the external books has no share in the world to come, such as the books of the minim; but the book of Ben Sira and the book of Ben La'anah and the books of Homer and all the books written from then on, one who reads in them is like one who reads a letter.' His intention is that here 'from then on' necessarily does not refer to the writing of the books, for these three kinds of books were not written at one time; therefore 'from then on' must refer to the time of the assembly. But I cannot decide, for he brings us a reading in the Yerushalmi that I did not find there; the reading before us is: 'has no share in the world to come, such as the book of Ben Sira and the book of Ben La'anah; but the books of Homer and all the books written from then on ...', and according to this reading 'from then on' refers to the writing of the books of Homer. That scholar strove there to prove that the book of Ecclesiastes was written at the end of the days of Herod, and that in the upper chamber of Ḥananiah ben Hezekiah the House of Shammai and the House of Hillel disputed whether to attach it to the holy writings and whether it defiles the hands, and the matter was not settled until the day on which they seated R. Eleazar ben Azariah in the academy. I must apologize for not heeding his decision, for there is much to question in his proofs, and especially regarding several readings that are not found in our books. There is no room here to go on at length, but I will bring the main reason that moved me to depart from his decision: namely, that I found that Hillel quoted a verse from the book of Ecclesiastes (Tosefta Berakhot end of ch. 1). Thus in the days of Hillel it was already held to be one of the holy writings. And even before Hillel, Simeon ben Shetaḥ quoted (Yerushalmi Berakhot 7:2, and in many places) two verses from the book of Ecclesiastes: 'In the shadow of wisdom, in the shadow of money,' and 'the advantage of knowledge is that wisdom gives life to those who have it' [Ecclesiastes 7:12]. Therefore what they disputed, whether Ecclesiastes defiles the hands, does not mean whether to attach it to the holy writings, for it had long been among the Writings; rather, they wished to store it away, as they wished to store away the book of Ezekiel. ↩ back to the text

The days of consecration are called 'a sweet savor' because the Holy One, blessed be He, acquired a friend for Himself and makes a festive day for Him (Sifra Tzav). 'Lest the land fall into harlotry' [Leviticus 19:29] — because he has intercourse with many women, etc. (Tosefta Kiddushin ch. 1). Why did He put the taking of the Passover lamb first, etc. (Mekhilta Bo ch. 5). 'Both man and beast' [Exodus 12:12] — the one who was first in transgression, with him the punishment begins (ibid. ch. 7; Sotah 9a). 'Bring before Me the omer so that the grain may be blessed, and the two loaves so that the fruits of the tree may be blessed' (Tosefta Rosh Hashanah ch. 1; Sukkah ch. 3; and the Gemara, Rosh Hashanah 16a). Rabban Yoḥanan ben Zakkai loved to give reasons for the commandments (see Tosefta Bava Kamma ch. 7, and Mekhilta Mishpatim ch. 12; Bava Kamma 79b; Kiddushin 22b, and the Yerushalmi there). 'Why did the Torah warn concerning the stranger, ⚠12 etc.' (Bava Metzia 59b). The meal-offering of the sotah is of barley, because her deed was the deed of a beast, etc. (Sotah 14a); and see further there (8b). Some translators were accustomed to give reasons for the commandments in their translations (Yerushalmi Berakhot 5:3). In a later time it was the manner of several sages to expound the reasons of Scripture (Kiddushin 65b ⚠13, and in many places). In several places in this work of ours we have noted that this was the custom of our ancients. See Me'or Einayim ch. 6, which brings several reasons of Scripture given by the masters of midrash that agree with the interpretations of Philo. ↩ back to the text

Uncertain renderings

  • ⚠1 (מ"ד ו' — ט') — Printed 44:6–9; the passage on wisdom seeking a dwelling and resting in Israel is Ben Sira 24:6–9 (probably כ"ד).
  • ⚠2 יבטח בד' — The print shows a letter like ד/ר with no clear mark; read as בד' (= in the Lord).
  • ⚠3 (אבות פ"ב — The saying מאד מאד הוי שפל רוח is in Avot ch. 4 (4:4), not ch. 2.
  • ⚠4 ורוהם — Printed ורוהם (or ורוחם), which gives no sense; read as ורבם, 'their master' (the sage named as author).
  • ⚠5 מ"ע פנ"ז — Expanded as Me'or Einayim (Azariah de Rossi), ch. 57, which discusses the Wisdom of Solomon; abbreviation not certain.
  • ⚠6 (שבת י"ד:) — The story of Ḥananiah ben Hezekiah and the book of Ezekiel is in Shabbat 13b; kept as printed.
  • ⚠7 (קדמוניות ס"ד פ"ה) — Josephus's statement that he has added nothing to what Moses left is in Antiquities 4.8.4 (§§196–197); kept as printed.
  • ⚠8 גנות ותהלה — Printed ותהלה ('praise'); the sense requires a word of blame (perhaps תפלה or קללה). Rendered by sense as 'reproach'.
  • ⚠9 וא"ת — Abbreviation read as 'and the Targum [Onkelos] renders'; Onkelos to Leviticus 19:32 has מן קדם דסבר באוריתא.
  • ⚠10 (י"ד ח' ט') — As printed; the parallel (old age is understanding, not length of days) is Wisdom of Solomon 4:8–9.
  • ⚠11 (סנהדרין מ"ז.) — This saying is in Sanhedrin 76a; kept as printed.
  • ⚠12 בגר — The letter is unclear in the scan (בנר or בגר); read בגר, which fits Bava Metzia 59b (the Torah warns concerning the stranger in 36 places).
  • ⚠13 (קידושין ס"ה:) — R. Simeon's expounding the reason of Scripture is in Kiddushin 68b; the print may have ס"ח. Kept as read.
